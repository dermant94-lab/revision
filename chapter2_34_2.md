# CHAPTER TWO

## Reconstructive Science and Post-Metaphysical Thinking: The Three Commitments, Their Tensions, and the Aporia of Truth

## §2.1 · Post-Metaphysical Thinking, Reconstructive Science, and the Three Commitments

What Habermas calls post-metaphysical thinking is presented by him as a situation before it is a doctrine, and the situation is dated. In the essay which opens *Postmetaphysical Thinking*, ‘The Horizon of Modernity Is Shifting’, the break with the tradition is characterised by four themes: ‘Four themes characterize the break with the tradition. The headings are: postmetaphysical thinking, the linguistic turn, situating reason, and reversing the primacy of theory over practice — or the overcoming of logocentrism.’ The first names the decline of ‘the tradition’s strong concept of theory, its grasp of the totality, and its claim to a privileged access to truth’; the second is the passage from the philosophy of consciousness to that of language, in which world-constituting accomplishments ‘are transferred from transcendental subjectivity to grammatical structures’ and ‘the reconstructive work of the linguist replaces a kind of introspection that cannot be readily checked on’; the third is the situating of reason in ‘historical embodiments’, in action, language and the body; the fourth is ‘the reversal of the classical relationship of theory to practice’, credited ultimately to ‘the honing of a Marxian idea’ and found corroborated by pragmatism, developmental psychology and the analysis of the lifeworld.[^1] The four are not offered as a system but as the motive forces of twentieth-century philosophising ‘in spite of the boundaries between schools’.

The fourth theme carries a word which must be fixed at its first use, since it is used in another sense later in this chapter. The logocentrism Habermas would overcome is a fixation of reason upon one of its dimensions: philosophy ‘has pinned reason down to only one of its dimensions, at first ontologically, later epistemologically, and then even in linguistic analysis’, to the fact-stating discourse ‘that specializes in only one dimension of speech, the truth of assertoric sentences’, so that questions of justice, of taste and of truthful self-presentation are ‘excluded from the sphere of the rational’, and ‘Contextualism is only the flipside of logocentrism’. The release is a change of position and not a further theory: philosophy ‘reverses this perspective and looks back upon the thicket of the lifeworld’, where it finds ‘a reason that is already operating in everyday communicative practice’.[^2] Derrida’s word is the same and his sense is not: for him logocentrism names ‘the metaphysics of phonetic writing’, the privilege of presence and of the voice, and it is not set aside by a turn from theory to practice, since on his account practice too proceeds by iterable marks. The two senses are held apart throughout; that the two charges nonetheless share a form, each convicting a confinement of reason or of sense to one of its dimensions, is shown at §2.3.2, where the argument from iterability on which the second rests is engaged on its own terms.[^3]

The metaphysics the situation is said to follow must be stated in Habermas’s own terms before the chapter regroups it, since the regrouping is a reading and the terms are not. In ‘Themes in Postmetaphysical Thinking’ the word *metaphysical* designates, ‘In a rough simplification that neglects the Aristotelian line’, the philosophical idealism which runs from Plato to Hegel, and three aspects of it are singled out: ‘identity thinking, the doctrine of Ideas, and the strong concept of theory’. The first relates the many to a one which is ‘both axiom and essential ground, principle and origin’; the second makes the order underlying appearances itself conceptual, so that being acquires ‘the further attributes of universality, necessity, and supratemporality’; the third embeds theory in an exemplary form of life, the *bios theoretikos*, which ‘demands a renunciation of the natural attitude toward the world and promises contact with the extra-ordinary’. The philosophy of consciousness is not a fourth aspect beside these but the modern refraction of the three, in which the ideal essences become ‘the categorial determinations of a productive reason’ and reason ‘is active as a simultaneously totalizing and self-referential reflection’; its terminus is a theory which wants ‘to satisfy the demand for justifying all premises from within itself’ and so sublimates the independence of the theoretical life ‘into a theory that is absolute and self-justifying’.[^4] Four developments, ‘socially conditioned’ in the last analysis, are said to have problematised this thinking from without: procedural rationality, which shattered philosophy’s cognitive privilege; the historical consciousness which set a detranscendentalisation of the inherited concepts in motion; the criticism of reification which carried the paradigm shift to language; and the mutual dependence of theory and practice which undid the classical precedence of the former.[^5] They are the four themes of the opening essay in another order, and the correspondence is what licenses reading the two essays as one account. The passage from the constituting subject to post-metaphysical thinking is accordingly bound to the overcoming of three commitments held together, the identity of thought and being surveyed from a standpoint above the world, the idealism which makes the order of things conceptual, and the precedence of a theoretical life which assigns the other disciplines their place; what displaces them is examined below as one operation with three faces, and the operation is named once the tenets have been stated.

What the break renounces is stated by Habermas in three respects, and the three are the measure of everything the chapter will call post-metaphysical. In the essay addressed to Henrich, philosophy after Kant is said to operate ‘under conditions of rationality that it has not chosen’: it can reclaim no access to essential insights privileged over science, morality or art and ‘now disposes only over knowledge that is fallible’; it must forgo the doctrinal form which intervenes in socialisation and ‘must remain theoretical’; and it can no longer rank the totalities of lifeworlds, which present themselves only plurally, but ‘is limited to grasping universal structures of lifeworlds in general’. ‘These are three respects in which there can no longer be, after Kant, a metaphysics in the sense of “conclusive” and “integrating” thought.’[^6] The renunciation is not a proscription of the questions, and the preposition carries the point, since *nach* says both *after* and *according to*: the thinking is conducted after metaphysics and by the measure metaphysics failed to meet.[^7] Nor is it the anti-metaphysical reflex Habermas separates from it at the opening of the essay on its themes, where positivism’s furore against metaphysics is said to betray a scientistic motive of its own; a philosophy which has taken its place among the sciences ‘need not by any means completely surrender the relationship to the whole that had distinguished metaphysics’, since the lifeworld is present to it as a totality which is ‘unproblematized, nonobjectified, and pretheoretical’.[^8] What is retained of metaphysics is named by Habermas as a residue and its status is fixed: the moment of unconditionality preserved in fallibilist concepts of truth and morality ‘is not an absolute, or it is at most an absolute that has become fluid as a critical procedure’, and ‘Only with this residue of metaphysics can we do battle against the transfiguration of the world through metaphysical truths’.[^9] The residue is a procedure and not a content; that it is a procedure is what §2.3.4 will find to be the source of the aporia.

The Introduction described the position post-metaphysical thinking vacates in three forms, a principle, a faculty and a standpoint, and the chapter regroups Habermas’s renunciations under three tenets of the philosophy of consciousness whose negatives its three commitments are; the regrouping is entered as a reading, and each of its members is drawn from his text while the enumeration is not. The first tenet is methodological solipsism: the position of a subject which constitutes its self-understanding without recourse to anyone and, in the words of the essay of 1981, ‘confronts objects and becomes reflective only by turning itself into an object’. The Introduction fixed the term as Apel’s, and as a thesis about the theorist’s position and not about other minds; Habermas uses it in his own voice, of the ‘monological action’ an exit from discourse would require and of the ‘solitary observer’ of a radical-interpretation scenario, and its displacement by the rule-following argument is examined at §2.1.4, where the second commitment is entered; what the tenet holds about meaning, which the phrase does not itself say, is stated in the paragraph which follows.[^10] The second is the extramundane standpoint, which Habermas calls, in the essay on the unity of reason, ‘the extramundane perspective of a God’s-eye view’ and finds surviving in negative metaphysics as ‘the perspective of the radical outsider’; in the essay on Putnam the same phrase names what metaphysical realism ‘chases’, ‘a fictitious view from nowhere, an external God’s-eye point of view on an uninterpreted world’; and the volume of 2012 states the whole thrust of post-metaphysical thinking as the deconstruction of ‘the divine standpoint which by definition has the last word’, whose place is taken by ‘the impartiality of the practice of argumentation among participants to which there is no alternative’.[^11] The third is foundationalism, the theory which justifies its premises from within itself and secures, in the words of the essay of 1981, ‘a cognition before cognition’, a domain between philosophy and the sciences from which it can show the sciences their place; its incompatibility with the fallibilism of the sciences is stated at the point where ‘Every comprehensive, closed, and final system of statements must be formulated in a language that requires no commentary’.[^12] Each tenet is the negative of one commitment. Reconstructivism is what the linguistic turn becomes when its object is a competence exercised between speakers rather than a grammar read off from formations ‘as if from something lying before one’, and it displaces the first tenet because its datum is public; immanence is what the situating of reason becomes when the reason situated is the theorist’s own, so that no standpoint beyond the practices remains from which their conditions might be secured, and it forecloses the second; fallibilism is what the deflation of the strong concept of theory becomes when philosophy holds a place open for the reconstructive sciences instead of assigning them theirs, and it is the refusal of the third.[^13] The correlation is a reading of the method and not Habermas’s ordering; so far as it holds, the three commitments are not additions to post-metaphysical thinking but the form it takes in a method, which is why a deficit derived from their conjunction is a finding about post-metaphysical thinking as Habermas practises it, and not about one theory conducted under it.

The first tenet requires a statement the phrase does not supply, since what it names is commonly taken for the philosophy of consciousness as such, and it is narrower. Methodological solipsism is not the thesis that there is consciousness before there is language, nor a thesis about the existence of other minds; it is a thesis about where meaning is constituted, and it holds that meaning is constituted in the intentional acts of a subject before it is shared. Its most exact form is Husserl’s, and the *Ideas* of 1913 state it in three steps. All real unities are ‘unities of meaning’, and unities of meaning presuppose ‘a sense-giving consciousness, which, on its side, is absolute and not dependent in its turn on sense bestowed on it from another source’; sense is bestowed in the noetic phases of the intentional experience, by an ‘“animating”, meaning-bestowing stratum’ laid over the sensile data, the noema being the correlate of that bestowal; and expression, when it comes, adds nothing to what has been constituted, since the stratum of expression, ‘apart from the fact that it lends expression to all other intentionalities, is not productive’.[^14] On those three steps language is posterior to meaning: a public medium serves the transmission of a sense already had, and the question how a sense constituted in one consciousness becomes the same sense for another is posed afterwards and from within. The *Crisis* poses it under the title of a paradox, that of a human subjectivity which is at once a subject for the world and an object in it, a world which ‘takes its ontic meaning entirely from our intentional life’; and the work is not to be read as though Husserl had overlooked the difficulty, since it insists that the constituting accomplishment ‘is not that of the isolated subject’ and that ‘meaning is never anything but meaning in modes of validity’. What it retains is a priority of method. The epoché ‘creates a unique sort of philosophical solitude which is the fundamental methodical requirement for a truly radical philosophy’, and justice is to be done to the ego’s ‘central position in all constitution’.[^15] Methodological solipsism, so fixed, is the methodical priority of the constituting ego in the order in which meaning is constituted, and not a denial that meaning is intersubjectively valid; the adjective is Husserl’s own in Carr’s rendering, and the position is the one Apel’s phrase names and dates.[^16] Habermas’s diagnosis is directed at that priority and at nothing wider. In the Gauss Lectures of 1970–71 the concept of intentionality is said to have been won ‘am Modell des einsamen, in bedeutungsverleihenden Akten auf einen Gegenstand gerichteten Subjekts’, on the model of the solitary subject directed upon an object in meaning-conferring acts, and to stand in need of reformulation in the theory of language; the conclusion drawn is that ‘Identische Bedeutungen bilden sich nicht in der intentionalen Struktur eines einsam seiner Welt gegenüberstehenden Subjekts’, identical meanings do not form in the intentional structure of a subject standing alone over against its world, but only in the identical validity of an expression for different subjects.[^17] The essay of 1988 on the theory of meaning repeats the diagnosis in one sentence: ‘Husserl spoke in this context of meaning-conferring acts.’[^18] Apel’s formulation is the same and is sharper on the point at issue: the epoché of methodological solipsism was to secure ‘the irreducibility of the sphere of the pure noetic-intentional meaning-constitutive ego-consciousness and its noematic act-correlates’, and a philosopher so placed must claim ‘any possible meaning to be a virtual correlate of his intentional act’, a claim Apel calls legitimate and unavoidable for the philosopher and, in Husserl, blind to the public meanings through which alone the philosopher’s self-understanding is possible.[^19] What is displaced is therefore a thesis about the constitution of meaning, and the displacement is executed where meaning is constituted, in a rule the speaker follows with others and did not institute alone; the argument is Wittgenstein’s as Apel takes it, and it is examined at §2.1.4, where the second commitment is entered.

The displacement has a name in Habermas’s own vocabulary, and the name designates the core of the position rather than one of its results: detranscendentalisation, the operation by which the transcendental subject is replaced, its questions retained, by socialised subjects whose cognition is entwined with speech and action. The word enters his self-description in 1980, where the strategy followed since 1968 is said to have taken up ‘die universalistischen Fragestellungen der Transzendentalphilosophie, bei gleichzeitiger Detranszendentalisierung des Vorgehens und der Beweisziele’, the universalistic questions of transcendental philosophy under a simultaneous detranscendentalisation of the procedure and of the aims of proof; what is detranscendentalised is the procedure and the proof-goals, and what is retained is the questions.[^20] The definition is given in 2001: ‘Detranscendentalization leads, on the one hand, to the embedding of socialized subjects into the context of a lifeworld and, on the other hand, to the entwinement of cognition with speech and action.’[^21] The two halves of the sentence are the two faces under which the operation must be read. Its pragmatic face concerns the knowing subject. The theoretical ideas of reason ‘step out of the static “intelligible world” and unleash their dynamics within the lifeworld’, and where the transcendental subjectivity of consciousness stood there now stands ‘die detranszendentalisierte Intersubjektivität der Lebenswelt’, the detranscendentalised intersubjectivity of the lifeworld; four idealisations performed in communicative action — that an objective world is shared, that the participants are accountable, that the validity claims they raise hold unconditionally beyond their context, and that argumentation makes demanding presuppositions of its own — are presented as the successors of Kant’s ideas of reason, the last of them pairing with reason as the faculty of principles and yielding ‘rational discourse as the unavoidable forum of possible justification’, and the four are said to ‘refer to one another and form aspects of a desublimated reason embodied in everyday communicative practice’.[^22] Its practical face concerns the acting subject. The discourse-ethical explanation of a morality of reason is declared, in the history of philosophy of 2019, ‘ein Vorschlag zur Detranszendentalisierung des kantischen Begriffs der Autonomie’, a proposal for the detranscendentalisation of Kant’s concept of autonomy, and what the detranscendentalisation of Kant’s moral theory costs in the way of grade is stated in the sentence §2.2.1 displays.[^23] The operation is neither a reduction nor a deletion. It changes the concept of the transcendental itself, in the German sentence §2.1.4 displays, and it ‘leaves the core of Kant’s philosophy untouched; what I have in mind here is the self-understanding of rational subjects as finite and autonomous beings for whom reasons “count.”’ It takes from Kant’s world-producing subjectivity ‘den Panzer des apriorischen Wissens’, the armour of a priori knowledge, and the situated reason which remains is to be grasped from two perspectives at once, ‘gleichzeitig aus der Perspektive des Teilnehmers zu rekonstruieren und aus der Perspektive des Beobachters in ihrem jeweiligen historischen Einbettungskontext zu beschreiben’, reconstructed from the participant’s perspective and described from the observer’s in its historical context of embedding.[^24] That last sentence carries the chapter’s thesis about the method in Habermas’s words, though not under his ordering: the three commitments named above are three faces of this one operation. Reconstructivism is its method, the participant’s reconstruction of a competence; immanence is its site, the lifeworld into which the subject is embedded and from which no reflection escapes; fallibilism is its grade, the surrender of the a priori armour, so that what the reconstruction yields is universal only presumptively and inescapable only de facto, in the formula §2.1.4 displays. That the three are faces of one operation, and not three theses which happen to be held together, is what §2.2 argues under the name of their co-originality, and it is why an enquiry into their conjunction is an enquiry into detranscendentalisation itself. The commitments are entered below at the points where the exposition of the method reaches them: the first at §2.1.1, the third at §2.1.2, the second at §2.1.4.

The situation so described is a stage in a learning process and not a settled theory, and Habermas describes it as such in every phase. The break is dated to ‘the first generation of Hegel’s disciples’, since when ‘there has been no alternative to postmetaphysical thinking’; the volume of 2012 restates the claim as the judgement that ‘today there is no alternative to the postmetaphysical mode of doing philosophy’ and assigns the genealogy which accompanies it a critical rather than a justificatory office, that of fostering ‘reflexive awareness of the contingency of the context of emergence’ of the premises a fallibilist thinking claims for the present; the thinking so reached ‘operates “without a ground” insofar as it creates the building blocks of its “ground of validity” from its own resources, instead of borrowing them from some traditional authority’.[^25] The genealogy is then extended to a history of philosophy, whose introduction describes the process it traces as one which can be followed from within though it is not steered from within alone: ‘als einen intern nachvollziehbaren, aber nicht als einen ausschließlich intern gesteuerten Lernprozess’.[^26] A learning process has no standpoint outside itself from which its result might be certified, and Habermas draws the consequence for his own recommendation. The preface to the second volume states that the recommendation to philosophise henceforth only in the post-metaphysical mode could not be justified ‘without at the same time arguing for the concept of “communicative reason”’, and that ‘Anyone who wants to engage in the business of clarifying philosophy’s proper role must actually do philosophy. This reflexive circle is unavoidable’.[^27] The self-understanding is therefore self-critical in a determinate sense: the account of what philosophy can be is itself a philosophical claim of the kind the account permits, and it can be sustained only by the practice it describes. Its interlocutors are fixed by the same circle. Kant and Hegel are addressed directly, as the two modes of justification, transcendental and dialectical, which the essay of 1981 follows through their own traditions of self-criticism, and the concept of communicative reason is said to free thought ‘from the dilemma of having to choose between Kant and Hegel’. Adorno, Husserl and Heidegger are addressed as stations of an incomplete detranscendentalisation: Adorno for a negative dialectics which aims at ‘a negative metaphysics’ and keeps the outsider’s standpoint the second tenet names; Husserl for the German university tradition in which the methodical attitude ‘was inflated into the internally justified precedence of theory over practice’; Heidegger for an existential rooting of the world-projecting subject which leaves the problem of intersubjectivity ‘unsolvable’ and then, in the later work, makes ‘the prejudicing force of linguistic world-disclosure’ devalue every learning process within the world. The verdict is the same for all: ‘All these attempts to detranscendentalize reason continue to get entangled in the prior conceptual decisions of transcendental philosophy’.[^28]

A critique of metaphysics conducted as a claim about what philosophy can be is itself a claim of the kind the vacated court used to certify, and the difficulty is one Habermas states against others before it is put to him. Against critical rationalism and the negativism of Adorno the essay of 1981 observes that both ‘reject transcendental and dialectical means of cognition while at the same time using them in a paradoxical way’, and adds, under a hedge which is his, that the two attempts may also be viewed as showing that the two modes of justification ‘cannot be abolished except on penalty of self-contradiction’; against the totalising critique of reason from Nietzsche to Derrida the lectures of 1985 hold that it ‘gets caught in a performative contradiction since subject-centered reason can be convicted of being authoritarian in nature only by having recourse to its own tools’; and against relativism the essay on the unity of reason holds that the thesis granting every view of the world its perspectival right ‘cannot be put forth without a performative self-contradiction’.[^29] The instrument is the one §2.2.2 fixes in a bounded sense, and what it convicts in each case is a universal claim advanced by a position which denies itself the right to universal claims. The recoil is evident. A thinking which declares metaphysics finished, and declares it of all philosophy, advances a thesis about the totality of philosophical claims; and Rawls presses the recoil in its sharpest form. Habermas’s position, he holds, ‘is a comprehensive doctrine and covers many things far beyond political philosophy’, and it is metaphysical in a stated sense: ‘His logic is metaphysical in the following sense: it presents an account of what there is. And what there is are human beings engaged in communicative action in their lifeworld.’ The premise of the charge is entered in a note, that metaphysics is ‘at least a general account of what there is’, so that ‘To deny certain metaphysical doctrines is to assert another such doctrine’.[^30] Henrich had reached the same point from Kant, quoting against Habermas the sentence of the first preface on the indifferentists who, in so far as they think at all, fall back into the metaphysical assertions they affected to despise.[^31] Both charges are sound against one reading of the word, and the reading is not Habermas’s. If a position is metaphysical whenever it holds a view about what there is, then no philosophical position can be otherwise, and the predicate marks nothing. A criterion is therefore wanted which sorts positions by their manner and not by their having a matter.

The criterion is the one the vacated court applied to itself. Kant distinguishes the dogmatic procedure of reason, which a science cannot forgo, from dogmatism, which is that procedure pursued by a pure reason that has not first examined what it is able to do, and declares criticism the opponent of the second alone.[^32] Habermas’s is the same criterion transposed from a faculty to a practice, and it is stated twice in his own voice. Against analytic materialism, in the essay addressed to Henrich, a position is called metaphysical which ‘sticks to what is universal when the real issue is carrying through an abstractly posed program with scientific means’: on that sentence the fault lies not in the universality of the claim but in its exemption from the enquiry which alone could earn it. Against Rawls, in the reply of 2011 whose concession on weak transcendental arguments §2.1.4 reads, the decisive impulse of post-metaphysical thinking is said to be Kant’s critique of metaphysics, and the mode of thinking is defined by what its statements may and may not do: ‘we no longer think that we are in a position to make unqualified statements about the whole of nature and history, about the totality of being, at least not without referring to our reflexively analysed means of representation’, while ‘a discursively redeemable validity claim’ attaches to every philosophical proposition which remains within that frame, so that ‘Fallibilism and continued controversies on all fronts are the inevitable price to be paid for metaphysical abstinence.’[^33] The criterion has two limbs and both are needed. The first is reflexive: a claim about the whole is admissible only through an analysis of the means by which the whole is represented, which is Kant’s antecedent critique of capacity conducted upon the medium rather than the faculty. The second is procedural: every such claim carries a validity claim redeemable in argument and is thereby exposed to correction, which is the tenet foundationalism denies. On this criterion the second tenet is not renounced by abstaining from a view of the totality but by relocating the place from which the view is had, since ‘No “observer perspective”, however neutral, can provide the appropriate meta-level for testing the premises of postmetaphysical thinking further, but only the horizontal exchange of pro and contra arguments’.[^34] The practice of argumentation thereby inherits two properties of the court it replaces: it is the place at which claims are certified, and it is said to be a place to which there is no alternative. The first property is what makes the thinking critical of its own tenets, since its premises are tested where every other claim is tested, in the exchange of reasons, and are exempt from nothing. The second property is a claim of a different kind, about the standing of the practice itself, and it is the claim over which Apel and Habermas divide; Chapter Three conducts the dispute, and nothing about it is settled here.

The dispute with Henrich is the test of the criterion, since Henrich meets it. Habermas reads him as the advocate of ‘a metaphysics that might be capable of enduring after Kant’, one which begins from the subject’s relation to itself and returns to world-constituting subjectivity ‘as the definitive horizon of self-interpretation’; he declines the terminological quarrel over the word, which ‘does not lead any further into the matter itself’, concedes that ‘The reconstructive tasks of philosophy … are not in dispute’, and locates the disagreement in ‘the enlightening role of philosophy in the strict sense, directed toward the whole of life practices’. He also concedes the fallibility point in Henrich’s own words, since Henrich’s second thesis holds that ‘every theory of the series of natural numbers is in fact fallible’, and Habermas replies only that what holds of algebra ‘is all the more valid for ethics’.[^35] Henrich’s theses accept the constraint and turn it. A metaphysics of the elementary, which examines the presuppositions of object-determining and normative knowledge, is distinguished from a metaphysics of resolving closure, whose thoughts are ‘not elementary thoughts, but thoughts of a resolving closure’ (*Abschluß*), and the latter is exempted from no criticism: the fallibility of every theory of the number series ‘is no argument against the spontaneous generation of the series itself’, and the justification of metaphysics ‘does not require the claim to possess an infallible theory’. What Henrich charges is not that Habermas is fallibilist but that he stops short of the reflection which fallibilism should have forced: the theory of communicative action, he holds, ‘opts — albeit unwillingly — for immediacy. It regards the resources of the lifeworld, without further enquiry, as ultimately reliable and valid.’[^36] On the two-limbed criterion Henrich’s metaphysics is not dogmatic, since it claims neither infallibility nor exemption from argument; what excludes it is not the first of Habermas’s three respects, which it satisfies, but the second and the third, since it seeks in a doctrine of closure an orientation of conscious life which the theoretical form cannot supply and a conclusive whole which post-metaphysical thinking declines to rank; and Dews is right that on Henrich’s definition the enterprise violates none of the constraints Habermas sets, so that the dispute is not one the criterion can decide. What the criterion can do is turn Henrich’s charge into a question with an address. If the lifeworld’s resources are taken as ultimately reliable without further enquiry, the immediacy lies in the categorial premise on which the reconstruction of those resources rests and in the standpoint from which the reconstruction is conducted; the premise is stated at §2.2.2 and the standpoint is the commitment C2 examines, and the charge is answered, or is not, at §2.3, where the resources by which the programme sustains its tensions are costed. A second observation Dews records is adopted as a limit on the whole section: a critique of answers which have become implausible is not a demonstration that the domain of questions is obsolete. Nothing in what follows treats the renunciation of the three tenets as a proof that the questions they answered badly have lapsed.[^37]

One further distinction is required before the method is examined, since the criterion has assigned to post-metaphysical thinking two critical tasks which do not have the same form: to be critical of the sciences with which it cooperates, and to be critical of its own tenets. Habermas separates the two procedures in the introduction to the essays of 1971, where he records that he had ‘not adequately distinguished posterior reconstruction [*Nachkonstruktion*] from reflection on oneself’. Rational reconstructions deal with anonymous rule systems with which any competent subject can comply, and so ‘do not encompass subjectivity, within the horizon of which alone the experience of reflection is possible’; self-reflection, by contrast, makes conscious what was unconscious ‘in a manner rich in practical consequences’, whereas the knowledge a reconstruction yields ‘has no practical consequences. By learning logic or linguistics I acquire theoretical knowledge, but in general I do not thereby change my previous practice of reasoning or speaking.’[^38] The distinction governs the reading of the chapter. The task of being critical of other disciplines is discharged by reconstruction, the method §2.1.1 expounds, which cooperates with the sciences by supplying hypotheses about competences and submitting them to the tests §2.1.2 states; it is a task in which the theorist’s own subjectivity is not in play. The task of being critical of its own tenets is not discharged by that method, since the tenets are not anonymous rules a competent speaker follows but commitments of the theorist, and what is turned upon them is reflection in the other sense, the argument turned upon its own conditions which §2.3.1 names philosophical self-reflexivity and whose three resources it costs. The two are the Kantian and the Hegelian forms of critical reflection respectively, the transcendental enquiry into conditions and the self-reflection which dissolves a false consciousness of itself, and Habermas’s programme, on the reading §2.3 pursues, requires both while its method is only the first. A third form, the elenctic, which convicts a denial by its own performance and derives nothing, is not Habermas’s and is introduced by Chapter Three as the instrument of these chapters, under the licence argued there; the question this section leaves is whether the second task can be discharged at all by a thinking whose only method is the first, and §2.3 returns that question with its cost.

The place philosophy occupies after the renunciations is named in the essay which opens the discourse-ethics volume: philosophy, ‘while well advised to withdraw from the problematic roles of usher (*Platzanweiser*) and judge, can and ought to retain its claim to reason, provided it is content to play the more modest roles of stand-in (*Platzhalter*) and interpreter’. The place is held for ‘Empirical theories with strong universalistic claims’, whose chance of emerging ‘is greatest in the reconstructive sciences’, which, ‘Starting primarily from the intuitive knowledge of competent subjects … and secondarily from systematic knowledge handed down by culture’, ‘explain the presumably universal bases of rational experience and judgment, as well as of action and linguistic communication’. Reconstructive science thus enters as the discipline for which philosophy holds a place, the modality of what it explains fixed by the definition’s own adverb, *presumably*; the method and the grade of its results are expounded at §2.1.1 and §2.1.2.[^39] What the passage records is an exchange of offices. Philosophy relinquishes the usher’s warrant, the authority to assign each science its place, whose assignments are final because the authority which draws them certifies them itself and exempts them from correction by the sciences it assigns, and takes up the stand-in’s, which holds a position open for theories whose results it neither anticipates nor certifies, and whose own results are put up for discussion as theirs are. On the reading adopted here it is the fallibilism, and no retreat from the claim to reason, which distinguishes the two offices: what has been renounced is a tribunal.[^40] After metaphysics philosophy ‘surrenders its extraordinary status’, is ‘no longer in possession of an affirmative theory of the good life’, and the lifeworld to which its gaze reverts is ‘now present only as horizon and background’ and ‘evades the grasp of theoretical objectification’;[^41] a discipline whose subject-matter is present to it only as horizon has no vantage upon that horizon, and the constraint this places upon its self-reflection is drawn out at §2.3.1.

### §2.1.1 · Reconstruction against explanation: object, method, the two theses, and the first commitment (C1)

The method by which that reconstructive science proceeds is best taken against its nearest rival, and Habermas separates the two by their object and not by their technique. Reconstructive procedures, he writes in 1976, are not characteristic of sciences developing nomological hypotheses about domains of observable events but of sciences which ‘systematically reconstruct the intuitive knowledge of competent subjects’, their data gathered by ‘a well-thought-out maeutic method of interrogation’. The essay of 1983 fixes the object in the same terms: formal-pragmatic analysis ‘focuses on the general and necessary conditions for the validity of symbolic expressions and achievements’ and concerns ‘rational reconstructions of the know-how of subjects who are capable of speech and action’, subjects who are credited with the ability to produce valid utterances and to tell, at least intuitively, valid from invalid ones. The grade is stated in a deflationary register. The theory of argumentation ‘does not have to provide a derivation for such a system in the sense of a transcendental deduction’, since ‘a reliable procedure for testing corresponding reconstructive hypotheses suffices’; the 1981 work opens by surrendering the ‘transcendental-philosophical hopes for an aprioristic reconstruction of the equipment of a nonempirical species subject’, and records that the theorist’s interpretation is exposed to the same criticism as the utterances he interprets.[^42] What the reconstructive commitment costs, and what holds it together with immanence and fallibilism, are stated at the close of this section.

Two claims are in play in the formal-pragmatic definition of 1983 displayed at the head of the section, and neither entails the other: the locative thesis situates the normative conditions of criticisable validity claims in the pragmatic structure of communication; the epistemic thesis makes rational reconstruction the mode in which those conditions become known to philosophy. A method could reconstruct something not located in communication, and a normativity located in communication could in principle be reached otherwise than by reconstruction. A normativity situated in communication might be reached by routes the programme declines — by a speculative account of language as the medium of spirit, or by a phenomenological intuition of the essence of address. The declining of these routes is a decision of the programme and not a consequence of where it locates normativity. The separability is not a scholastic nicety, and its bearing shows in the shape that objections take. An objector who shows that reconstruction cannot deliver what it promises — that the theorist’s articulation is fallible, revisable, and possibly parochial — has not thereby shown that normativity is elsewhere than in the pragmatic structure of communication; he has shown that knowledge of where it lies remains corrigible, which is a different and much weaker result. An objector who shows that normativity is elsewhere — in an inherited ethical life, in an existential election, in a natural teleology — has not thereby impugned reconstruction as a method, which would remain available for whatever competences there are.[^43]

The attestation is unequal: the locative thesis receives no separate defence in the volume of 1983, its nearest standing in the Gauss Lectures, drawn by contrast with nomological theory. That absence need not be read as an oversight, though a programme’s foundational commitment is expected to be its most heavily defended proposition. A commitment determining what counts as an instance of the enquiry cannot be derived from an instance of it: a programme offering such a derivation would exhibit a circularity, not discharge a burden. The locative thesis is accordingly attested in the practice of reconstruction rather than argued from a result. A method reconstructing competences exercised between speakers, rather than deducing categories from a solitary consciousness, exhibits the location of validity in the pragmatic structure of communication in what it takes as its data; it does not thereby establish that location, and no result of the enquiry could, since any such result presupposes the commitment which individuates the enquiry, and the separability just stated forbids the epistemic thesis to deliver the locative one.

The know-how which reconstruction takes as its object and the knowledge which reconstruction produces stand at two levels. At the first level stands the intuitive rule-knowledge of the competent speaker: a knowing-how, exercised in the production and assessment of utterances, and directed *intentione recta* upon the world spoken of — upon the object and not upon the rule by which the speaking proceeds. The speaker attends to what he is talking about; the rules are what he attends with. At the second level stands the theorist’s articulation of those rules: a knowing-that, propositional, corrigible, and directed upon the rules themselves. The interpreter who moves to the second level turns from the surface of what is said towards the rule governing it, so that, in Cooke’s rendering of Habermas’s sentence, ‘she no longer looks through it *intentione recta* to the world’.[^44]

The relation between the levels is not that of a whole to a summary. What the theorist articulates is no compressed version of what the speaker knows, since the speaker does not know it in a form of which the articulation could be a compression. What the speaker exercises is no implicit version of what the theorist states, since an implicit proposition is still a proposition and the exercise is not. The relation is rather one of answerability in one direction only: the articulation answers to the exercise, and may be corrected by it, while the exercise does not answer to the articulation and is not corrected by it. A speaker does not argue better for having read a reconstruction of the presuppositions of argument, and the point is not one about the uptake of philosophy: what the articulation renders is the rule the exercise was already following, and a rendering of a rule is no addition to the competence which follows it.

Collapsing the two levels is destructive in either direction. Collapse the first into the second, and the speaker’s acknowledgement becomes one more corrigible theory competing with the theorist’s. No privileged relation remains between a speaker and the rules he follows, and the maieutic confirmation §2.1.2 examines becomes a poll of amateur theorists. Collapse the second into the first, and the theorist’s articulation inherits the speaker’s immediacy, in which case the transfer forbidden at §2.2.2 is licensed at the outset and the whole modal question of §2.2.3 is settled by stipulation. The distinction is thus no refinement upon the account but the condition under which the argument of this chapter can be conducted at all, and to decline it is to be left without the terms in which that argument could be stated.

A consequence for method follows. Where what is sought is a theorist’s account of the presuppositions, and the modal standing he assigns them, there is no access to that account independent of the texts which articulate it; a speaker’s access to the presuppositions in practice is of another kind and is not in question. The texts are not a report upon a commitment held apart from them, since prose is the form in which this object is given to a second-level enquiry. A reading conducted upon them is therefore no exegetical preliminary to the philosophical work but the philosophical work itself. Where a theorist’s modal commitments are what one wants to know, and where he has stated them in prose rather than in a formalism, the wording is the evidence and there is no other. This fixes what may count as an objection to a reading. An objection that a reading places more weight upon a word than its author is likely to have intended does not tell, intention not being the standard; nor does the objection that a formulation is loose, since what it would have to be tightened to is settled only by producing the tightening. What does tell, and what is invited, is a different reading of the same words together with an account of why it is the better one.

Apel accepts the description of reconstructive science and denies the epistemic settlement attached to it. Such a science, he holds, ‘must ultimately reconstruct its own rule-based competence in the rule-based competence of the “ideal speaker-hearer” (Chomsky)’. He divides the reconstructive process in two: an *a priori* ascertainment of features of discursive rationality, taken to be ‘normative conditions of the possibility of the empirical-historical reconstruction of human competencies in the social sciences’, and that reconstruction. He places the self-accountability he postulates, with the validity claims and the normative conditions of their realisation, not among what the reconstructive sciences could establish but among what makes them possible, an exemption stated in his own words: the self-accountability of discursive rationality ‘does not belong, I consider, to the possible empirical results of the reconstructive sciences but to the conditions of their possibility’.[^45] The division is Apel’s, and whether it can be sustained is asked at §2.2.3 and answered in Chapter Three. It fixes the disagreement: both parties describe one procedure and divide over whether the reflection which states its conditions falls within it at all.

The first commitment, C1, holds that philosophy can lay claim to the structures of communicative rationality only by reconstruction: by taking as its data the competences speaking and acting subjects exercise, and rendering explicit the implicit know-how those competences instantiate.

*The object.* What is reconstructed is neither an empirical regularity open to inductive generalisation nor a structure of pure consciousness, but the operative competence exercised whenever a speaker acts with an orientation to reaching understanding. The reconstruction is accordingly transformative and not descriptive, and it is no two-stage affair. Habermas states the connection and its ground in one movement: ‘There is then a fundamental connection between understanding communicative actions and constructing rational interpretations’, the connection being fundamental because a two-stage procedure, grasping the course of an interaction first and measuring it against an ideal type afterwards, is unavailable.[^46] The thesis governs the interpretation of communicative action, and it is read in this chapter as reaching the reconstruction of a competence by way of the datum: what formal pragmatics reconstructs is a competence exercised in actions, and an action is identifiable as communicative only under an interpretation which has already taken a position upon the claims it raises, so that no stage of the reconstruction is carried out upon a datum secured independently of such a position; the further step, to a procedure itself single-staged, belongs to the reading and is not a consequence of the manner in which the datum is had.

*Answerability.* Because the competence is one the speaker already commands, its reconstruction must be confirmed from within, by the maieutic confirmation §2.1.2 established; and that confirmation is not sovereign: on the account assembled here from Habermas’s statements of the procedure, it is one of three mutually constraining criteria, alongside convergence with developmental enquiry and explanatory power across the cognate domains, no one of them foundational. One formulation is entered against a loss the reception has repeatedly incurred, and it is not Habermas’s: acknowledgement is an identity-condition upon the object of the reconstruction and not a warrant for its content, settling whose competence is described and nothing about whether the description is accurate or general; a further objection to the procedure, from the side of power, is met in the note.[^47]

A more serious objection comes from the transcendental-pragmatic side, and its seriousness is owed to its coming from the ally rather than the opponent. Apel’s charge against the reconstructive programme has three limbs, the first standing one page before the other two. The first is that it declines to distinguish ‘the transcendental-pragmatic reconstruction of the ultimate presuppositions of argumentation’ from ‘an empirical-hypothetical reconstruction of cultural evolution or concrete history’, running the two together under a single methodological title. The second is more exacting: the proposal that the presuppositions be tested empirically, by questioning competent speakers, is incoherent, ‘for in order even to understand what “empirical testing” is supposed to mean, one must at least presuppose the validity of those presuppositions of argumentation’. The same form recurs in a later statement of Apel’s (FTSPV 21): it does not deny that the presuppositions may be described, but denies that a description which presupposes them can certify them; it rests upon the division of the reconstructive process recorded at §2.1.1, and the route it prefers, strict reflection, is the claim stated at §2.2.3 and examined at Chapter Three §3.3.3. The third is not methodological at all: on Apel’s charge, the modesty claimed for stand-in and interpreter is an appeal to modesty and to the democratisation of cross-disciplinary dialogue, and conflates methodological with sociopsychological categories.[^48]

The replies entered against the three limbs are offered on the programme’s behalf and not drawn from it, and the second is taken first. It is conceded, under a restriction which is the whole of the concession: no empirical finding could confirm or disconfirm the presuppositions *as enacted*, and the procedure Habermas describes asks none to. What the three criteria certify is the articulation. The hypothetical elements are the two Habermas himself lodges in substeps (2b) and (2c), and he lodges no third, so that what is established is a limit upon the procedure he describes and not upon one assembled here for the purpose; the cognate sciences enter as indirect corroboration and not as foundation, Habermas’s own characterisation, and no empirical finding bears upon the presuppositions as enacted in either direction — not because corroboration is weak, since a single counterexample may refute where no finding confirms, but because an enactment is not the kind of item a finding confirms or refutes. What the counterexample procedure of substep (2c) can refute is the articulation and its scope. The criteria so construed are no test of the presuppositions but a constraint upon their statement, and the incoherence Apel alleges does not arise. The first limb is answered by the distinction C1 has been drawing throughout: what is reconstructed fallibly is the *Was*, what is exhibited elenctically is the *Dass*, on the ground §2.2.2 supplies, so that the two enterprises Apel finds run together are here held apart. That answer is available to the reconstruction offered here and not, without more, to the letter of the programme, since Habermas nowhere draws the line in Apel’s terms; against the letter the charge therefore holds, as a demand rather than a refutation, since a reconstructive science which does not mark the boundary will be read as having crossed it. The third limb alleges a conflation of categories, methodological with sociopsychological, which is a fault in an argument and not in an author. The charge asks whether the doctrine of the stand-in substitutes a posture of parity among the disciplines for a determination of what philosophy can certify, and it would succeed if the two offices were distinguished by their manner; they are not, stand-in and usher being separated by finality against provisionality, a term of method which carries no sociology with it. What Apel has identified is narrower and more exacting, that the doctrine’s usual statements foreground the posture and leave the methodological content to be supplied; supplying it is C3’s office, and whether what C3 supplies amounts to a modesty or to an abdication is decided at §2.2.3.[^49]

*The modal status of the results.* §2.1.2 below pairs the second and third of the failure-modes Habermas enumerates at MCCA 32 with the two substeps of the reconstructive procedure, the first, a false choice of examples, bearing upon the data and upon neither substep, and what this section exploits is the ranking he gives: of the three ways a reconstruction may fail, the one he marks ‘even more frequently’ is overgeneralisation, so that the corrigibility he concedes bears most frequently, on his own ranking, upon the scope of the reconstruction, the two remaining modes, one of which bears upon its content, being conceded in the same sentence.[^50] What hypothetical status amounts to in practice is stated most plainly where the reconstruction is most ambitious. No one, Habermas writes of the system of rights, can claim access to it in the singular apart from the interpretations history has already made available to her; the system of rights ‘does not exist in transcendental purity’, and what can instruct a generalising reconstruction of the intuitions guiding the intersubjective practice of self-legislation is two hundred years of European constitutional law.[^51] Reconstruction works from historically available models, whose provenance is neither concealed nor apologised for; what the admission does not concede is the inference the provincialism charge draws from it, which the distinction C2 states at §2.1.4 blocks, so that models drawn from one tradition may instruct a reconstruction whose object is not confined to it, and whether they do is a question their provenance does not settle.

The position which emerges is not intermediate between *Letztbegründung* and global fallibilism but categorially distinct from each, and the two respects in which it departs from them do not lie upon one scale:[^52] it grounds otherwise than the first, certifying no propositional catalogue at all and conceding that every articulation of the presuppositions, Apel’s own included, remains a reconstruction answerable to the practice it renders; and it anchors otherwise than the second, since the denial of the conditions, performed in argumentation, is self-defeating in the deed, whether or not any theory has correctly described what is denied. How the position stands to Kuhlmann’s strict reflection and to Wellmer’s negative justification is fixed at Chapter Three §3.3.3; the self-defeat of the denial is what the third answer to the yield-question of §2.2.1 — the question of what modal status the reflexive exhibition confers — and the reduction of the compulsion to a sanction, refused at §2.2.2, deny, and the licensing of the elenctic result itself falls to Chapter Three. The cost is this: the position claims to secure the conditions against scepticism, a claim whose title is examined at Chapter Three §3.2, and does not secure the account given of them, and that is the *Begründungsdefizit* under its constitutive description, the cost of the position and not an incidental consequence of it.

One difficulty reaches C1 at the level of its own procedure, and Habermas states it himself, in another connection. Discussing the moral point of view he grants that actually entering discourse belongs to the idea of it, since ‘otherwise the solipsistic character of an examination of norms undertaken “in the privacy of individual reflection” would reassert itself’, and he names what must be avoided, the isolated subject who takes upon himself, as ‘custodian of the transcendental’, the examination of norms for everyone else. For practical discourse the remedy is actual engagement. That remedy is unavailable to the reconstruction of the presuppositions of argumentation itself, which is conducted by a theorist alone and issues in a claim about what any arguer whatever must presuppose, the ‘necessary and general’ of the sentence §2.1.2 displays below; no discourse actually convened could canvass that constituency.[^53] A reply is available to the programme: formal pragmatics is a fallible reconstructive science, which renders explicit what competent subjects have already mastered, claims no *a priori* derivation, and submits its results for correction to the speakers whose competence it reconstructs, so that the theorist legislates for no one. The charge lapses for the content and not for the modality, and the distinction is what the residue consists in. What competent speakers can correct is *what* the reconstruction says they presuppose; what no assent from them can supply is warrant for the claim that these presuppositions are unavoidable for *any* arguer, since assent, however wide, is assent from a finite set, and the modal claim outruns every such set. The correction which the reconstruction properly invites therefore cannot reach the feature of the reconstruction which does the philosophical work. This is, in its methodological form, what may be named the projection gap: the distance between the finite set whose assent a reconstruction can gather and the constituency over which its modal claim ranges, the *Begründungsdefizit* met at the level of the theory’s own procedure rather than at the level of its object; its practical form is stated at §2.2.3 and pursued in Chapter Four.

Apel’s defence is addressed to this difficulty, and his distinction is taken while his conclusion is refused. He holds his transcendental-pragmatic argument untouched by detranscendentalisation on the ground that, unlike Strawson’s, it concerns not the propositional content of arguments nor the categorial schemes of knowledge but the performative part of the argument by which validity claims are raised, adding that even were every transcendental argument about categorial schemes relativised, the speech act of raising that question would lay claim to the presuppositions at issue.[^54] The position so defended is two-level: Apel opposes not detranscendentalisation *tout court* but its extension to the conditions of argumentation, granting elsewhere a fallibilistic relativisation of Kant’s constitutive principles. The distinction is correct and is adopted; the inference drawn from it is not. What the performative locus secures is that an arguer cannot, while arguing, repudiate what arguing requires, an operative undeniability fixed at §2.2.2; what it does not secure is the propositional claim that the presuppositions so exhibited are the presuppositions of argument as such, a claim made in a proposition, by a theorist, which stands under whatever fallibility propositions stand under. The asymmetry between an enacted *Dass* and its propositional articulation therefore tells here against the philosopher from whom it is taken. Nothing in this shows the reconstruction false; the conclusion is that its warrant cannot be of the kind the reconstruction itself supplies. A claim about what cannot be repudiated in performance is not established by a description of performances, however competent, but, if at all, by an exhibition which the one addressed carries out. The exhibition so named shares its grammar with Apel’s *Aufweis*, the reflexive exhibition by which a presupposition is shown undeniable in the act which would deny it, and parts from it at the yield: what the addressee’s own performance establishes is operative and indexed to the occasion of its enactment, and the elenctic route claims nothing propositional from it in the theorist’s person, where the transcendental-pragmatic route reads a *Letztbegründung* off the same exhibition. Nor is that reading rescued, so far as reconstructive science reaches, by taking the propositions of strict reflection for the enactment’s own self-explication rather than a description of it: an explication put to the tests of recognition and counterexample stands under them whatever its parentage, and neither test discriminates the modality claimed for it (§2.1.2). Whether an explication reached by strict reflection is put to those tests at all, or claims an exemption from them, is the question §2.2.3 states and Chapter Three §3.3.3 decides; the same bounded ground meets the rejoinder available to Apel, that a science which reconstructs its own competence thereby closes the interval between the performance and its reconstruction. What is thereby established is negative: the warrant, if there is one, is not of the kind a description of performances supplies. The elenctic route is the one this argument leaves standing, and Chapter Three takes it up at §3.2 as a route in its own right and not as a repair offered to Habermas.

### §2.1.2 · Hypothetical status, the two loci of corrigibility, the silence of reconstruction, and the third commitment (C3)

Habermas states the proviso in enumerating the steps his justification of the universalisation principle (U) requires, the enumeration belonging to Chapter One §1.3.3. The method’s concern is the second step: the identification of pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation which are inescapable and normative in content. That step, ‘for which the search for performative contradictions provides a guide, relies upon a maieutic method’ discharging three offices. It serves to make a sceptic ‘aware of presuppositions he knows intuitively’; ‘to cast this pretheoretical knowledge in an explicit form that will enable the skeptic to recognize his intuitions in this description’; and ‘to corroborate, through counterexamples, the proponent’s assertion that there are no alternatives to the presuppositions he has made explicit’. Habermas labels these (2a), (2b) and (2c); the argument below concerns the last two.[^55]

Of those two he says that they ‘contain unmistakable hypothetical elements’. The description making the passage from knowing how to knowing that is ‘a hypothetical reconstruction that can provide only a more or less correct rendering of intuitions’, and ‘it needs maieutic confirmation’. Three characterisations are thereby made: it is a proposal about the competence, not a transcript; its correctness is qualified in advance as approximate; its warrant lies in elicited assent, not in a deduction dispensing with assent. A fourth follows, hinged to the third, in the English of record, by ‘Similarly’: the assertion that no alternative to a given presupposition exists, ‘that it is one of the inescapable (i.e., necessary and general) presuppositions, has the status of an assumption’, and, ‘like a lawlike hypothesis’ — *Gesetzeshypothese* — ‘it must be checked against individual cases’.[^56]

The parenthesis is conceded first. Habermas glosses inescapability as comprising necessity and generality, so that what falls under the status of an assumption is a conjunction with necessity as a conjunct; a reading confining that status to generality is not what the sentence says. What licenses a narrower result is the test Habermas prescribes. A lawlike hypothesis is checked against individual cases, and the individual cases of a presupposition of argumentation are argumentations. Such a check can establish that no argumentation has been found lacking the presupposition, not that none could have lacked it: a presupposition holding of every arguer as a matter of fact would survive it as intact as one no arguer could lack. The necessity conjunct is thus hypothesised without being testable by the procedure assigned to it. A cognate admission stands in the essay of 1976: ‘Every reconstruction of a basic conceptual system of possible experience has to be regarded as a hypothetical proposal that can be tested against new experiences’, and ‘We can no longer exclude the possibility that our concepts of objects of possible experience can be successfully applied only under contingent boundary conditions’.[^57] The proviso of 1983 is stated in a single passage:

‘To be sure, the intuitive knowledge of rules that subjects capable of speech and action must use if they are to be able to participate in argumentation is in a certain sense not fallible. But this is not true of our reconstruction of this pretheoretical knowledge and the claim to universality that we connect with it. The certainty with which we put our knowledge of rules into practice does not extend to the truth of proposed reconstructions of presuppositions hypothesized to be general’.[^58]

The concessive (‘To be sure’) marks the non-fallibility as granted, not argued; the hedge (‘in a certain sense’) withholds the sense in which it holds. The concession is then withheld from two constructions: from ‘our reconstruction of this pretheoretical knowledge’, and from ‘the claim to universality that we connect with it’. The hypothetical elements were lodged, a few sentences earlier, in substeps (2b) and (2c). The reading that the two sentences answer to the two substeps one apiece — the description-and-confirmation sentence to (2b), the ‘Similarly’-sentence to (2c) — is a reading, and it is the one the sentences’ own subject-matter invites: the first speaks of the description and its confirmation, the second of the assertion that no alternative exists and its check against cases. What is added here is the consequence. The German verb of the last sentence is *sich übertragen*: the certainty of practice ‘überträgt sich nicht auf die Wahrheit von Rekonstruktionsvorschlägen für hypothetisch allgemeine Präsuppositionen’, does not transfer itself to the truth of reconstruction proposals for presuppositions hypothesised to be general. The passage of a practice’s certainty to the truth of a reconstruction proposal is named a transfer, and denied, by Habermas himself; what he denies is the transfer of certainty, his reason being that a reconstruction is put up for discussion as a logician’s description is.[^59] The transfer §2.2.2 forbids is of modal standing, and it is closed on each of the two routes reconstruction commands, description and self-application (§2.2.3); the sentence of 1983 is its nearest textual analogue and not its warrant.

Description and generality-claim are advanced, warranted and tested separately, and separately liable to fail; both are propositional. Substep (2b) stands or falls by a speaker’s recognition of the description offered him, a first-personal acknowledgement; (2c) by the search for a deviant case, a third-personal check upon a generalisation. A reconstruction could render correctly what competent speakers do while the claim that all speakers must do it remained unwarranted; two loci of corrigibility are thus distinguished. Habermas enumerates the failure-modes elsewhere in the same terms — a false choice of examples, the obscuring and distorting of correct intuitions, the overgeneralising of individual cases — the second answering to the description, the third to the generality-claim.[^60] Of any formal-pragmatic result it may be asked which of the two things it says — that this is the rule, or that the rule is general — is claimed.

Neither test reaches the modality of what is reconstructed. Maieutic recognition attests *content*: what a speaker acknowledges is which rule he was following. His acknowledgement runs identically whether the ‘must’ he concedes is a necessity no experience could correct or an unavoidability for beings of a certain constitution. Corroboration by counterexample tests *extension*, and the two are not symmetrical even in success: a recognition secured is a positive result, a counterexample not found a refutation not yet obtained. Habermas holds that demonstrating performative contradictions ‘helps to identify the rules necessary for any argumentation game to work’, and that ‘the fact that there are no alternatives to these rules of argumentation is what is being proved; the rules themselves are not being justified’.[^61] What the corroboration-test can deliver falls short of that claim: a contingent universal is as free of counterexamples among actual cases as a necessary one.

Nor is the demonstration of performative self-contradiction a third test. Habermas assigns it to the identification of the rules, where, in the sentence just displayed, it helps to identify them, and not to their corroboration. Whatever modal reach it carries is therefore the reach of an exhibition, which convicts a denial and derives nothing, and not of a reconstructive test; the licence for it is sought at Chapter Three §3.3.1, where the refutation of *Metaphysics* Γ.4 is read, and not here.

Reconstruction is therefore silent at both tests, and silent by construction: each is directed upon content or extension, and the modality of what is enacted is neither. A counterfactual variation of the practice must be put to a speaker for acknowledgement or surveyed as instances, falling back into one of the two; that the method has no third resource is a claim made here about it, and §2.1.3 gives the demarcation of the data on which it rests, the exclusion of a third test being the premise just entered. What would settle the question between a transcendental and a naturalistic reading must arrive from outside reconstruction, as a premise about modal standing the method neither furnishes nor certifies. The requirement is identified here and is not a finding of the programme under examination. Pedersen registers the difficulty as an unclarity about how the reconstructive hypothesis is to be tested, without naming its source; the tests are not indeterminate in application but silent in subject-matter.[^62] Nor does that silence license the inference that the enacted inescapability is itself in doubt; the objection which presses it, and its answer, stand below, where the third commitment is entered.

C3 f

The third commitment, C3, holds that the propositional yield of any reconstructive enterprise, formal pragmatics not excepted, remains in principle revisable. Its sharpest textual registration is the point at which Habermas confronts Apel’s demand. The presuppositions cannot be denied from within argumentation, since any attempt to deny them enacts them; yet the demonstration of this inescapability is not the justification of the presuppositions: in the sentence displayed above, what is proved is that the rules of argumentation have no alternatives, and the rules themselves are not thereby justified.[^63] The discrimination the chapter needs is carried in the sentence’s grammar, and the German is the safer ground: what is there said to be proved is *die Alternativenlosigkeit dieser Regeln für die Argumentationspraxis* — a state of the rules, and so the sort of item a proposition states, and one already indexed to the practice of argumentation, the first restriction §2.1.4 counts; what is said not to be justified is no state of the rules but the rules themselves, as followed, and rules as followed are not items a proposition states, on the ground §2.2.2 supplies. The two governing verbs are thus given objects of different categories within a single sentence, and upon that assignment the withholding is intelligible rather than merely stipulated, since justification is a relation in which a norm stands to grounds, and the demonstration that a norm has no available alternative furnishes no ground for the norm but reports upon its standing. The reading of the grammar is offered as a reading; nothing in the argument requires that Habermas drew the distinction under those terms, only that the sentence he wrote observes it.

C3 is thus not the thesis that the conditions of rationality are uncertain, the misreading the language of hypothetical status invites, but the thesis that the philosopher’s articulation of those conditions is corrigible, even where the conditions themselves lie operatively beyond denial. Three items are run together in that misreading, and separating them fixes what the commitment concedes and what it does not. The first is the standing of the conditions, which C3 leaves untouched. The second is the knowledge, had on each occasion of denial, that a condition is being enacted, which the elenctic exhibition secures for that occasion without the mediation of any particular formulation, since the denier defeats himself whether or not a theorist has described what he denies; that what is so enacted is a condition of argument as such is not secured at this level, and §2.2.2 states why. The third is the correctness of any given statement of their content, and it is here alone that fallibility is conceded. A programme conceding the first would have no elenctic result to defend; one conceding the second could not say what its exhibition exhibits; one declining the third would be *Letztbegründung* under another name. C3 concedes the third and stops.

An objection runs upon that concession, and it is entered against the instrument used in these chapters rather than against Habermas’s programme. If the theorist’s reconstruction of the presuppositions is fallible, the objection runs, then the elenctic force of exhibiting a performative self-contradiction is fallible too, and the sceptic is not caught; the presupposition may be misidentified, described in terms the sceptic may reasonably reject, or mistaken for a parochial feature of one argumentative culture, and none of these possibilities is denied. The reply turns upon what the sceptic’s denial undoes, and it is not one Habermas gives. What it undoes is not a proposition of the theorist’s but his own performance: he denies, in argument, a presupposition without which his denial would not be an argument, and the undoing occurs *in actu*, in the performance and for its performer, whether or not any theorist is present to describe it and whether or not the description, if offered, is correct. A better description would not make the undoing more secure, there being no degrees in a performance not performed; nor would a worse one make it less secure, since what a description is a description of is not brought into being by being described. The theorist’s failures are failures to identify which presupposition is at work, and not failures which restore a vacated position to the sceptic. What the sceptic would have to show is not that the theorist’s description is inaccurate, but that there is a way of arguing which does not undertake what the description was attempting to state; that is the possibility substep (2c) exists to test, and it concerns the extension of the presupposition and not the standing of the compulsion where the presupposition holds. A sceptic who produced such a case would have narrowed the claim, not shown that arguing without the presuppositions of argument is something one might do badly rather than something one does not do; the two stages of what such an exhibition can establish are distinguished at Chapter Three §3.3.6. Operative irrefutability does not, therefore, depend upon descriptive infallibility. The term is used of an enactment in the sense that no performed denial of it succeeds, and not in the sense in which a proposition resists refutation; refutation, upon the criterion §2.2.2 states, is an operation upon claims, and the enactment is not one.

The concession this requires is nevertheless real, and it falls upon the argument of these chapters and not upon Habermas. What the argument establishes is negative, that descriptive fallibility does not defeat operative irrefutability; it does not establish that operative irrefutability holds, nor what its title would be if it did, nor whether the exhibition supposed to display it succeeds in displaying anything. Those three questions are handed to Chapter Three §3.2, where the elenctic *Aufweis* is developed and the debt entered here is discharged or is not. Of the first it may be said now that the title, if there is one, derives from the pragmatic inescapability of discursive commitment and not from any epistemic privilege of the theorist; and the deficit named at §2.2.3 does not depend upon the discharge, since its second interval, reached from the warranty-structure at §2.2.3, would stand even if the *Aufweis* failed entirely.[^64]

The coherence of the programme has been contested from two opposed directions, and one distinction answers both. The circularity charge holds that a reconstruction of the presuppositions of argumentation is itself an argument, and so presupposes what it sets out to exhibit; The concession is entered here on the programme’s behalf, and the concession is the reply: the reconstruction *enacts* the presuppositions it articulates, and enactment is not entailment. Apel puts the same difficulty to himself in a sharper form, examined next; the diagnosis is common ground and the remedy is not, since whether the paradox is escaped by exempting the presuppositions from fallibility, as he proposes, or by locating fallibility at the propositional register alone, as is argued in these chapters, is decided at Chapter Three §3.2. The second charge runs in the contrary direction, holding the programme to be empiricism in transcendental dress; it is met by the demarcation of data entered at §2.1.3. Whether a restriction of that shape is principled or expedient is the question C3 must answer, and the answer is sharper than it appears, because the demand for a restriction is Apel’s and the *form* of the restriction is Apel’s too. The self-application of the fallibilism principle, he argues, leads to a paradox on the pattern of the liar: ‘If the “Fallibilism” principle is itself fallible, it is to just that extent not fallible and vice versa’. What the argument concludes is not that fallibilism is false but that the principle of fallibilism, and with it the principle of criticism it underwrites, are meaningful and valid only under a restriction entered at the outset, so that at least some evidence is withheld from possible criticism, namely the evidence upon which the principles are themselves grounded.[^65] A restriction is therefore required by both, so that the charge of *ad hoc* exemption cannot be levelled on the ground that a restriction is drawn at all, but only against the principle upon which each restriction is drawn; what divides them is not whether an exclusion is drawn but where, and upon what kind of item. Apel withholds a class of *evidence*, ‘at least some philosophical evidence’ ‘excluded from possible criticism’ from the beginning. The withholding has two parts, and the reading given here keeps them apart: the certainty is placed first in the arguer’s performative self-knowledge, since, in a sentence of 1986, ‘I do not need to presuppose a fallible speech-act theory in order to know that I make an affirmation or put forward a question’, and it is then extended to the explication which states what is so known, so far as the explication is adequate.[^66] The first part is common ground with the position argued here; the disagreement is with the extension, which keeps the exempted item inside the category to which fallibility could intelligibly attach, and so owes an account of how a proposition comes to be immune. C3, upon the reconstruction offered here, withholds no evidence at all: what falls outside is the enactment, which on the categorial ground §2.2.2 states is not evidence and not a proposition, and so not the kind of item an exemption could be granted to or withheld from. The difference is categorial and not one of extent, and §2.2.3 shows it to be what separates the two positions upon the yield of the *Aufweis*, once the vocabulary of a stronger and a weaker claim is set aside.

The commitment is examined at length in the essay where Apel submits critical rationalism to metacritique, under a disclaimer which governs how the dispute must be read: his purpose ‘cannot be the questioning of the principle of “critical examination”’.[^67] What Apel targets is the asserted inner connection between criticism and unrestricted fallibilism, and the shape of the examination may be set out in a dilemma drawn from it rather than found stated in it. Let the principle of criticism be the demand that every claim be exposed to examination, and unrestricted fallibilism the thesis that every claim may be false; the metacritique reports the second as advanced upon the authority of the first. Applied to itself, the second divides: either the thesis of unrestricted fallibility is exempt from its own scope, whereupon the fallibilism is not unrestricted; or it falls under itself, whereupon the fallibilist has granted that it may be false and surrendered the ground upon which he was pressing it. What the division permits is a discrimination Apel’s own statement leaves implicit: the exemption the first horn requires is no exemption from criticism but an exemption from the assumption of possible falsity, and a defender of critical examination may withhold the second thesis without withdrawing the first. Whether the survival extends as far as the modal conclusion Apel draws from it remains open at this point; §2.2 states the question and Chapter Three §3.2 answers it.

What the metacritique targets is not the fallibilism C3 holds, and the point is documented on Habermas’s side before it is pressed from Apel’s. In 1983 Habermas records that Apel ‘has subjected fallibilism to an illuminating metacritique’ — in the German, has defused the objection which the Münchhausen trilemma constitutes, ‘den Einwand des Münchhausentrilemmas entkräftet’, the English ‘refuted the objection to’ misrendering the genitive — and he reconstructs the result himself: Apel ‘uncovers a performative contradiction in the objection raised by the “consistent fallibilist,”’ since ‘even the consistent fallibilist has already accepted as valid a minimum number of unavoidable rules of criticism’.[^68] The fallibilism C3 states is accordingly not the pan-critical fallibilism of Albert, Bartley and Radnitzky, which both parties reject in the same terms; it is the restriction of fallibility to ‘our reconstruction’, in the sentence displayed above, and what remains in dispute between them is whether that restriction is stable.

Apel’s own line is drawn in 1975 and held thereafter. At the level of philosophical reflection one may formulate ‘a fallibilism proviso as virtually universal doubt with regard to the paradigmatic evidence of all possible language games, except for the philosophical language game of doubt’, and the distinction of levels this requires ‘is not a question of an arbitrarily repeatable distinction between levels of reflection’; in 1992 the principle of fallibilism, which is said to hold ‘for all empirical and categorial knowledge-claims’, ‘reaches its limits, when we reflect on the necessary presuppositions of argumentative discourse’.[^69] The charge against Habermas is dated between 1986 and 1989 and is aimed at the procedure examined above. In a note of 1986 the objections to the possibility of an ultimate grounding which Apel has just listed are said to correspond to Habermas’s position, the essays of 1976 and 1983 being named; the thought of an empirical testing of the presuppositions is declared unintelligible, since any test would presuppose them, so that a correction of their explication ‘kann daher nur den Charakter einer Selbst-Korrektur haben’, can only have the character of a self-correction, and differs in kind from the method of widely scattered examples and counterexamples put to native speakers; and the radicality of the transcendental question is not to be relaxed ‘zugunsten halb empirischer und insofern schwacher transzendentaler Argumente’, in favour of half-empirical and to that extent weak transcendental arguments, the phrase running over a page break.[^70] In the same year, in the essay whose following pages Chapter Three §3.1.4 reads, the principle determining the rules of discourse is denied ‘the status of a discursive hypothesis — only provisionally without alternatives, but essentially fallible’, and Habermas is charged with ‘a false modesty, understandable indeed in view of dominant relativism and skepticism’.[^71] In 1989 the charge is generalised: by extending the principle of fallibilism to the statements of universal pragmatics, Habermas has, ‘in spite of apparent reservations’, ‘*de facto* accepted the unrestricted and thus self-applicable principle of fallibilism held by the late Popperians’, the absence of alternatives being read as a merely factual finding, the presuppositions ‘necessary and universally valid only insofar as they have proven themselves up to now to be without alternatives’.[^72] The sharpest statement stands in a note of the same essay, and the chapter must carry it, since it turns the instrument §2.2.2 fixes against the restriction C3 draws. The philosophical statements which explicate fallibilism can be corrected as explications, ‘But they are infallible precisely insofar as they state necessary presuppositions of the principle of fallibilism’; and of Habermas’s sentence of 1986 that ‘There are no meta-discourses in the sense that a higher discourse is able to prescribe rules for a subordinate discourse. Argumentation games do not form a hierarchy’, Apel asks: ‘Might he have noticed that this sentence claims exactly what he denies and thus that he falls into a performative self-contradiction?’[^73]

Habermas’s reply, in 1991, proceeds by redescription and not by refutation. Under Apel’s transformation of philosophy, transcendental reflection is said to uncover a realm of knowledge ‘which, itself infallible, accounts for the presuppositions of fallibilism and thereby satisfies the conditions of an ultimate grounding’; the talk of ultimate justification ‘suggests a residual foundationalism limited to the analysis of transcendental conditions’, and philosophy becomes ‘the final haven of certainties that are otherwise unavailable on the assumptions of a generalized fallibilism’ — in the German, ‘unter dem allgemein gewordenen Fallibilismusvorbehalt’, under a fallibilism proviso become general, the proviso being Apel’s word of 1975 before it is Habermas’s. The refusal rests on one ground, that ‘there is no hierarchical gradation between discourses and corresponding metadiscourses’, and it concludes that an ultimate justification of ethics ‘is neither possible nor necessary’.[^74] The ground is aimed at a hierarchy Apel had disclaimed in the sentence of 1975 quoted above: what Apel claims is that two classes of statement differ in principle, and not that discourses form a ladder, so that the reply of 1991 meets the note of 1989 at one point only, the no-hierarchy sentence which the note charges with self-contradiction, and meets it by restating the sentence. The exchange is entered here in the form it took and is not adjudicated, since what would adjudicate it is not available to reconstruction.

What the exchange settles for the commitment is this. Both parties restrict fallibility, and both reject its unrestricted form; what divides them is whether the restriction Habermas draws, to the reconstruction as against the practice reconstructed, survives the submission of universal-pragmatic statements to the two tests examined above. Apel’s answer is that it does not, since the tests presuppose what they would test, and the note of 1989 gives the answer its strongest form: a statement which explicates a necessary presupposition inherits the standing of what it explicates, and a fallibilism which withholds that standing from its own statements says of them what it denies of them. The answer C3 gives, on the reconstruction offered in this chapter, is that the inheritance is the point at issue and not a premise: a statement which explicates a presupposition is put up for discussion as any statement is, and what the exhibition of a denial’s self-defeat secures is the enactment and not the statement, on the categorial ground §2.2.2 supplies. Whether an explication reached by strict reflection can be exempted from the tests without being exempted from argument, which is what Apel’s ‘insofar as’ requires, is the transfer question of Chapter Three §3.2.3 and §3.3.3; its application to the discourse principle (D), where the difference of classes is pressed against the derivation, stands at Chapter Four §4.5.2. One consequence is drawn here and marked as the chapter’s. A thinking which declares every statement revisable, its statement of the presuppositions of argument included, does not thereby shed the claim to universality which the declaration itself raises, since a discourse on the impossibility of universal and normative structures is advanced under them, in the recoil the head of §2.1 recorded from Habermas’s own charge against relativism; what such a thinking requires is a modality for its presuppositions weaker than Kant’s and stronger than a hypothesis, and that requirement is what the weak transcendentalism of §2.1.4 is asked to meet. Whether it can be met without the ground Apel holds necessary, or whether the refusal of a ground is itself exposed to the self-contradiction Apel charges, is decided by nothing in this section: the charge is entered as Apel states it, its target is the propositional statement of the refusal and not the refusal as enacted, and its adjudication is Chapter Three’s.

### §2.1.3 · The demarcation of data and the three exclusions

The silence argument of §2.1.2 rests upon a demarcation of the data. What the reconstruction has to go upon is of two kinds: what competent speakers do, taken as instances, and what they acknowledge, on being asked, to be the rule they were following. The definition quoted at the head of §2.1, from the essay of 1981, names a second point of departure, the systematic knowledge handed down by culture, and the demarcation concerns tests and not points of departure: on the reading given here, what a culture transmits in its logics and grammars supplies candidate reconstructions, and a candidate is confirmed or defeated by the same two tests as any other. The exhaustiveness claimed is therefore of the tests the method possesses as Habermas describes it, which is all the silence argument requires; whether a reconstructive science could be furnished with a test of another kind is not decided here; that it is not so furnished is common ground between the programmes, Habermas assigning hypothetical status to every reconstruction (§2.2.3) and Apel placing the reflection he relies upon among the conditions of the reconstructive sciences and not among their results (§2.1.1). The first of three exclusions is of the sub-personal: what is reconstructed is not the grammatical competence isolated by generative linguistics, which a speaker exercises without being able, even in principle, to acknowledge its rules. Habermas separates linguistic from communicative competence on this ground and works it out against Searle by name, conceding that an institutional account of speech-act competence may be granted in full, since it describes rules the speaker could recognise if put to him.[^75]

Apel presses the most exacting objection to that exclusion, from within the Chomskyan material it invokes. The rules of grammar, he writes, are ‘nevertheless internalized as a component of social norms, i.e. in the sense of a normative consciousness’, however automatically selected. Every competent native speaker ‘can make explicit his ability to follow rules correctly, to a certain extent’; and Chomsky is right to refuse confinement to ‘Ryle’s disjunction between “knowing how” and “knowing that”’.[^76] The exclusion therefore requires an argument, and the argument is one of object before it is one of criterion. What formal pragmatics reconstructs is the competence to reach understanding, and a rule of grammar belongs to that competence only where its following is answerable to a speaker’s acknowledgement; acknowledgeability marks the margin of the object, and is not a property every rule of language must lack in order to be excluded. What is demanded at that margin is not that a speaker state a rule but that he acknowledge as his own the rule offered him. Apel’s qualification ‘to a certain extent’ concedes the criterion and marks its threshold; granted in full, it shows some grammatical rules to lie within reach of acknowledgement, which places them under the reconstruction of another competence and supplies no case of a rule of communication followed while no acknowledgement is available.

Naturalist pressure arrives at the second source of data. Speakers acknowledge rules they do not follow and follow rules they cannot acknowledge; a programme resting on acknowledgement would be one about what speakers believe. Rorty’s case against the universality of the practices Habermas reconstructs runs here, treating such self-descriptions as a particular culture’s contingent vocabulary.[^77] The objection would be decisive if acknowledgement were asked to certify the generality of a rule; it is not, being criterial for the description and silent upon its extension. A speaker’s folk theory may be false about what he does while his recognition remains the test of whether the rule offered him is the rule he was following: what is asked of him is not a description of his practice, which he may get wrong, but an acknowledgement of one put to him.

The second exclusion is of the anthropological. The genealogical objection — that a historically particular argumentative culture has been mistaken for a species-universal — misidentifies the level at which invariance is asserted: the structure of positions occupiable by anyone who argues, not the incidence of arguing. A sharper form reaches past that reply: a speaker can be asked what rule he was following, and his acknowledgement count, only where reason-giving already has authority. The programme’s evidential base is therefore internal to the argumentative culture whose universality is at issue, and the exclusion looks question-begging in method rather than in doctrine. Availability of the data only from within a reason-giving culture shows that the investigation is situated, not that its object is. The parallel holds so far as the objection reaches: that arithmetical competence can be studied only among calculators shows something about the enquiry and nothing about number, and whether an argumentative practice is like number in that respect is what the genealogist must argue; the analyses invoked establish a provenance, and the step from provenance to the constitution of the object is left at §2.1.5 to be met upon ground not occupied here. The strong forms of the charge, which abandon the evidential ground and turn upon the reconstructive relation itself, are stated at §2.1.5, which refers the charge against presence to §2.2.2 and §2.3.2 and returns the genealogical charge to the discipline entered here. What the genealogist would need, and the objection does not supply, is the further claim that a situated investigation can reach only situated objects — a claim which, advanced in argument, claims for itself the unrestricted reach it denies to situated investigation as such, its own included, and whose standing falls under the analysis of denial §2.2.2 gives. What the exclusion leaves standing is a claim indexed to a practice, and §2.1.4 shows the indexing to be Habermas’s own throughout the dated series.

The third exclusion is of the statistical, the one most readily taken for evasion. The complaint that the idealising presuppositions are rarely satisfied measures a claim indexed to a practice against a criterion it never answered to. The programme can reply that the presuppositions are made rather than met. Three differences tell against assimilation to empirical generalisation. The object is a competence and not a regularity: a single competent exercise is evidence where a single co-occurrence would not be. The data are acknowledgements and instances and not measurements: what counts as a case is settled by the participants. Confirmation is by recognition, and the check against cases is a search for a defeater and not an accumulation of support: a further instance adds scope and not weight. Each exclusion removes a kind of datum from the evidential base; what the base leaves is a relation between positions, and that it leaves no other is a claim about the base and not about the world. The normativity so bounded is located rather than derived; the modality under which it binds is not yet fixed. Do the presuppositions constrain a speaker as what he must do to be arguing at all, as a condition holding of any rational being in the strict Kantian sense, or as what beings of a certain constitution cannot in fact avoid?

### §2.1.4 · Weak transcendentalism: the dated series, the reservation grid, and the second commitment (C2)

Habermas answers that question with a qualified predicate, and the qualification governs three things at once. It governs the modality, the presuppositions being necessary relatively to a practice and not absolutely. It governs the scope, the sociocultural forms of life so far known offering no functional equivalent for the practice. It governs the epistemic standing of the theorist’s specification, a fallible reconstruction certified by no result of the enquiry. The first two are stated together in 1991 and again in 2020; the third is drawn from the pages examined at §2.1.2, of which those two loci say nothing.[^78] The three are separable, and taking the qualification to bear upon the modality alone misplaces it: relativity to a practice is not a diminished grip upon the arguer within that practice, and the reading which treats it so yields a constraint weaker than any of the loci states.

Dating the occurrences shows the predicate in continuous service. In 1976 Habermas defines the weakened conception: ‘as long as the assertion of its necessity and universality has not been refuted, we term transcendental the conceptual structure recurring in all coherent experiences’. He then gives ‘two reasons for hesitating to adopt this usage’, and finds the expression ‘unsuited to characterizing, without misunderstanding, a line of research such as universal pragmatics’. What is defined is the predicate; what is declined is the word. In 1981 it returns as a concession about wording, Habermas preferring to ‘make allowance for weak transcendental connotations’, a clause rendered in 1998 as ‘despite the weak transcendental connotations’, so that the concessive force is not stable across the translations. The same volume surrenders the confidence that a rigorous transcendental-pragmatic programme claiming ultimate grounds can be carried out, offering plausibility in place of conclusive redemption, and, in place of a transcendental deduction, a check of the reconstructions against speakers’ intuitions across the broadest sociocultural spectrum.[^79]

In 1983 the demonstration of performative contradictions is itself called ‘this weak form of transcendental analysis’, in the sentence reporting Schönrich’s warning against overburdening it. By 1988 the predicate is attached to a necessity without the earlier concession about wording, the structural constraints of a shared language impelling actors, ‘in the sense of a weak transcendental necessity’, out of an orientation to their own success. In 1991 it marks what that necessity does not yield: the communicative agent is subject to a ‘“must” in the sense of weak transcendental necessitation, without encountering the prescriptive “must” of a rule of action’. The same formulation stands in the monograph of 1992, where Rehg renders *Nötigung*, a compulsion of performance, ‘necessity’, and both carry the tail excluding a deontological, an axiological and an empirical construal of the prescriptive ‘must’.[^80] The 1991 statement of what such arguments achieve is the most explicit. Lacking an equivalent for Kant’s transcendental deduction, Habermas writes, ‘we are left with weak transcendental arguments in Strawson’s sense’, which ‘can establish only the factual inescapability’ of the presuppositions of a non-replaceable discursive order. What an argument of Strawson’s kind can establish has been contested since Stroud’s objection that it reaches what must be presupposed and not what is the case; the objection is entered and confined below in this section, and nothing here relies on the confinement, since the silence result of §2.1.2 concerns the method’s tests and not the reach of any argument. Two lines below he states the modality of the scope: ‘Because we do not in fact have a functional equivalent for rational discourses, we are left with no choice: we unavoidably accept the pragmatic presuppositions of this demanding form of communication because there is no alternative.’ Cronin’s ‘factual inescapability’ renders *faktische Nichtverwerfbarkeit*, non-rejectability; a presupposition which cannot be rejected is not thereby one which could not have been otherwise.[^81]

The vocabulary of 1999 is German and states the restriction twice. With detranscendentalisation ‘verändert sich der Begriff des Transzendentalen selber’, the concept of the transcendental itself changes, the profane lifeworld having taken the transmundane place of the noumenal; and the ‘epistemisch unhintergehbare Zusammenhang’ of truth and justification is not to be stylised into a ‘konzeptuell unauflöslichen Zusammenhang’. In the essay of 2011, later collected in *Postmetaphysical Thinking II*, it survives inside a concession, all post-metaphysical approaches being declared ‘“freestanding”, even those which employ weak transcendental arguments’. In 2020 the modality of 1991 is restated as the absence of a substitute, the discursive examination of criticisable validity claims being ‘only “ineluctable” or “unavoidable” in the weaker sense that there is no functional equivalent for this practice’.[^82] A counter-instance stands on the page following the Strawson passage of 1991: Habermas writes that ‘the weak transcendental proof, if we can indeed carry it out as I assume in agreement with Apel, suffices to ground the universalistic validity claim’ of a procedurally conceived moral principle. He adds that it might be ‘deduced from the normative content of factually inescapable presuppositions’.[^83] Agreement with Apel upon the proof, a claim of sufficiency, and the vocabulary of deduction stand together in two sentences of 1991. The constancy claimed above is therefore of the predicate and its restrictions, and is withheld from the sufficiency claim, whose adjudication against the statement of 2020 stands at §2.2.3.

What the predicate restricts may be counted, and the count is five. Inescapability is borne by a genitive in the sentence of 1983 which introduces the arguments, ‘aimed at demonstrating that the presuppositions of relevant practices are inescapable’: what is said to be inescapable is the presuppositions *of relevant practices*, individuated from the outset as the presuppositions of a practice, and the clause of 1983 quoted at Chapter One §1.3.3, which lets the compulsion be shaken off on leaving the field of argumentation, marks the boundary of the practice from outside. It is restricted a second time to discourses ‘so general that it is impossible to replace them by functional equivalents’; a third time in the passage which names the *Faktum der Vernunft*, where the absence of alternatives means that the conditions are ‘inescapable *for us*’; and a fourth time temporally, in the characterisation of weak indispensability arguments as such, where what is claimed of a presupposition is not that it is ‘unrevisable or necessary, but that it is — for the time being — unavoidable’, a characterisation given of Putnam upon bivalence and transferred here to the presuppositions of argumentation. The fifth appears where the demonstration is most exposed: what the weak transcendental argument establishes is ‘indeed conditional on the constancy of this life-form’, the possibility of alteration being set aside as one that ‘remains an empty possibility’. Indexed to a practice, to a practice without substitute, to a form of life, to a time, and to the constancy of that form of life, the predicate is executed without exception across the dated series, the one sentence of 1991 which stands against it being entered below and adjudicated at §2.2.3; and a presupposition unavoidable for the time being is not a *Faktum* in Kant’s sense, which is indexed to no time. What the conjunction of the commitments generates (§2.2.3) is, in Habermas’s own predicate, what this indexing restricts.[^84]

Because modal status is to be adjudicated, the modal vocabulary is reserved. *Unvermeidlich*, unavoidable, is reserved to Habermas’s weaker modality, the word being his own in the German of 1999 for the formula quoted below, ‘nur de facto unvermeidlichen Bedingungen’; *unhintergehbar* and its cognates to the contested thesis of Apel and Kuhlmann, called apodictic in these chapters by a label which is the reception’s and not Apel’s, whose own predicate for the standing of the presuppositions is *Nichthintergehbarkeit*; the word ‘apodictic’ stands in his text of the deductive model of grounding he rejects and of the *ego cogito*, and not of *Letztbegründung*, and its use here marks the thesis’s reading of what his predicate claims.[^85] Neither word is used here of Habermas’s position. The reservation is a rule of usage, and what it reserves is the unqualified predicate and not the word: Habermas writes the word, with the qualifier *epistemisch*, in the sentence of 1999 quoted above, and his own formula makes the rule necessary. Transcendental analysis, after the pragmatic deflation of the Kantian approach, seeks ‘presumptively universal, but only de facto inescapable, conditions’ which must be met if certain fundamental practices are to be possible. Every qualifier in that formula is a restriction. The claim on which the rule rests — that Habermas’s own predicate is everywhere the restricted one — is falsifiable, and three candidates stand, dated 1974, 1999 and 2019. In 1974 Habermas assumes for every speech act ‘eine nicht hintergehbare, gleichsam transzendental nötigende Geltungsbasis’, a validity basis which cannot be got behind and which necessitates as it were transcendentally; the phrase pairs the unqualified word with a hedged predicate, the necessitation being ‘gleichsam’ transcendental, and the basis is one assumed ‘für jede Sprechhandlung’, which is the first restriction, while what it yields on the same page is the four claims a participant in communication cannot avoid raising, which the essay then sets out to reconstruct. In 1999 Habermas writes of the ‘nicht hintergehbaren Kommunikationsvoraussetzungen der Argumentationspraxis’. The genitive indexes the presuppositions to a practice, which is the first of the five restrictions; what the sentence omits is the remainder, the absence of a functional equivalent, the indexing to a form of life and to a time, and the constancy of that form of life. What it does not supply is the sense: what those presuppositions yield in the same volume remains a reconstruction. The third candidate stands in 2019, in the sentence Chapter Three §3.2.4 displays, where the word is predicated of the presuppositions a practice of enquiry must make; there too, by the same sentence, they are accessible only in a reconstruction. None of the three falsifies the rule, each carrying the first restriction and each yielding a reconstruction; none is thereby removed as an occurrence, which is why the reservation is stated of the predicate and not of the word.[^86]

A second pair requires the same discipline, which the reception has not given. ‘Quasi-transcendental’ and ‘weakly transcendental’ are not synonyms. The one occurrence of ‘quasi-transcendental necessity’ in Habermas’s own voice in the discourse-ethics volume renders *eine gleichsam transzendentale Nötigung*, an as-it-were transcendental necessitation: a hedge in the German has become a term of art in the English, *Nötigung* a ‘necessity’. The construction is older than the volume: in 1980 participants in argumentation are said to be ‘in einem gleichsam transzendentalen Sinne genötigt’ to suppose their demanding presuppositions sufficiently realised, the hedge governing the predicate and the verb carrying the compulsion. Where the word stands unhedged in his own voice it characterises a framework, a principle or an approach rather than a modality — the ‘quasi-transcendental systems of reference’ of the processes of inquiry in 1968, ‘den quasitranszendentalen Charakter, den ich dem universalpragmatischen Regelsystem zuschreibe’, the character ascribed to a system of rules, in 1972 — with the single exception read at §2.2.3: Davidson’s principle of charity ‘has a quasi-transcendental status’, and Peirce’s indefinite community supplies ‘the quasi-transcendental enabling conditions for criticism of claims to truth’ and gives his approach its ‘quasi-transcendental character’. The word is absent from *Between Facts and Norms*. In the English of the 1991 essays it stands in the translator’s introduction, in scare quotes; in *Legitimation Crisis*, in McCarthy’s introduction.[^87]

Apel uses the word in both directions: approvingly of Peirce, whose indefinite community is called the quasi-transcendental subject of that unity (Chapter Three §3.2.4), and of the preunderstanding of the lifeworld; pejoratively of the *Tractatus*, to name a philosophy ‘quasi-transcendental, yet one free of transcendental reflection’.[^88] At none of these occurrences, in either author, does the word grade a modality. Where it stands next to one, at MCCA 203, *gleichsam* hedges the predicate *transzendental* and leaves the *Nötigung* ungraded, which is not the office the vocabulary adopted here requires. *Weakly transcendental* is accordingly written wherever the modality is predicated, while *weak transcendental argument* is retained as Habermas’s own name for the argument-type of Strawson’s kind; and the German is displayed wherever the German is quoted; the one sentence of 2020 in which the prefix bears argumentative weight is read at §2.2.3. What Kuhlmann states, and Apel presses with him in the passage Habermas displays in order to refuse it, is not a higher degree of certainty within the reconstructive mode but a claim that something falls outside it, placed by Apel among the conditions of the reconstructive sciences (§2.1.1), and taken up at Chapter Three §3.2.3.[^89] The modality so fixed is one term of a deficit not yet derived, designated here in anticipation the *Begründungsdefizit*, the justificatory deficit. The word is Habermas’s, used at MkH 86 of the point at which Tugendhat’s chain of argument breaks off; what is introduced here is its application to the second of the intervals §2.2.3 reaches.

The second commitment, C2, generalises into a thesis about the standpoint of philosophical reflection what the features of the first jointly enforced (§2.1.1). The commitment is that no such reflection occupies a standpoint beyond history from which the conditions of communicative rationality might be secured more firmly than in the practices where they are operative, an undertaking the programme makes and not a limit imposed upon it from without. Its textual centre is the programme of detranscendentalisation, in the definition of 2001 the head of §2.1 displays, on which socialised subjects are embedded in the context of a lifeworld and cognition is entwined with speech and action. The conditions so recovered are necessary in the weak sense fixed above: what is stripped away is, in a summary which is not Habermas’s wording, the apodicticity, the worldlessness, and the finality the Kantian transcendental had claimed,[^90] and what is not stripped away is the necessity itself, since a presupposition which cannot be set aside by anyone engaged in the practice whose presupposition it is remains necessary relatively to that practice. It is this relativised necessity, and no weaker thing, which the elenctic exhibition secures and which the count above finds indexed, under five restrictions, in the predicate Habermas uses across the dated series, the sufficiency claim of 1991 excepted and adjudicated at §2.2.3.

The philosophy-of-language ground of the commitment is Wittgenstein’s, and Apel takes a negative result from it first, in the Wittgenstein essay and the monograph of 1967 the note cites: the impossibility of a private language, which he calls a ‘truly revolutionary insight for all philosophical thinking’ and restates as the thesis that ‘one cannot follow a rule all by oneself, guided perhaps by introspectively accessible standards’, since a solitary rule-follower would possess no criteria for the correct use of language, and so could not distinguish the arbitrary from the norm.[^91] What the argument defeats is methodological solipsism, in the form the head of §2.1 fixed from Husserl’s text: the constitution of meaning in the intentional acts of a subject before it is shared, the public medium serving only for the transmission of a sense already had. Apel dates the doctrine, in the notes to his Wittgenstein chapter, from Descartes’s *Meditationes* to Husserl’s *Cartesian Meditations*, and holds that participation in a language game ‘replaces the methodological solipsism of attaining understanding through empathy’. The refutation is not an argument that private constitution is impossible, and the difference is the whole of its force; it is an observation about what the solipsist offers: ‘the self-understanding which the methodological solipsist seeks to offer … is itself mediated through the public rule of a language-game and the “life-form” that is interwoven with the latter’. The starting-point is thus already public, had under a rule the solipsist did not privately institute, and expressible only in a vocabulary whose correctness is not his to determine;[^92] what is shared is the public rule, and not the experience had under it. The same dating recurs in the Peirce essay quoted at Chapter Three §3.2.4, and locates the doctrine’s home, on the reading taken here, in the phenomenological tradition rather than in the analytic one, the reception of Husserl’s lifeworld through Schütz and Luckmann standing at Chapter One §1.2.1.

Three questions must be put to the displacement before it is called an overcoming, and the answers divide:[^93] whether the plurality installed in place of the solitary subject is constitutive or merely aggregative; whether the theorist can reach that plurality from outside it; and whether the theory’s own procedure repeats the structure the charge condemns. The first is answered by the rule-following argument itself. Were the displaced subject replaced by a sum of subjects each of whom might in principle have managed alone, solipsism would merely have been multiplied; what the argument establishes is stronger, since following a rule admits of mistake, and the correction of a mistake requires a standard whose application the follower does not himself certify, so that a rule-follower is in a position to answer to a critic for what he does and the virtual division of labour between practitioner and critic belongs to the concept of rule-following; the middle step is contested, and it is taken here as a reading the individualist denies. The second person is thus constitutive and not cumulative, a condition of there being that competence at all. The second is answered by the participation requirement, and the answer is where immanence shows itself a commitment of method. If meanings are accessible only from within a practice, the theorist of meaning cannot occupy the observer’s position without losing his object; an interpreter who would understand an utterance cannot, on Habermas’s account, occupy the third-person standpoint of an observer, since understanding what is said requires taking up a position towards its claim, and he is therefore a *virtual participant*, drawn at least potentially into negotiation over the meaning and validity of what he interprets. He does not thereby forfeit theoretical standing but acquires the only access there is, and because he must assess the reasons he reconstructs, the standards by which he assesses them are the general structures of communication he shares with those he interprets, structures which, in Habermas’s own words, ‘provide the critical means to penetrate a given context, to burst it open from within and to transcend it’; a capacity for critique is thus recovered from the immersion which was thought to preclude it.[^94] Apel’s own formulation converges: the standpoint from which a third person is spoken about is a partial and not a complete objectivisation, the one spoken about remaining a possible co-subject who might join the discourse, and radical interpretation on the Quinean and Davidsonian model is to be refused, for proceeding as though a subject not yet a member of any communication community could begin framing hypotheses about others.[^95]

The third is answered at the close of this section, by the self-application the incoherence charge forces; before it, Apel’s step beyond Wittgenstein must be taken up, since it is where the displacement is first called an overcoming. From the impossibility of a position outside the language game Apel infers the standing of that game as transcendental: ‘One cannot decide to affirm or negate the norms of the transcendental language-game from a position outside the language-game’, and ‘The negation of this constitutes the basic error of methodological solipsism’. Where the *Investigations* disperses the public into a plurality of incommensurable language games, Apel asks in which game the thesis of that plurality is advanced, and answers: ‘I will call this institution the transcendental language game’; language is on his account not only the normatively binding institution of institutions but ‘the “meta-institution” of all dogmatically established institutions’.[^96] The inference conflates two claims, and only one of them is carried by the argument from which they are drawn. The claim that there is no position outside the language game from which its norms might be affirmed or denied is established by the rule-following considerations so far as they reach, what they exclude being a standpoint answerable to no practice whatever and not a standpoint taken within some other practice; it is a claim about the *unavailability of an exterior*; the claim that the game whose norms cannot be denied from outside is therefore *transcendental*, its rules stating conditions of possibility rather than the constitutive features of one historically achieved practice, is a further claim, which the argument from the impossibility of a private language does not reach. A practice may be inescapable and still be a practice; what excludes the alternative is not shown by the impossibility of getting behind the practice in order to look. The distinction between an inescapability of that kind and a transcendental necessity is what the whole modal dispute of §2.2.3 and of Chapter Three turns upon, and it is drawn here, in the philosophy of language, before any question of ethics arises. Habermas refuses the further step, and the refusal is what detranscendentalisation names. He takes the Peircean half as it stands, describing Peirce’s semiotics as taking ‘as its starting point the triadic relation of the sign to the designated object and to the interpreter’, and he orders the plurality of games by the double structure of the speech act, expounded at Chapter One §1.1.2, rather than by a game of higher order. What is thereby declined is not the unavailability of an exterior, which he affirms as strongly as Apel does, but the inference from that unavailability to a transcendental standing for what is exhibited within it; on the reading taken here the refusal follows from the three commitments, and its cost is what this chapter states.[^97] A further inheritance, Heidegger’s, is contested rather than assimilated; the contest is recorded in the note.[^98]

What detranscendentalisation *does*, as against what it gives up, Habermas states in two sentences: ‘With validity claims that transcend all merely local standards of evaluation, the tension between transcendental presuppositions and empirical facts now moves into the facticity of the lifeworld itself. The theory of communicative action detranscendentalizes Kant’s realm of the Intelligible by revealing the idealizing force of anticipation in the unavoidable pragmatic presuppositions of speech acts, that is, at the heart of everyday communicative practices themselves’.[^99] The tension between the transcendental and the empirical is not dissolved by detranscendentalisation and is not shown to have been illusory; it is relocated, and the site to which it is relocated is the practice whose reconstruction the programme is. Immanence is accordingly a thesis about where a tension is borne and not a thesis that there is none to bear, and the bearer is the lifeworld, a domain the programme reaches only through fallible reconstruction, so that C2’s relocation delivers the tension to C1 and C3 to hold. What Habermas describes as a movement inward is taken here for the deficit’s provenance; the reading is not his, and the movement is.

The dilemma of long standing attends any argument of the ambitious form, and it is Stroud’s; whether an argument of the elenctic kind escapes it is answered at Chapter Three §3.3.2, and only the objection and its confinement are entered here. Against this weak necessity Stroud objects that a transcendental argument can establish at most that a subject must *believe*, or must *presuppose*, that such-and-such is the case if experience or discourse of a certain kind is to be possible, never that such-and-such *is* the case, and that the premise which would license the passage from what must be believed to what obtains is verificationist or idealist, conceding in the licensing what the argument was to have won.[^100] The reply is one Habermas does not make, and it confines the objection rather than contesting it. Stroud’s objection has purchase wherever a transcendental argument is construed as a probative inference of the shape just given, and none against the operative claim, which is no inference of that shape: the exhibition that the denial of a presupposition of argumentation is performatively self-defeating establishes no conclusion of the form *Q* at all, and where no such conclusion is drawn there is no interval between what must be believed and what obtains, and so nothing for the objection to divide. What the exhibition brings about is that a certain speech act is not executed by the one who attempts it: the result is his, in the order of the deed, and the theorist’s report of it stands under whatever fallibility reports stand under.

The confinement accepts the second of the two alternatives such an argument faces: either the passage from what must be believed to what obtains is made, and made upon a premise which concedes in the licensing what the argument was to have won, or no such passage is attempted and nothing is established about what obtains. An exhibition which convicts a denier of enacting what he denies employs no transcendental claim of the form ‘q is a condition of the possibility of p’ and draws no conclusion about what obtains; it is an argument from self-defeat, and the title *transcendental* is not claimed for it in these chapters, where the word is reserved to Habermas’s predicate and its five restrictions. The cost is stated rather than hidden: what the exhibition yields is that a certain speech act is not executed here, not that the presupposition obtains. Against the critical rationalist, for whom a justification which stops has chosen its stopping-point, the arbitrary break-off of Albert’s trilemma, the burden rule of §2.2.2 is no break-off chosen but a distribution the arguer has already made in arguing, and it is exhibited and not decided. That the exhibition of it is itself corrigible is the categorial premise applied to itself; why the corrigibility opens no regress is argued at Chapter Three §3.3.3.[^101]

The confinement carries a consequence the position must not suppress, and it is where the aporia §2.3.4 states is first located. Stroud’s gap, defeated at the conditions of argumentation, stands *undefeated* wherever the programme is tempted to convert the operative inescapability of a presupposition into a propositional truth about the world, and it stands with full force at the orientation towards truth. The disanalogy is principled: the presuppositions of argumentation are conditions the deed enacts, reachable by a demonstration conducted at the level of the deed, whereas the orientation to truth points beyond the practice to a world obtaining independently of it, and no demonstration confined to the deed can reach what by hypothesis transcends it. The exception is accordingly a corollary of the reply and no exception to it: the confinement held because the conclusion drawn was about a deed and not about a world, and where the conclusion would be about a world the ground of the confinement is absent, so that the interval opens by the same principle which closed it. Followed to the point at which the confinement’s own ground is absent, Stroud’s objection locates the aporia exhibited at §2.3.4 below, where the claim this exception rests upon, that an orientation to truth is of a different category from a condition the deed enacts, is established by distinguishing a relation of presupposition from one of orientation, of which the disanalogy drawn here is the anticipation and not the proof.

Immanence in this sense is neither that of historicism, which relativises validity to particular cultural formations, nor that of Rortyan contextualism. It is a commitment of method and no thesis in the sociology of validity, and one distinction sorts it from both misreadings: the genesis of a reconstruction and the reach of what it reconstructs are separately assessable. The conditions are universal in their operative reach, their reconstruction is immanent in its genesis, and that these two are compatible is the substance of the commitment; what would defeat it is not the demonstration that the reconstruction has a history, which is conceded at the outset, but the demonstration that the reach of what it reconstructs is bounded by that history, a further claim the misreadings assume rather than argue. The contextualist objection is that the distinction begs the question, helping itself to the standpoint-independence immanence had renounced. The distinction claims no such standpoint: what was conceded at §2.1.1 of the reconstruction of the system of rights is that it works from historically available models, and what was declined is a single inference, from the provenance of the models to the boundedness of what they instruct, and declining an inference requires no vantage outside the practice in which the inference is offered. What the genealogist would need, and what neither historicism nor contextualism supplies, is the additional premise §2.1.3 identifies and finds unsupplied, that an investigation conducted from a standpoint reaches nothing but standpoint-bound objects; that premise, advanced in argument before an audience entitled to weigh it, has its own status put in question by the analysis of denial §2.2.2 gives. The same section carries the compressed form of the concession, that the demarcation of data is itself historically constituted, whose bearing upon C2 is that because the investigation is situated its findings are indexed, and it is the indexing, and not the situatedness, which §2.1.4 counts.

The refusal invites a charge of incoherence: the reconstructing philosopher’s self-description is itself a move within communicative practice, so that the theory either generates a regress of reconstructions without end or falls under its own verdict. The reply is that the self-implication is the self-application of a single commitment, and an application of that shape does not defeat what it applies. The charge depends upon a failure to distinguish a series of reconstructions, each a fallible assertion about the level below it, which does regress, from the series of enactments in which those assertions are made, which does not; why a regress of that shape is not vicious is argued at Chapter Three §3.3.3, where the arrest is the third of the three levels drawn there. Apel names the requirement the *Selbsteinholungsprinzip*, the self-recuperative principle: a critical-hermeneutic reconstruction of history must be able to account for its own presuppositions of rationality as a possible and factual result of the history it reconstructs, and the principle ‘follows from the principle of performative self-contradiction, given the further premise that the reconstructive sciences are themselves a fact of history to be accounted for’.[^102] He states it of the reconstructive sciences in his own voice: they must reconstruct progress and regress in human history ‘in such a way that the possibility of their own enterprise becomes intelligible as a result of history’.[^103] The principle states a constraint the reconstructive programme also accepts, on the reading taken here, while the modal conclusion Apel draws from it is declined, and the division is the one tracked throughout these chapters.

### §2.1.5 · Mode and theory, the three traditions, and the charge of provincialism

The procedure of universal pragmatics is characterised by Habermas in 1976 as rational reconstruction, the methodological attitude which distinguishes the reconstructive from the empirical-analytic sciences, and formal-pragmatic analysis is described in 1983 as a rational reconstruction of the know-how of competent subjects (§2.1.1).[^104] The characterisation settles the relation between the theory and the mode of enquiry in which it is conducted, and the relation is not that of a species to its genus but that of a body of results to the procedure by which they are reached: whatever formal pragmatics asserts about the general structures of understanding-oriented utterance is asserted as a hypothesis corroborated by recognition and by the search for a counterexample, and carries the grade those tests confer. A theory reaching its results otherwise would be conducted in another mode, whatever its content, and its results would not be formal-pragmatic results in Habermas’s sense of the term. The bearing of this upon modality is stated in both directions. From the competence to its description nothing modal passes: the inescapability with which a speaker enacts a presupposition, whatever that inescapability is, is a standing of the enactment, while the description of the presupposition is a proposition put up for discussion, and corrigibility is a property of the proposition as such, indifferent to the standing of what it describes. From the description to the competence nothing passes either: the corrigibility of the account licenses no inference to the corrigibility of what is enacted, the inference from a corrigible access to a corrigible object being the misreading C3 excludes. A crossing in either direction would require a further premise relating the standing of an enactment to the standing of the proposition which states it, and §2.2.2 argues, from the behaviour of denial, that the two belong to different kinds, so that the premise is unavailable to the programme on its own terms. Whether an access of another kind can cross where the tests cannot is Apel’s claim: strict reflection, the arguer’s reflexive return upon what he must already have conceded in arguing at all, taken by Apel to deliver what no reconstructive test could. The claim is localised outside reconstructive science, on his own avowal, and examined at Chapter Three §3.2; nothing is decided against it here. Two consequences follow for the commitments stated above. C1 and C2 are commitments of the mode, fixing its relation to its object and the standpoint from which it is conducted, and C3 is a commitment about the grade of its results; formal pragmatics holds all three, since it is conducted under them, and establishes none, since no result of an enquiry can establish what determines whether it is a result of that enquiry (§2.1.1). The deficit whose two intervals §2.2.3 names is accordingly, in its first interval, a deficit of the mode and not of the theory’s content: the modal silence belongs to the tests, and a revised account of validity claims, being itself a reconstruction answerable to the same tests, would carry the silence with it. The second interval, from the proposition to the bindingness of a norm, is reached from the theory’s content and not from the mode, and its derivation from the warranty-structure (§2.2.3) is not covered by the present argument.

The programme is assembled from three traditions, each of whose monological or empiricist premises it subverts: Peirce’s conception of inquiry, through Apel’s appropriation (Chapter Three §3.2.4); Chomsky’s reconstruction of an intuitive rule-consciousness (§2.1.3); and the developmental psychology of Piaget and Kohlberg, which supplies not the foundation of the reconstruction but its indirect corroboration.[^105] The last exposes the programme to a charge of provincialism, which has a weak form, met here, and two strong forms, met elsewhere.[^106] Weakly, the objection is evidential: Piaget’s and Kohlberg’s schemes were built upon Western subjects and culminate in a modern post-conventional morality, so that the corroboration is drawn from the culture whose self-understanding it is enlisted to corroborate. In its most general form the charge is Habermas’s own, entered against his programme: restrictive specifications of rationality can only be proposals for definitions or descriptions of empirically widespread discursive behaviour, neither of which confirms ‘the appropriateness of the reflexive self-understanding’ of a way of thinking as a whole, so that the enquiry moves within its own horizon of argumentation and prejudges the question in dispute.[^107] Against the weak form the reply is narrow, and it is sufficient only if the criterion it invokes is content-neutral: what is credited to the decentred structures is not a preferred ethos but a formal competence, the differentiation of the three world-relations and the uncoupling of validity-claims from social acceptance, so that regression below that content-neutral criterion is no departure from modern preferences but the loss of the capacity to hold validity apart from facticity. The neutrality is not assumed: a criterion is content-neutral if any substantive morality can satisfy it, and holding a norm’s validity apart from its acceptance constrains the form of the holding and not the norm held, so that what the criterion excludes is the identification of a norm’s rightness with its acceptance, and an objector who denies the neutrality must show that identification to be the property of one tradition rather than a position available within any. Pressed to the end, the evidential objection identifies not a fault in the method but the corrigibility the method builds in.

The strong forms leave the evidential ground and contest the reconstructive relation itself, and are met where their ground lies. The charge against presence — that to explicate a competence is to posit a plenitude awaiting articulation, which the reconstruction then claims to find — delivers, pressed to its conclusion, the thesis of this chapter rather than displacing it: that the enactment taken as enacted, the *Dass*, is reachable only under a corrigible articulation of it, the *Was*, is the *Begründungsdefizit* under its constitutive description, and the two registers, named here under their German designations, are grounded at §2.2.2, where the further claim the charge would need, that the enactment is nothing apart from its articulation, falls under the analysis of denial. Derrida’s form of the charge, from citational repeatability, reaches not the parasitism thesis as Chapter One §1.4.1 bounds it but a requirement the elenctic exhibition, in the form Chapter Three §3.2.1 gives it, makes on its own behalf; it is quoted and costed at §2.3.2.[^108] The charge from genealogy, that the decentred subject who holds validity apart from acceptance is no competence discovered but a subject-form produced, falls to the discipline of §2.1.3, where the passage from provenance to title is blocked. What is conceded is that the corroboration drawn from developmental psychology is indirect and partial, carrying the weight of a corroboration and not of a ground; where power reaches not the genesis of communicative structures but their constitution, the objection must be met upon other ground, not occupied here, and the question is entered among those the Conclusion leaves open.

The three commitments so stated individuate the programme. Each is defensible upon grounds which do not appeal to the other two, separately defensible whether or not Habermas himself holds them as distinct theses, a qualification §2.2 will show to matter. Separate defensibility is not logical independence: that reconstructivism entails immanence (§2.2.1) leaves immanence with a ground of its own, the unavailability of an external court, which appeals to no reconstructive premise, and it is the ground and not the entailment which the independence concerns. What follows depends upon the independence: were any of the three sustained only by the support of its neighbours, a deficit derived from their conjunction would be an artefact of that mutual support and no consequence of the commitments. Each bears upon the post-metaphysical character of the enterprise, and each is affirmed by Habermas in his own voice at the loci collated for the three commitments above; whether any of the three could be surrendered with the enterprise remaining post-metaphysical is not settled by those loci, and no answer to it is claimed. C3 in particular marks the programme off not from incoherence but from Apel, which is why the next section’s question is not whether fallibilism is true but whether it is entailed by the other two or stands with them in another relation.

## §2.2 · Co-originality, Not Circle: The Categorial Premise, and the Three Tensions

### §2.2.1 · Co-originality, not entailment-circle

The route through the modal status of the presuppositions, opened at §2.1.4 upon the joint Chapter One §1.4.5 located, is demonstrated in this section and terminates at §2.3.4 below. The deficit rests upon the joint operation of the three commitments and not upon any one taken in isolation, and the question is what kind of jointness that is. Two readings are set aside before a third is argued. The first, which the vocabulary of three mutually supporting commitments invites and which is attributed to no critic, would hold them to stand in a closed circle of mutual entailment — reconstructivism entailing immanence, immanence entailing fallibilism, fallibilism entailing reconstructivism — and would locate in the circle the source of the deficit, presented thereby as a quasi-logical necessity. The second would hold them to be three theses which happen to be conjoined, each defensible on its own ground, the conjunction contingent and the deficit an artefact of one theorist’s having held all three. The third reading, which this section argues, is that the three stand in the relation Habermas’s own theory predicates of its central pairs and names *Gleichursprünglichkeit*: a mutual presupposition without derivational priority, in which neither term is derived from the other and neither is intelligible without it, and which he himself holds to be clarified by a circle rather than vitiated by one. The English word is entered here as the thesis’s designation, extended from the use Chapter One §1.1.1 makes of it for the three dimensions of the speech act to the commitments of the method, and it borrows no thesis from Habermas’s uses of the German, which are reported below and left to him.[^109]

Habermas predicates the relation of at least five pairs across four decades, and the predication has one form throughout. In 1972 there are ‘mindestens vier Klassen von gleichursprünglichen Geltungsansprüchen’, and in 1980 the claims to truth, rightness and truthfulness ‘sind gleichursprüngliche Aspekte’ of communicative everyday practice; in 1981 the competence to follow a rule and the competence to say, with a yes or a no, whether a rule has been correctly applied are equally constitutive for rule-consciousness, and ‘they are equiprimordial in regard to logical genesis’; in 1988 subjects ‘relate to the medium of their language both autonomously and dependently’, and in the same passage language ‘sets itself off from the speaking subjects as something antecedent and objective, as the structure that forges conditions of possibility’ while ‘the linguistically disclosed and structured lifeworld finds its footing only in the practices of reaching understanding within a linguistic community’, and ‘Both moments are equiprimordial’; in 2001 the objectivity of the world is interlocked ‘with an equiprimordial intersubjectivity’; and in 2019 the ‘Gleichursprünglichkeit von Freiheit und vernünftiger Einsicht’ which marks Kant’s concept of autonomy is one which ‘können wir uns am Kreisprozess des Bewusstwerdens beider Elemente klarmachen’, which can be made clear through the circular process in which both elements come to consciousness.[^110] In each case what is asserted is a relation between two items neither of which is prior in the order of derivation, each of which presupposes the other, and the two of which can be grasped only together. The circle the last locus names is the form the relation takes for a reflection which would state it, and it is entered as a clarification and not as a defect; that Habermas avows a circle of the same shape at the foundation of the programme, where the explication of what rational motivation means ‘wird sich zirkulär auf den eigentümlich zwanglosen Zwang des besseren, weil einleuchtenderen Argumentes berufen müssen’, must appeal circularly to the peculiarly unforced force of the better, because more illuminating, argument, is recorded with its date, since the sentence belongs to the Gauss Lectures of 1970–71 and is not voiced here as a later position.[^111] The four idealising presuppositions of communicative action, which the head of §2.1 read as the successors of Kant’s ideas, are said in the essay of 2001 to refer to one another and to form aspects of one desublimated reason, in the sentence quoted there; the predicate is not applied to them, and the mutual reference is the same relation under another description, on the reading proposed here*./// co-originality must be put either in chapter on principled D, or in a note, for sure not here.*

The extension made in this chapter is to the commitments of the method, and Habermas nowhere makes it; it is the thesis’s, and it is argued from what employing discourse theory commits a theorist to rather than read off a text. To conduct philosophy as a reconstructive science is to take as datum a competence exercised between speakers and to render it from within. The reconstruction is therefore immanent in its standpoint, since the dimension it renders is accessible only to a participant (§2.1.4), and fallible in its articulation, since what it renders is a competence the speaker exercises and does not state, so that the theorist’s statement of it is a proposal put up for discussion (§2.1.2). Immanence, in its turn, is not a thesis in the sociology of validity but a thesis about the standpoint of a reflection which has nothing to reconstruct but practices; it is intelligible only as the standpoint of a reconstruction, and it is weakly transcendental in the sense §2.1.4 fixes only because the presuppositions it recovers are those which concrete participants in discourse use as unavoidable, so that its content is given to it by the reconstruction and its modality by the participants’ inability to set the presuppositions aside. Fallibilism, lastly, is the grade of a yield, and a yield presupposes a method and a site: it is the fallibility of a reconstruction conducted from within, and of nothing else, which is why C3 was stated at §2.1.2 of the theorist’s articulation and not of the conditions articulated. Each commitment thus presupposes the other two in order to be the commitment it is, and none is derived from another. Reconstructivism is fallible in its theoretical articulation of the concept of its own object, since what counts as the competence to be reconstructed is itself fixed by a reconstruction that could be wrong; it is immanent in its weakly transcendental acceptation, since the presuppositions it recovers bind from the perspective of the concrete partners in discourse who use them as unavoidable and not from a standpoint above the practice; and the fallibility and the immanence are in their turn intelligible only as properties of a reconstruction. This is what the co-originality of the three amounts to, and it differs from a circle of entailment in the way Habermas’s pairs do: no member is inferred from another, so that the failure of an inference from immanence to fallibilism, demonstrated below, is no breach in the relation but a feature of it. It differs from a mere conjunction because none of the three can be stated as the commitment it is without the other two, so that a programme could not weaken one and leave the others as they were, which is the criterion §2.2.3 applies to the tensions. What the relation does not do is secure any member: co-originality is a relation of mutual presupposition and not of mutual support, and a deficit derived from the three is accordingly a consequence of the form they jointly take, the form the head of §2.1 named detranscendentalisation, and not of one theorist’s having happened to hold them together.

Within the co-original three, the relations of entailment nevertheless hold asymmetrically, and the asymmetry is what distinguishes the relation from a circle; two of the relations are examined, and the third — whether fallibilism entails reconstructivism in its turn — needs no separate adjudication, since a circle requires every one of its links to close and the failure of the single link demonstrated below is sufficient to open it. That reconstructivism carries fallibilism with it is conceded at the outset, since Habermas assigns hypothetical status to every rational reconstruction in the clause §2.2.3 quotes below; the concession is not the circle, which requires the passage from immanence to fallibilism, and that passage is what fails.

Reconstructivism entails immanence, and the entailment is conceptual and holds within the co-original three, grounded in the requirement, Habermas’s own at §2.1.4, that the interpreter take up a participant’s perspective. If philosophy proceeds as reconstructive science it cannot conduct that reconstruction from a standpoint transcending the practices whose structure it renders explicit, since the dimension it reconstructs is the dimension of validity, and validity is accessible only from within the performative attitude. The claim is one of access and not of warrant, and the discrimination is added to it. It may be objected that reconstruction could proceed observationally. Were the object a competence whose deliverances could be exhaustively specified in non-normative terms, the observational route might be open; the object of formal-pragmatic reconstruction, however, is the capacity to raise criticisable validity-claims and to discriminate the reasons which would redeem them, and that capacity cannot be reconstructed without reconstructing the validity-dimension which is its point. The entailment is thus conditional upon the normativity of the reconstructed object. It is not a finding drawn from Habermas alone: it is the formal-pragmatic version of the repudiation of an extra-practical standpoint which Apel presses against methodological solipsism. That repudiation, entered below in Apel’s own voice, is what the counterexample to the second relation depends upon.

The second relation does not hold, and demonstrating this is not the examination of an inference Habermas states as such. One passage is a candidate: in 1983 he holds that Apel ‘bases the transcendental-pragmatic claim to ultimate justification, if I understand him correctly, on the equation of propositional truth with the experience of certitude’, an equation possible, he continues, only on the premises of a philosophy of consciousness and impossible once analysis reaches the level of the pragmatics of language, which is an argument from the pragmatic turn to the standing of what is claimed within it; the diagnosis is adjudicated at §2.2.3. In the loci examined here the inference from immanence to fallibilism is nowhere stated as premise and conclusion; at the point where *Theory of Communicative Action* opens the two stand within two pages of each other, connected by no inference. An expression meets the precondition of rationality, he writes there, when it embodies knowledge which is fallible and for that reason open to objective assessment. It is constitutive of an utterance’s rationality that the speaker raises a criticizable validity claim, a claim the hearer can accept or reject for reasons.[^112] Fallibility is not derived from criticisability; the two are given together. One late sentence must be displayed against this finding, since an examiner would set it there. In the afterword of 2022 to the history of philosophy, the detranscendentalisation of Kant’s moral theory is said to mean, beyond the surrender of the epistemic force of a priori insight, ‘die Anerkennung der Fallibilität von Ergebnissen einer diskursiven Willensbildung’, the recognition of the fallibility of the results of a discursive will-formation, and the sentence reads as though fallibilism were being derived from detranscendentalisation.[^113] Its scope is the results of will-formation, which are claims raised and decided within the practice, and for those the sentence states what the passage of 1981 states, that a claim and its fallibility are given together; what it does not reach is the reconstruction of what the raising presupposes, which is the object of C3 as §2.1.2 stated it, and the distinction drawn in this paragraph is the one the afterword’s own scope observes. The sentence therefore does not derive C3 from C2; it derives, from the detranscendentalisation of autonomy, the fallibility of what autonomous subjects decide in discourse, which no party disputes. A validity claim has no standing except as something raised before a hearer who may accept or reject it for reasons, and nothing outside that practice of raising and rejecting could certify it once for all. To hold that no standpoint outside discursive practice is available, and to hold that every claim raised within it remains open to revision, are accordingly not two theses Habermas connects. They are one thesis about what it is for anything to count as a validity claim — a finding §2.1’s claim of independence accommodates, since what is claimed there is separate defensibility and not separate authorship. What that one thesis does not contain is C3 as §2.1 stated it, which concerns not the claims raised within the practice but the reconstruction of what the raising presupposes. Whether that yield inherits the fallibility of what is raised is the question the following paragraphs put, and it is because the presuppositions are not themselves validity claims in Habermas’s sense that the question is open.

If that is right, the chapter owes an account of why C2 and C3 must still be examined for independence, and the account is architectural rather than logical. The reason stands at the close of §2.1: a deficit derived from the conjunction is a consequence of the commitments only if none is sustained by the others. What must be shown is therefore not that some inference from C2 to C3 fails, but that immanence does not by itself settle the modal status of everything a practice contains. Habermas’s ground for immanence is the unavailability of an external court before which a claim’s fitness might be certified independently of its being raised, contested and redeemed within the practice. That ground forecloses a standpoint *external* to practice, and leaves untouched a possibility lying between such a standpoint and the bare claim that nothing internal could be apodictic: an internal and reflexive apodictic grounding, executed from within argumentation through the elenctic exhibition of what any denial of a condition performatively enacts. Such a grounding appeals to no standpoint outside the practice, only to the structure of performative self-contradiction, itself internal to it on the categorial ground entered at §2.2.2. To rule it out requires the stronger premise that no apodictic grounding of any kind is available, internal or external, and that premise exceeds the ground Habermas states; ascribing it to critical rationalism is the reading proposed here.

Apel occupies that space. He affirms immanence in Habermas’s own sense, so far as that sense is the unavailability of an exterior and not the standing he adds to what the exterior’s absence exhibits (C2), and states it more insistently, holding that no position outside the transcendental language-game is available from which its norms could be affirmed or negated, and that to suppose otherwise is the basic error of methodological solipsism, in the passage quoted at §2.1.4; that he presses immanence at least as insistently as Habermas is recorded by Habermas himself, who credits Apel with having gained, in argumentation as such, a reference point which does for the analysis of unavoidable rules what the ‘I think’ did for the philosophy of reflection, in the sentence Chapter Three §3.2.3 displays.[^114]

Where the two part is over what falls within it. Habermas’s criticizable validity claim is something a speaker *raises* — a determinate proposition put forward for a hearer’s acceptance or rejection, revisable because that is what putting something forward for acceptance or rejection means. What Apel’s transcendental-pragmatic *Aufweis* discloses is not a claim of that kind but the condition of there being any claim-raising at all: the reciprocal structure a speaker and hearer must already occupy for anything to be put forward as acceptable-or-rejectable in the first place. That structure, on his account, is not itself raised for acceptance or rejection; it is exhibited in the raising, whatever is raised. Apel’s position is accordingly not a denial of Habermas’s thesis that validity claims are constitutively fallible. It is the claim that not everything operative within discursive practice is a validity claim in that sense, and that the reciprocal structure presupposed by claim-raising as such does not inherit the fallibility Habermas’s own definition attaches to what is raised.

Whether that further claim is sound is what the categorial premise §2.2.2 states exists to test, and nothing here anticipates the result. What the counterexample requires at this stage is narrower than truth. It requires only that affirming immanence while withholding fallibility from what claim-raising operatively presupposes be a consistent position, since an entailment is defeated by a position which affirms its antecedent and denies its consequent without contradiction. What is at issue is the consistency of the position and not whether anyone finds it credible. Apel’s architecture shows it to be so, unless the diagnosis of 1983 recorded above succeeds, which §2.2.3 declines on Apel’s own testimony; for the category under which fallibility is incurred, upon Habermas’s own definition of it, is one his target does not fall under. The counterexample reaches C3 itself, and not the presuppositions alone, because Apel withholds fallibility also from the explication which states them, so far as it adequately explicates the performative evidence, a proviso which makes the certainty of the explication conditional upon an adequacy the explication does not itself certify. The proviso does not restore the entailment: a position which withholds fallibility conditionally withholds it still, and what the counterexample requires is consistency and not warrant.

The counterexample is strengthened, and the disagreement narrowed, by the architecture Apel gives his own ethics, whose terms are taken as he states them and not as the anglophone reception has settled them. In *Teil A* the principle of ethics is to be anchored, in accordance with the intention of a transcendental-pragmatic ultimate grounding, in the ideal communication community which any arguer indisputably presupposes and counterfactually anticipates. In *Teil B* the task is to take express account of the counterfactual character of that necessary anticipation, as a problem for an ethics of responsibility related to history.[^115] Two precisions follow, and both tell against the received summary. *Teil B* is itself a part of the *grounding* — Apel calls it a *Begründungsteil* — and not the counterpart to grounding; and he distinguishes its problem, the history-related application of the ethics of communication, from the familiar problem of applying moral norms. Apel therefore occupies the space the inference denies, and occupies it where the inference denies it: he affirms immanence, withholds fallibility from what claim-raising presupposes, and locates the residue of contingency in a second part of the grounding. The disagreement thereby stands exhibited as one about *where a restriction falls* rather than about whether there is one.

Apel withholds his strongest predicate at one point, in the case of experimental action and by contrast with the existence of language, and the withholding is entered in his own voice, in the discussion immediately following his reply to Stroud. On von Wright’s account of the certainty attaching to the margin of freedom in experimental action, he writes, in the sentence the note displays, that a transcendental-pragmatic reflection cannot be credited with a certainty of the same non-circumventable quality as attaches to the existence of language.[^116] The passage does not show the restriction to be warranted; it records that Apel enters it, and its use here is confined to the record: the party who withholds fallibility from the presuppositions of argument himself marks where his own certainties end, and marks it within his own position and not along a scale joining it to Habermas’s.

Why, then, should Habermas hold fallibilism at all, if immanence does not entail it? Because reconstructions are, on his own account at MCCA 32, theoretical knowledge, and stand under the tests such knowledge stands under: the commitment is substantive, a considered position answerable to reasons, and not a deduction from immanence. A third argument is a reading of a connective, offered as corroboration and not as a premise the argument requires. Habermas notes that reconstructions acquire a critical function, a constructive role and the standing of a theoretical knowledge which competes with knowledge of other kinds. Those three characteristics, he then observes, are what has ‘occasionally led philosophers to burden certain reconstructions with claims to ultimate justification’, and he concludes: ‘*For this reason* it is important to see’ that all rational reconstructions have hypothetical status only, in the clause §2.2.3 quotes.[^117] What the connective supplies is a danger against which the hypothetical status must be insisted upon, and no premise from which it follows.

The objection that would defeat C3 — that fallibilism, if unrestricted, must apply to itself and so to the presuppositions in which it is advanced — has been met at §2.1.2, where C3 is stated, and the form in which the reception presses it against Kuhlmann, as collapsing the reflecting act into the reflected proposition, is adjudicated at Chapter Three §3.3.3, where that charge is found not to reach him.[^118] What C3 showed is that the restriction the objection demands is the one the operative–propositional asymmetry already supplies, and that it is principled rather than *ad hoc*: fallibilism is restricted to the propositional *Was* and withheld from the operative *Dass* because the *Dass* is an enactment and not a proposition, hence not an item to which fallibility could intelligibly attach. Apel’s own restriction, displayed at §2.1.2 from SE2 87, withholds an item of the propositional kind from the assumption of possible falsity, and so falls on the other side of the categorial line C3 draws.

This is why the entailment-reading could seem plausible: C3 stands to C1 and C2 in a relation close enough to be mistaken for entailment while being of the kind this section has named, and co-originality leaves open a question which entailment would have closed. Jointly, C1 and C2 leave open a question they cannot themselves decide — the yield-question: granted that reconstruction proceeds immanently and reflexively, what modal status, if any, does it confer upon the presuppositions it exhibits? Two answers are available to parties who share the elenctic result, each coherent and each immanent. Habermas’s is that the reflexive *Aufweis* yields no more than fallible reconstruction; Apel’s is that some evidence must be withheld from the assumption of possible falsity. C3 is the Habermas-side resolution, and Heath fixes the same division at the level of the grounding strategy.[^119] The division has its root in the yield-question: Apel can ground apodictically only because he first withholds the presuppositions, and their reflexive *Aufweis*, from the assumption of revisability.

The framing invites an objection: to speak of what the *Aufweis* ‘yields’ presupposes that the matter is open, when upon Apel’s account the reflexive exhibition has already yielded its result. The objection mistakes dialectical for ontological openness. To frame the yield-question is to record that Apel and Habermas disagree over the modal status of the *Aufweis*, and not to assert the matter objectively undetermined. It is well-formed as a question about what two parties committed to one reconstructive method are entitled to conclude from it, and naming it does not beg it.

A third answer is available and is not taken. It holds that the reflexive *Aufweis* yields nothing modal at all: that performative self-contradiction is a rhetorical embarrassment and no impossibility. This is not a variant of Habermas’s answer but the denial of what both parties share, since it withholds from the operative register the inescapability which is the one thing they do not dispute. It is refused at §2.2.2, where the compulsion is shown to be no sanction refusable at a cost. What the yield-question presupposes, and what the third answer denies, is therefore the elenctic result itself, the condition of the yield-question’s having an object; the constructive labour of Chapter Three §3.2 is accordingly a condition of the diagnosis and not an addition to it.

### §2.2.2 · The categorial premise stated, and the two consequences drawn

What a speaker enacts in arguing and what a theorist says of it belong to different kinds. The first is a performance: that the speaker has undertaken what arguing requires, individuated by what a denial of it, performed, would undo. The second is a proposition: the statement of what he has undertaken, individuated by what would falsify it. The two are the operative and the propositional registers, and the German designations introduced *Dass* for the enactment taken as enacted, the *Was* for its articulation — name them throughout. Stated as a premise — that an enactment and its description belong to different kinds — the asymmetry is referred to below as the *categorial premise*.[^120]

Its ground lies in a difference of behaviour under denial, and three such behaviours have to be kept apart. A proposition meets its denial in the assertion of a negation, which is a further proposition and is assessed by the standards governing the first; the operation stays inside the propositional order and leaves that order as it was. A performative compulsion meets a second behaviour, miscarriage: no denial of the first shape reaches it, there being no negation available to assert, and one who sets out to argue while setting a presupposition of argument aside has not argued badly but has not argued. Bindingness meets a third behaviour, the withholding of compliance, which is neither the assertion of a negation nor a performance that miscarries but the refusal of a force which a norm exerts upon an agent; the validity claim raised for the norm is a proposition still, and is contested by negation as any other is, so that one and the same norm may be refused validity in discourse and refused obedience in action. Upon that difference the two registers are held apart as kinds and not as grades, and the holding apart owes nothing to any estimate of how reliably a reflection might reach the first. The premise is entered here with its ground and under a debt, and the debt is named: the criterion under which the three behaviours individuate at all, what the premise must survive before it may be used, and the two objections it attracts, stand at Chapter Three §3.2.1 and §3.2.2, where the instrument is wanted for the work it was made for. Two consequences of it are drawn in this chapter.

The first is the burden rule. Where a denial cannot be carried through without its denier enacting what he denies, nothing is thereby proved; what is settled is where the burden of proof lies in the exchange in which the denial miscarried, and the distribution so settled is one the arguer made in arguing rather than a stopping-point anyone chose. Its yield is operative and indexed to the occasion, and a report of the distribution is corrigible as reports are. The second bears upon refutation. Refutation operates upon claims, and an enactment is not a claim; what the exhibition of a performative self-contradiction shows is that a speech act has gone unexecuted by the one who attempted it, and not that a proposition is false. Performative self-contradiction is used in these chapters in that bounded sense, of a denial which enacts in the performing what it denies in the content, and in no wider one. With it falls the reduction §2.2.1 leaves to be refused at this point, which would make the compulsion so exhibited a sanction laid upon would-be participants and refusable at a cost: a sanction is refused by the third behaviour, the withholding of compliance, and the denier of a presupposition of argument withholds nothing and pays nothing, having failed to argue rather than argued against a penalty. What the exhibition secures, it secures for the occasion of the denial, in the deed and for its performer; that the condition enacted holds of argument as such is a claim of another kind, about the type and not the token, and the passage from the one to the other is the first of the two intervals §2.2.3 reaches.

What the premise claims for the enactment is distinctness from the proposition and not exemption from correction, and a claim to distinctness which may be corrected is not defeated by being correctable. It is declared falsifiable under two conditions, either of which anyone may test: that negating the description of an enactment should undo the enactment, or that there should be an item at once performed and truth-apt which does not resolve into an utterance and a sentence uttered. The conditions are defended, together with the two objections the premise attracts — the inferentialist’s, and the meaning-critical objection drawn from the position Apel and Kuhlmann hold — at Chapter Three §3.2.2, and nothing below rests upon the answers. One further thing is done to the premise in this chapter: §2.3.2 turns it upon its own individuation of the *Dass* under Derrida’s argument from iterability, and the individuation is amended there.

### §2.2.3 · The three tensions, and what is handed on

Held together, the three commitments generate three tensions, one for each, and each is internal: it arises from what the programme holds on purpose, and not from anything an opponent supplies. Each is stated with what is claimed for it, and the criterion by which it is assigned to a commitment is given at the end.

The first is the tension of the relation, and it belongs to reconstructivism. The object of the reconstruction is a competence exercised, and the exercise of a presupposition of argument is, on the testimony of both programmes, not to be set aside by anyone who argues; the yield of the reconstruction is a description which is put forward for discussion as a logician’s is, and which has, in the clause of 1983 Habermas himself supplies, ‘only hypothetical status’.[^121] The programme is thus committed to an irrefutability it can specify only fallibly: the inescapability is enacted, the account of it is revisable, and the two tests §2.1.2 examined, recognition and counterexample, reach the content and the extension of the account and not the standing of what it describes. Nor can the standing be carried across by either route the method commands. The route of description is closed by the passage §2.1.2 displays, which withholds from the reconstruction the certainty of the practice it reconstructs, together with the premise of §2.2.2, under which the two stand as proposition to enactment; the route of self-application, on which a science that reconstructs its own competence would thereby close the interval between the performance and its reconstruction, is closed by fallibilism applied reflexively, the reconstruction of the presuppositions being itself an argument and claiming no exemption. The tension is not a contradiction, since the premise assigns the two standings to different kinds; it is the question §2.2.1 named the yield-question, what modal status the reflexive exhibition confers upon what it exhibits. Apel’s answer is that a reflection of another kind carries what the tests cannot: at the reflexive face of the performative, where the arguer thematises what his own arguing enacts, the difference between enacting and describing lapses, and the explication so reached is certain so far as it is adequate. Habermas’s diagnosis of that answer in 1983, that the certainty rests upon equating propositional truth with the experience of certitude, which he holds possible only where a philosophy of consciousness supplies the premises (§2.2.1), is declined on Apel’s own testimony that his procedure renews the problematic of self-reflection ‘in a non-psychological way’;[^122] what remains in dispute between the programmes is not the paradigm but the yield. Whether an access of that kind exists, and whether the passage from the enacted token to the necessary type can be made by it, is the first interval of the deficit, and it is handed to Chapter Three, the chapter of the irrefutability dispute, with the localisation just given.

The second is the tension of the standpoint, and it belongs to immanence. The reconstruction is conducted from within the practice whose conditions it renders, by a theorist who is a virtual participant and has no exterior (C2), and it claims for what it renders a reach which no evidence gathered from within the practice can certify: the assent of the speakers who correct a description is assent from a finite set, and the claim that the presuppositions are unavoidable for any arguer outruns every such set. This is the projection gap C1 named, and it has a practical as well as a methodological form: the community actually arguing is always finite, and the universality claimed for a norm justified in its discourse is not. The tension shows at two places examined below. It shows where the reconstruction is asked to account for its own possibility as a result of what it reconstructs, the requirement Apel names the *Selbsteinholungsprinzip* and Habermas meets with the genealogy of learning processes (§2.3.1); and it shows where the exhibition on which the confutative method rests makes a requirement on its own behalf, that a denial be recognisable as the denial of what the denying enacts, which Derrida’s argument from iterability tests (§2.3.2). Neither the provincialism charge of §2.1.5 nor the genealogical charge of §2.1.3 resolves the tension against the programme, since each shows the investigation to be situated and neither shows its object to be; what survives them is a claim indexed to a practice, whose indexing §2.1.4 counted.

The third is the tension of the grade, and it belongs to fallibilism. Every articulation of the presuppositions is revisable, and the programme nevertheless undertakes to justify a principle of universalisation whose validity is to bind, and to derive it from the content of presuppositions so articulated (Chapter One §1.3.3). What the reconstruction can confer upon a norm is the grade its tests confer, a hypothesis corroborated by recognition and by the absence of a counterexample; what the programme’s deontological undertaking requires is a *Sollgeltung*, the mode of validity attaching to norms in the sense Chapter One §1.3.3 fixes, which no corroboration confers. The interval is reached on two routes. On the categorial route it is the premise of §2.2.2 applied once more: the standing of what a speaker enacts does not pass to the norm whose validity the reconstructed presuppositions are said to secure, since a norm’s bindingness is a third kind of item, refused by the withholding of compliance, and neither an enactment nor a proposition. The interval between it and the other two is the second of the two this section reaches. On the route of the illocutionary bond, which uses none of the three commitments, it follows from the warranty-structure as Chapter One §1.4.2 analyses it, a rightness claim warranting that reasons for a norm’s validity can be produced and thereby deferring to a validity it does not institute; what that deferral costs a procedure is drawn out at Chapter Four §4.1.1, and is not drawn out here. Habermas states both halves of the result in 2020, in two sentences which stand together: ‘Therefore, the mode of the normative validity of moral statements cannot be derived from the idealized content of any presupposition of discourse but only from the obligatory meaning and the socially integrating force of social norms that demand recognition and actual respect from acting subjects’; and ‘The quasi-transcendental necessitation of unavoidable presuppositions of argumentation alone cannot generate morally binding force. This force belongs instead to the norms that are introduced into moral–practical discourses because they have become problematic.’[^123] The sentence of 1991 displayed at §2.1.4, on which the weak transcendental proof is said to suffice to ground the universalistic validity claim of a procedurally conceived moral principle, is not withdrawn by these: it concerns the universality of the principle, and what the statement of 2020 withholds is the derivation of the ought’s bindingness from the presuppositions, so that the two fall on either side of the interval; the reading is offered as a reading. The deficit so reached, with the objections to it and with the architecture *Faktizität und Geltung* builds upon the discourse principle as the programme’s own answer to where bindingness is constituted if not in the presuppositions of argument, is handed to Chapter Four.

The assignment of the tensions to the commitments is not a mnemonic, and its criterion is this: each tension is resolved, while the programme’s claim stands, only by weakening the commitment to which it is assigned. The tension of the relation is resolved by admitting an access of another kind, strict reflection, which surrenders the exclusiveness reconstructivism asserts and is Apel’s course; the tension of the standpoint by admitting a standpoint beyond the practice from which the reach of its conditions might be certified, which surrenders immanence; the tension of the grade by exempting the ground of the principle from revision, which surrenders fallibilism and is again Apel’s course, in the *Letztbegründung* of the principle of ethics. Each tension draws upon more than its own commitment — the second upon the finite assent C1 gathers, the third upon the deontological undertaking — but what dissolves it is the loss of one commitment only, and that is the commitment which owns it; the cost of each tension is accordingly the cost of a commitment, which is what §2.3 requires when it asks what sustaining the programme costs. A programme holding the three on purpose can take none of the three courses without ceasing to be the programme; a fourth course, the withdrawal of the deontological undertaking itself, resolves nothing but changes the programme, and is examined in Chapter Four, where Habermas’s statement of 2020 and the legal form are read. What remains to the programme is to sustain the tensions from within, meeting each objection by a bounded concession whose boundary is then declared, and that discipline is what §2.3 names philosophical self-reflexivity and examines in the three resources Habermas brings to it. Whether the discipline reaches the aporia of truth, or leaves it standing as a limit, is the question with which the chapter ends.

## §2.3 · Habermas’s Response, the Cost of the Categorial Premise, and the Aporia as Limit

### §2.3.1 · Self-reflexivity, and the three resources with their costs

The tensions the three commitments generate from within, named at §2.2.3 after §2.1 and §2.2.1 had exhibited them under pressure from Apel, are not to be overcome: a resolution reached by weakening any commitment would defend the programme by abandoning it. If the commitments are to be retained, the tensions must be sustained, and the discipline of sustaining them — each objection met not by refutation but by a bounded concession whose boundary then determines the tension’s shape — is the office this section assigns to philosophical self-reflexivity, argument turned back upon the conditions of its own possibility. Two properties of different orders meet in that phrase: the self-referentiality of argumentation, a property of the object, since every argument instantiates the conditions under which arguments are made, and the self-reflexivity of philosophy, the consequence at the level of the discipline, since an enquiry whose object is the conditions of argument, and whose instrument is argument, has no exterior from which to specify what it studies. The warrant is Habermas’s own diagnosis of what the tradition had to surrender, which §2.1 expounds, and the consequence drawn from it here, not by him, is that the reversal of the gaze cannot be executed from outside: a discipline which has renounced the objectivating stance towards its own conditions cannot reoccupy that stance to describe its renunciation without withdrawing the renunciation, and a philosophy to which the lifeworld is present only as horizon has no vantage upon the horizon, only its position within it. The discipline’s turn upon its own conditions must therefore itself be conducted from the participant’s perspective, and this is a constraint upon the argument conducted here and not merely a thesis it reports: a self-reflexivity which borrowed an observer’s standpoint would reoccupy, in its method, the position its own premise forecloses.

*The fact of reason.* The first resource is the *Faktum der Vernunft*, the Kantian term Habermas borrows analogically for a compulsion given and not derived. It is put to that use here, sustaining irrefutability at the operative register; the term stands in his text concessively and by analogy, and the use is the chapter’s. The commitment §2.2.3 records is this: whoever argues that no presupposition of argumentation is beyond denial defeats his own performance in the arguing, and the defeat occurs in the deed, whatever any theory has said of what he denies (C3), the licence for the result falling to Chapter Three. At MCCA 95 the demonstration is first delimited, in the sentence §2.1.2 displays; only then is the concession entered, concessively: ‘True, the participants must have accepted them as a “fact of reason” in setting out to argue.’ The denial follows adversatively: ‘But this kind of argument cannot accomplish a transcendental deduction in the Kantian sense.’[^124] The three moves are printed in that sequence, and the sequence carries the reading: the concession stands between the delimitation and the denial and identifies what the delimited demonstration has secured, the rules themselves, which the participants are said to have *accepted* as a fact of reason, acceptance being a predicate of an act and not of a proposition. The sentence distributes its predicates across the categorial boundary §2.2.2 states without remarking on the alignment. The restriction is stated again thirty-five pages later: the presuppositions ‘always already underlie the communicative practice of everyday life and that cannot be picked or chosen like makes of cars or value postulates’, and the fact of reason itself, in the sentence Chapter One §1.3.3 displays, cannot be deductively *grounded* but can be *clarified*, the clarification consisting in treating argumentative speech as a derivative case of understanding-oriented action.[^125] What the resource secures is accordingly indexed to performance from the outset. Its office against the sceptic is one thing; its office against an argument about the conditions of a sign’s recognisability is another, and §2.3.2 states the difference and its cost.

*The confutative method.* The second resource is the confutative method itself, post-metaphysical philosophy’s characteristic *via negativa*, establishing results by exhibiting the self-defeat of denials rather than by deriving them from self-evident premises. The label is the chapter’s. Habermas’s own descriptions of the procedure are ‘this weak form of transcendental analysis’, in the sentence §2.1.4 quotes, a maieutic method for which the search for performative contradictions provides a guide, in the passage §2.1.2 displays, and, of Apel’s use of it, a turning of ‘this form of performative refutation of the skeptic into a mode of justification’; the reply of 2003 restates the adoption as following Apel’s nondeductive model, in the sentence §2.3.2 displays. The method is addressed to the arguer as arguer, and the sense in which it is *ad hominem* is the classical one Chapter Three §3.3.1 fixes, and not the one Habermas sets aside when he quotes Peters’s dismissal of arguments ‘depending upon private idiosyncrasies’; Apel’s separation of the merely incidental *tu quoque* from the reflexive *elenchos*, which the same section of Chapter Three reads, marks the boundary from his side.[^126] It meets Apel where §2.2.1 leaves him standing. Apel does not dispute that the presuppositions bind; what he disputes is the status of the binding, and he affirms the binding itself, on the charge Kettner reports in his name and on that warrant alone, more consistently than Habermas’s architecture allows. What his objection reaches, on the reconstruction offered here from Kettner’s exposition, is the reach the programme claims for the method’s results. The confutative exhibition operates within discourse: its verdicts fall upon performances of denial, and a performance of denial is available only where someone argues. The priority of communicative over strategic action, the ranking upon which the parasitism apparatus inherited at Chapter One §1.4.1 depends, is a thesis about language at large, and the openly strategic actor, using language without even a pretence of an orientation to reaching understanding, performs no denial within discourse upon which the exhibition could operate. The denier of the priority is another matter, since whoever denies it in argument argues; what his defeat secures, upon the categorial premise §2.2.2 states, is the operative result and not the proposition he denies. This, on Kettner’s exposition of Apel’s charge, is the point beyond which universal pragmatics on its own cannot reach: the derivative status of the strategic can be demonstrated, if at all, only from within argumentative discourse, by the strictly reflexive transcendental argument Habermas’s method declines.[^127] The refusal carries a cost. The priority thesis stands, on Apel’s own terms, undischarged by confutation alone, a debt this chapter enters rather than conceals, and one which recurs wherever the *Selbsteinholungsprinzip* requires a reconstruction to render its own possibility intelligible as an outcome of what it reconstructs.[^128]

Apel does not situate the boundary of that compulsion where Habermas situates it, and the difference concerns the placing of the floor — the widest field within which the compulsion holds — and not the kind of boundary each party draws, which §2.2.2 fixes as categorial. He holds the presupposition of an unlimited communication community to be undertaken not in argumentation alone but ‘in any argument, indeed in any human word’, and, in his own qualification, ‘with any action that is to be intelligible’.[^129] On that account the openly strategic actor exits argumentation without exiting the field, and the debt just entered would be discharged by the wider scope rather than owed. Where Habermas places the floor is reported at §2.3.3 from MCCA 99–101 and displayed at Chapter Four §4.5.1; which of the two boundaries holds is a question Chapter Three states at §3.3.6 and declares open there. The reply entered at §2.1.5 against the iterability argument does not depend upon the debt: it turns upon the parasitism thesis read diagnostically, as Chapter One §1.4.1 bounds it, a dependence established case by case, whereas what Apel’s objection leaves undischarged is the general priority of the communicative over the strategic, which the diagnostic reading neither asserts nor needs. Nor is the debt one-sided, since Apel discharges the office by a reflection whose availability is the matter in dispute between the programmes (§2.2.3); a debt which one party cannot discharge and the other discharges by a means the first does not accept as discharging it marks a disagreement about what discharge would be.

*The genealogical resource.* The third resource answers the narrower objection Habermas puts against his own programme, stated at §2.1.5 from the same page: a specification of rationality can be no more than a proposal for a definition or a description of empirically widespread discursive behaviour, and neither warrants the reflexive self-understanding of a way of thinking as a whole.[^130] It answers it not by referring validity to genesis, which its own proviso forbids, but by referring the rational acceptability of a formation of convictions to learning processes which can be rationally retraced, in his phrase, and which, on the reading taken here, participants could themselves acknowledge as learning. The proviso is entered in Habermas’s own voice: a genealogical consideration of learning processes would be a contradiction in itself, he writes, were it not framed so that the independence of the validity of statements from their genesis can be preserved at the logical-semantic level.[^131] What the resource vindicates is therefore a practice’s acquisition and not its validity, the proviso withholding the passage from genesis to validity rather than stating the limitation in those terms; the limitation is legible in the shape of the move itself, which answers a genealogical question with a solution no longer genealogical in the sense that posed it. The taxonomy under which the move is made is threefold — subversive, problematising, vindicatory — and only the first and third bear upon the charge at issue; that the author to whom Habermas credits the taxonomy advances it against the genealogy he undertakes, and not as an instrument offered to it, is recorded in the note.[^132]

### §2.3.2 · The constancy requirement, and the concession it forces

The general charge must first be given the form in which it reaches the chapter, since the head of §2.1 fixed two senses of logocentrism and undertook to show what they share. In Derrida’s reading of Husserl the logocentric gesture is a confinement: the theory of meaning recognises ‘an initial limitation of sense to knowledge, of logos to objectivity, of language to reason’, so that the non-discursive forms of signification are refused the standing of sense, their common matrix being ‘being as presence’.[^133] Habermas’s diagnosis has the same shape and the opposite valuation. In the lectures of 1985 the critique of subject-centred reason becomes determinate only ‘as a critique of Western “logocentrism,” which is diagnosed not as an excess but as a deficit of rationality’, the deficit being the confinement of reason to one of its dimensions, in the sentence of 1988 the head of §2.1 quotes; and the topology of an inside and an outside of reason, on which a mystical other of reason would stand beyond discourse, is dismantled in the same lecture, since ‘Only a reason to which we ascribe a “power of the keys” could either include or exclude’, so that ‘the other of reason remains the mirror image of reason in power’.[^134] The two charges therefore converge in form, each convicting a confinement of reason or of sense to one dimension, and they part on what the unconfined whole is: for Habermas a spectrum of validity dimensions differentiated in the expert cultures and re-integrated in everyday practice, for Derrida a field in which no dimension governs the scene. The question the convergence puts to discourse theory is accordingly not whether an external dimension stands over against discourse, which is the topology Habermas rejects, but whether a discourse differentiated by validity dimension can speak of what is not a validity dimension without speaking for it. Habermas’s architecture already withholds from discourse the office of an overarching dimension. Strict discourses are ‘tailored to a single validity dimension’, truth or normative rightness, and ‘The polar tension between world-disclosure and problem-solving is held together within the functional matrix of ordinary language’ and not within any discourse; in 1981 arguments which justify standards of value ‘do not satisfy the conditions of discourse’, since ‘Cultural values do not appear with a claim to universality, as do norms of action’, and the validity claim attached to them does not, in the sentence Chapter One §1.3.1 displays, transcend local boundaries as claims to truth and rightness do; the unity of a reason which has split into its moments is sought in everyday life, ‘a more promising medium for regaining the lost unity of reason than are today’s expert cultures or yesteryear’s classical philosophy of reason’; and of religious and aesthetic experience the later work holds that philosophy at best ‘circumscribes the opaque core’ of the one, which ‘remains as profoundly alien to discursive thought as the hermetic core’ of the other, discursive practice operating ‘within a horizon of impenetrable and opaque experiences which can be represented iconically but cannot be completely grasped and explained in discursive terms’.[^135] The extension of the word *discourse* itself moves across the phases, from the denial of 1981 that evaluative argument satisfies its conditions to a discourse specialised for questions of taste in 1985 and an ethical-existential discourse in 1988, and no one statement is voiced here as constant. On these texts the reach of discourse is graded: universal for truth and rightness, bound to a form of life for evaluative and ethical-existential claims, a circumscription only for the religious and the aesthetic core, and, for the media-steered coordination of systems, which is ‘largely disconnected from norms and values’, a reach available to the observer’s description and not to the participant’s discourse, the shift of perspective being marked where it occurs.[^136] What discourse can speak of without speaking for is therefore stated by Habermas himself, dimension by dimension, and a logocentrism charge which took discourse for a claim to comprehend every dimension would strike a position he does not hold. What the charge reaches is the point at which the differentiated architecture must be held together, and there Habermas’s own answer is the one this chapter costs: the idealising presuppositions which hold it together ‘are not arbitrary, logocentric acts brought to bear by theoreticians on unmanageable contexts in order to give the illusion of mastery; rather, they are presuppositions that the participants themselves have to make if communicative action is to be at all possible’, and among them stands the supposition of identical meanings, of which he concedes that it ‘is unavoidable but often counterfactual’.[^137] The participant-indexing of that answer is what the thin reading below keeps; the counterfactuality is what Derrida’s argument presses, and it presses it at the requirement of constancy the exhibition makes on its own behalf.

Derrida’s charge, taken at the point where it bears upon the requirement the exhibition makes on its own behalf, is not the general charge against presence which §2.1.5 states and §2.2.2 answers. It is a specific objection to a specific requirement: that participants in an exchange use expressions with constant meaning. The requirement can be found in the catalogue of rules of argumentation Habermas adopts, at the logical-semantic level, as rule (1.3): ‘Different speakers may not use the same expression with different meanings.’ It cannot be staged upon that rule, and the reason is on the same page. Habermas takes the catalogue over from Alexy ‘for simplicity’, and in the sentences immediately following the rule he sets the whole level aside: the presuppositions of argumentation at this level ‘are logical and semantic rules that have no ethical content. They are not a suitable point of departure for a transcendental-pragmatic argument.’[^138] A concession extracted from rule (1.3) would therefore be extracted from a requirement the programme has already declined to build upon. What the objection reaches is not the catalogue but the exhibition. For a performative self-contradiction to be exhibited at all, the denial a speaker performs must be recognisable, by him and by his interlocutor, as the denial of the presupposition his denying enacts. That recognisability is a requirement of constancy which the *Aufweis* makes on its own behalf, whatever the catalogue says, and which Chapter Three §3.2.2 states for the exhibition in its own terms. How much constancy it requires is not settled by stating it: an identity of content holding across every context of use would answer to it, and so would the weaker condition that a denier and his interlocutor take the expression of the denial for the expression his performance presupposes. It is that requirement, and the question of its strength, which Derrida’s argument tests.

Against it Derrida’s argument runs as follows. A mark is what it is only in being repeatable: a written communication ‘must be repeatable — iterable — in the absolute absence of the addressee or of the empirically determinable set of addressees’, and a mark ‘is constituted, in its identity as a mark, by its iterability in the absence of whoever, and therefore ultimately in the absence of every empirically determinable “subject.”’ The argument is generalised from writing to every element of spoken language: the unity of a signifying form ‘is constituted only by its iterability, by the possibility of being repeated in the absence not only of its referent, which goes without saying, but of a determined signified or current intention of signification’; ‘this iterability of the mark is not an accident or an anomaly, but is that (normal/abnormal) without which a mark could no longer even have a so-called “normal” functioning’; and ‘given this structure of iteration, the intention which animates utterance will never be completely present in itself and its content’.[^139] Derrida’s target is Austin’s exclusion of the non-serious, and the sentences he quotes from Austin set aside uses of language which are ‘not seriously’ meant but are used ‘in ways parasitic upon its normal use — ways which fall under the doctrine of the etiolations of language’, and Derrida’s complaint is that what is thereby excluded is what Austin ‘nevertheless recognizes as the possibility to which every utterance is open’. The conclusion is drawn on the side of success and not of failure: a performative could not succeed ‘if its formulation did not repeat a “coded” or iterable statement’, so that ‘a successful performative is necessarily an “impure” performative’, and the ‘relative purity’ Austin allows ‘is not constructed against citationality or iterability, but against other kinds of iteration within a general iterability’. In place of the exclusion he proposes ‘a differential typology of forms of iteration’, within which ‘the category of intention will not disappear; it will have its place, but from this place it will no longer be able to govern the entire scene and the entire system of utterances’.[^140] The essay closes with a disclaimer which is relied upon below: Derrida will not conclude ‘that there is no relative specificity of the effects of consciousness’, nor that ‘there is no effect of the performative, no effect of ordinary language, no effect of presence and of speech acts’; these effects ‘do not exclude what is generally opposed to them term by term, but on the contrary presuppose it … as the general space of their possibility’.[^141]

Habermas contested this argument in print, and the contest must be entered before anything is conceded. In the excursus on the levelling of the genre distinction between philosophy and literature he replies that Derrida ‘obviously already presupposes what he wants to prove: that any convention which permits the repetition of exemplary actions possesses from the outset not only a symbolic, but also a fictional character’. ‘Only the actually performed speech act is effective as action; the promise mentioned or reported in a quote depends grammatically upon this’; Derrida ‘makes no attempt to “deconstruct” this distinctive functional mode of ordinary speech within communicative action’; and the illocutionary binding force Austin discovered places normal speech ‘under constraints different from those of fictional discourse, simulation, and interior monologue’, constraints which ‘define the domain of “normal” language’. The burden he lays upon Derrida is stated in one sentence: ‘it must first be shown that the conventions of a game are ultimately indistinguishable from norms of action’.[^142] The reply is considered and is not empty, though the note records what it engages and what it does not; it does not restore what the objection removes. What it defends is the asymmetry of the ordinary and the parasitic, the priority thesis, whose separate cost §2.3.1 has entered against Apel; what the objection targets is identity of meaning across contexts, and a defence of differential constraints upon use does not deliver identity of content across uses. Habermas’s wording is, besides, weaker than the position he attacks in the form Culler gives it would require: ordinary speech stands under constraints ‘different from’ those of fiction, and the claim that they differ in kind is carried not by that sentence but by the burden the same passage lays upon Derrida, which is where it must be met. The two exchanges pass each other, since nothing in Habermas’s reply claims the context-invariance a thick constancy requirement would need.

The concession this forces is prepared by a sentence Habermas writes against Apel, in the reply of 2003, and is not made by him. In that reply he records that in deriving the universal validity of (U) he follows Apel’s nondeductive model of justification by the uncovering of a sceptic’s performative contradictions, and locates the disagreement in one sentence: ‘The point of contention between Apel and me is not this justification move but its status in a nonfoundational game of justification.’ Nine pages later the status is fixed. The transcendental-pragmatic argument, to which Apel accords the role of an ultimate justification, ‘has in reality the status of a presumptively infallible, and at any rate discursively nontestable, reassurance. If it were an argument, it would be located in a linguistic context that offers as many opportunities for criticism as it has facets.’[^143] What Habermas denies the transcendental-pragmatic argument, so long as it is accorded Apel’s role, is the standing of an argument at all: what remains is a reassurance, presumptively infallible and discursively untestable, and no such reassurance was claimed for the exhibition in these pages. The conditional which follows states what the thin reading below keeps, that were it an argument it would stand in a linguistic context and open to criticism at every facet. The sentence is directed at Apel and says nothing of Derrida, and the step from it to what Derrida’s argument needs, that an exhibition which is an argument is conducted in iterable marks and nowhere else, is taken here and not by him.

The categorial premise of §2.2.2 cannot then be defended unchanged, and does not need to be abandoned; it needs the downgrade §2.1.4 recorded at the modal register, from an unconditional to an indexed necessity, applied now to meaning itself. What the *Aufweis* requires from constancy of meaning is thin: that a denial and the affirmation it contradicts be recognisable, within a given exchange, as instances of the same citational type, sufficient for the incoherence of denying-while-affirming to register between these interlocutors, here. The requirement is participant-indexed, and the index is its content. A prohibition upon different speakers using the same expression with different meanings, read at face value, is thicker: a demand for a stable identity of meaning across speakers, which is what iterability puts out of reach, the identity it constitutes being that of the mark and not of a signified stable across its uses. The deployments of the instrument in this chapter — the analysis of denial at §2.2.2, the confinement of Stroud’s objection at §2.1.4, the reply to the fallibility objection at §2.1.2 — draw upon no more than the thin requirement. Nor is the thin requirement one Derrida’s argument excludes: a recognisability sufficient for incoherence to register is one of the ‘forms of iteration’ which the differential typology he proposes would have to sort, a typology whose tenability as an exhaustive programme he expressly holds off from affirming, and the place he assigns to intention within it, from which intention no longer governs the entire scene, is a place the thin reading can leave it, since the thin reading asks of intention nothing the typology denies it.

The individuation of the *Dass* is thus amended, and the premise stands: the *Dass* is individuated no more finely than by the citational recognisability sufficient for incoherence to be exhibited, and not by a self-identical meaning surviving the exchange intact. What §2.2.2 fixed — that the *Dass* is individuated by what its denial, performed, would undo — is unaltered; the amendment fixes the reach of the undoing, which is the exchange in which the denial is performed. The cost is that a thin, citational *Dass* is individuated only relative to a given exchange, and nothing guarantees its stability beyond it; the interval between what is operatively secured and what any reconstruction can state opens earlier than the thick reading suggested, though it does not close. The two intervals §2.2.3 reaches are reached with the amended premise and not the thick one: the first premise of that account is the programme’s commitment to an irrefutability Habermas himself indexes, and an individuation relative to an exchange is a further index of the same kind. What the amendment alters is the width of the interval and not its existence, and a wider deficit is not a repaired one. What is owed, and handed to Chapter Three §3.2, is the passage from it to an irrefutability which no exchange bounds.

One consequence of the amendment strengthens an objection framed here in the terms of Wellmer’s critique of the idealisations, and not attributed to him in this form: that the presuppositions of argumentation are counterfactual idealisations which participants project, and not conditions a performance enacts. On that objection a *Dass* individuated thinly is harder to distinguish from a projected idealisation than a thickly individuated one was. The distinction nevertheless holds, by the criterion §2.2.2 fixed: the *Dass* is individuated by what its performed denial would undo. An idealisation can be given up, and giving it up is within a participant’s power; it cannot be undone by a performance, since nothing a participant does within the exchange bears upon whether the projection was made. A condition enacted in the performance is not given up but attempted against, and the attempt undoes itself. Thinning the individuation alters how widely the undoing reaches, within this exchange rather than across all exchanges, and alters nothing about whether there is an undoing to be had; what the amendment surrenders is scope, and not standing. A sentence of Habermas’s own stands against the Wellmerian reading besides: what the participants ‘must have accepted’ as a fact of reason is, in the sentence §2.3.1 read, the predicate of an act and not of a projection.

Derrida himself later supplies the nearest counterpart to the illocutionary bond, in the ‘irreducible “faith”’ of a ‘“sworn faith”’ which he writes in 1996 is ‘a performative of promising at work even in lying or perjury and without which no address to the other would be possible’; the thin *Dass* stands nearer to that pledge than the thick reading stood, being likewise individuated within an exchange and guaranteed nowhere beyond it, and what separates them, that the pledge is held answerable in these chapters to the practice of giving and asking for reasons and by Derrida to nothing that practice could supply, is recorded in the note and recurs at §2.3.3 as the difference between an exhibition and a founding.[^144]

The question the concession relocates is accordingly sharper and not weaker: whether exchange-relative recognisability can carry irrefutability *simpliciter*, or only irrefutability-within-this-exchange. That question belongs to Chapter Three, which receives it at §3.2.2 and makes the passage at §3.3.2, by the retorsion — the turning of a denial upon its own performance — and within the limit declared there; it arrives sharpened by a concession made here rather than as an unexamined debt deferred.

### §2.3.3 · Internal critique, its outside, and the decisionist challenge

A further thesis stands upon the second resource and upon the record of the architecture’s amendments: that standing critiques of the architecture are *internal* to the self-reflexive practice, amendments within it and not defeaters of it. The thesis has two components, differently secured, and the charge that it converts the programme into an unfalsifiable dogma is answered by keeping them apart. The categorial component, that a critique advanced in discourse enacts the conditions it disputes, is secured performatively, and no case history could defeat it. The historical component, that the architecture in fact answers such critiques by amendment rather than by relabelling, is falsifiable in the ordinary way, since a single sustained objection met by relabelling would defeat it; it is carried by one instance, the retraction of the consensus-theoretic conception of truth, itself a documented fact, read as an amendment under pressure from named critics, and the reading is an interpretive construal.[^145] A charge of unfalsifiability pressed against the instrument rather than the thesis is met on firmer ground: §2.2.2 states two determinate conditions under which the categorial premise would be defeated, both checkable by anyone, and the instrument, turned upon the premise of the argument deploying it at §2.3.2, returned the verdict that the premise could not be defended unchanged and cost the *Dass* its thick individuation, which no re-description does. The programme’s own self-understanding supports the distinction, in the sentence §2.1.2 displays in which Habermas withholds from ‘our reconstruction’ of pretheoretical knowledge the non-fallibility he grants to the intuitive knowledge of rules,[^146] which is the operative–propositional asymmetry so far as certainty is concerned, and in his own voice to that extent. The thesis has, lastly, a determinate outside, named rather than concealed: the decisionist’s refusal to argue, which is not an objection raised in discourse and falls under neither component.

The tension takes a further form: not an objection which would ground communicative reason in something further, but a challenge whose content assigns the standing of any ground to a decision, and Apel’s own texts supply its shape. Fundamental evidence, on the position he reports and rejects, ‘can neither be denied without self-contradiction nor be grounded without *petitio principii*. Therefore, it is said, the *fundamental-grounding must be superseded by a fundamental-decision*’ — a solution he ascribes to Stegmüller and to Popper, and which ‘clearly corresponds again to the purely logically deduced “Münchhausen-Trilemma” of fundamental-grounding in the sense of Hans Albert’.[^147] Against a challenge of this shape no demonstration is available, and the unavailability is principled, since a demonstration would presuppose, as the court before which its own case is tried, the practice whose title is disputed. What can be exhibited is the challenge’s own performance: raised as a claim addressed to interlocutors, it enacts in the address the conditions of claim-raising its content denies. Reciprocity of address is all that is claimed for the enactment here, in the measure Chapter Three §3.2.1 allows the exhibition, and nothing of the symmetry or equal entitlement that chapter withholds from it; this secures a compulsion and no refutation, and forecloses the challenge only as something arguable. The decisionist position licenses a reply which changes the dispute’s shape: not to deny that arguing enacts the presuppositions, but to exit the practice, refusing to argue and demonstrating in conduct what could not be asserted in speech.

At this point the parties divide, and not where a summary would place the division. Habermas concedes the exit at the level of argumentation and without reservation: the consistent sceptic who will not enter into argumentation leaves the transcendental-pragmatic argument without purchase and voluntarily quits the community of those who argue; the concession is at once bounded, since the sceptic remains bound, in the everyday communicative practice he cannot leave, to presuppositions he has not shaken off. Apel denies the exit outright and at a different level: the defence of irrationalism refutes, by the performative act which accompanies it, the attempt to refuse argumentation, and even one who decides to leave presupposes, in taking his leaving for a decision, the principle he has denied. Both passages are displayed, and the dropout examined as the figure in which the question reaches the legal-political architecture, at Chapter Four §4.5.1. The parties therefore do not divide over whether an exit exists but over where the inescapable floor lies, at argumentation as such or at the everyday practice of communication beneath it: Habermas lets the sceptic leave the community of arguers and holds him within the lifeworld, and Apel holds that leaving is itself an act which lays claim to sense and so re-enters what it would quit. The division is one of scope in the placing of the floor, and not the categorial difference C3 locates in the kind of item each party exempts; and the two axes cross.[^148]

Before that stronger form is heard, a challenge which stands between the decisionist’s exit and Derrida’s founding must be entered, since it is the form in which the logocentrism charge of §2.3.2 returns against discourse itself, and it is the inversion of a charge Habermas made his own. Adorno’s charge, as Habermas reports it, is that ‘reason itself is suspected of the baneful confusion of power and validity claims, but still with the intent of enlightening’, instrumental reason having abolished, on his account, the difference between what claims validity and what serves self-preservation; the change of paradigm to a communicative reason differentiated by validity dimension is Habermas’s answer to that charge, and the answer is what the inversion takes for its target.[^149] In his own report of Foucault, ‘Truth is an insidious mechanism of exclusion, because it only functions on condition that the will to truth prevalent within it remains hidden’, and power ‘conceals itself in discourse as the will to truth’; Foucault states the reciprocity in his own words, ‘We are subjected to the production of truth through power and we cannot exercise power except through the production of truth’, and describes the task of the critique of reason since Weber as ‘isolating the form of rationality presented as dominant, and endowed with the status of the one-and-only reason, in order to show that it is only one possible form among others’.[^150] Turned upon discourse theory, the charge runs that the multidimensional communicative reason which answers Adorno is itself the new one-and-only reason: a differentiated architecture which ‘tells them to sort their questions into three types and to exchange reasons in accord with the three forms of argumentation’, a communicative rationality which is ‘a prime object for a genealogical inquiry that is motivated by the search for the power effects of that which is presented as power-free’, and a lifeworld which assumes at the conceptual level ‘the existence of power-free spheres of communication’.[^151] The peril the inversion names is not that discourse excludes an outside, which is the topology §2.3.2 found Habermas rejecting, but that the presuppositions which hold the architecture together, the ones he calls the participants’ own and not the theorist’s, are themselves the sedimented form of an asymmetry, so that what he calls from the Gauss Lectures onwards ‘der eigentümlich zwanglose Zwang des besseren Arguments’, the peculiarly unforced force of the better argument, would be the form power takes when it has become invisible to those it moves.[^152] Habermas’s reply to Foucault is framed as the aporias of a self-referential genealogy — that Foucault ‘gets entangled in aporias as soon as he is supposed to explain what the genealogical historiographer himself does’, and that genealogy emerges as ‘the presentistic, relativistic, cryptonormative illusory science that it does not want to be’ — and it is not framed in the lectures on Foucault as a performative contradiction, the phrase being the translator’s in his introduction and not Habermas’s there.[^153] The reply is bounded in these chapters by a rule Chapter Four §4.5.2 states: an exhibition that a genealogy is incoherent in its articulation does not dispose of what the genealogy discloses, since the coherence of a claim and the content of its disclosure are different things. What can be said of the inversion here is accordingly limited to its form. Where the charge reaches the genesis of communicative structures it falls to the discipline of §2.1.3, which blocks the passage from provenance to title; where it reaches their constitution, so that the presuppositions themselves would be what power has made and not what participants must make, the question is the one §2.1.5 entered for the Conclusion, and it is not settled here. It is, in a further form, the legitimacy question named below: whether a discourse which has no exterior, and which holds its presuppositions to be the participants’ own, is entitled to certify that they are not also something else.

The challenge has a form stronger than either party’s statement of it, and Derrida supplies it where he asks what founds the authority of law. The founding and justifying moment which institutes law implies, he writes, ‘a performative force, which is always an interpretative force’; the operation which amounts to founding law ‘would consist of a coup de force, of a performative and therefore interpretative violence that in itself is neither just nor unjust and that no justice and no previous law with its founding anterior moment could guarantee or contradict or invalidate’. The challenge is then turned upon the exhibition itself: ‘No justificatory discourse could or should insure the role of metalanguage in relation to the performativity of institutive language or to its dominant interpretation.’ Since the origin of authority ‘can’t by definition rest on anything but themselves’, the founding acts ‘are themselves a violence without ground’, ‘neither legal nor illegal in their founding moment’, and they ‘exceed the opposition between founded and unfounded, or between any foundationalism or anti-foundationalism’.[^154] This is the decisionist’s reply raised to a structural claim: not that a sceptic may leave the practice, but that the practice itself rests upon an act which no discourse conducted within it can adjudicate, and that the same limit ‘will reappear at the supposed origin’ of whatever conditions or conventions the practice invokes.

The reply available is bounded, and its boundary is the one already drawn. What Derrida’s sentence denies to any justificatory discourse, the office of a metalanguage over the performativity which institutes a practice, is nothing the exhibition claims: the exhibition grounds nothing, displays within an exchange that a denial performed there defeats itself, and forecloses the exit only as something arguable, which is what this section conceded before Derrida was heard. Where his argument reaches past that concession is at the founding of the practice, and there no verdict is entered: a verdict upon the founding would have to be returned from the standpoint §2.3.1 forecloses, and one returned from within would be a justificatory discourse of the kind his sentence excludes. That the founding cannot be certified from within does not show that there is nothing to certify, a distinction the Introduction draws and this chapter observes. What Derrida calls the mystical limit is accordingly neither refuted nor accepted; it is the place at which the legitimacy question is put in its strongest form, whether a discourse without exterior is entitled to declare an exit foreclosed, and the question is handed on in that form. One difference is marked, since it governs the terminus of this chapter: for Derrida the limit is a limit of justice, ‘an experience of the impossible’, at which ‘the decision between just and unjust is never insured by a rule’; in these chapters the limit is a limit of the practice of grounding, at which that practice is oriented towards a validity it cannot discharge, and not a warrant owed within it, a difference §2.3.4 draws. The two are related and are not one, and §2.3.4 draws the distinction under its own name.[^155]

Whether the floor holds where Apel places it or where Habermas does governs where the deficit named at §2.2.3 has its practical face: if the exit is available, between the compulsion binding the participant and the *Sollgeltung* the agent owes; if it is not, between a compulsion reaching the agent as such and any statement of what binds him. Both outcomes locate one structural finding, that modal status does not pass between the registers, and neither refutes nor vindicates it. What the second interval adds, and Chapter Three §3.3.6 and Chapter Four §4.5.1 record, is that the deficit’s practical face rests upon Habermas’s own concession of the exit, and is therefore charged to his architecture rather than entered as a neutral datum so long as the second-stage question is open; were Apel’s floor to hold, the finding would be charged to no party’s architecture in particular, the compulsion then reaching the agent as such.

Adjudicating between them is the recurrence the resources of this chapter generate, and the recurrent item may now be named: *the legitimacy question*, whether philosophical discourse, confutative, self-referential and without grounding external to itself, is entitled to the verdicts it returns upon its own conditions. It has arisen at five points in this chapter, in the definition-or-description dilemma of §2.1.5, in the framing objection to the yield-question at §2.2.1, in the third of the questions §2.1.4 puts to the displacement of the solitary subject, in the inversion of Adorno’s charge entered above, and at the boundary of internal critique here, taking here its sharpest form, whether such a discourse may declare an exit foreclosed. Its adjudication is the Apel–Habermas dispute over the presuppositions’ irrefutability; it opens Chapter Three with the tensions of §2.2.3 in hand, is taken up there at §3.3.6 as the second-stage question, whether the norms of reciprocity are among the conditions of determinate signification and with whom argument must therefore be had, and is left open there in a stated form; its limb concerning the agent as such is drawn at Chapter Four §4.3.1.

The corrigibility Habermas concedes attaches to the articulation and is redeemed empirically, which is what the objection exploits, since an empirical falsifier can be deflected by redescribing the recalcitrant speaker; the elenctic condition cannot be so deflected, since where a speaker rejects a reconstructed rule the burden falls upon the theorist to demonstrate that the speaker relies upon that rule in formulating the rejection, and where the demonstration cannot be made the hypothesis is not saved by a diagnosis of incompetence but refuted in the generality it claimed, and narrows to the cases in which the demonstration can be made. The check upon the theory is thus not the theorist’s charity but the structure of the speech act, which does not admit of arbitrary imposition. The falsifiability of a second kind, and the burden it places upon the theorist, are not drawn from Habermas.

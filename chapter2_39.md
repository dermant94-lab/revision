# CHAPTER TWO

## Reconstructive Science and Post-Metaphysical Thinking: The Three Commitments, Their Tensions, and the Aporia of Truth.

## §2.1 · Post-Metaphysical Thinking, Reconstructive Science, and the Three Commitments

What Habermas calls post-metaphysical thinking is presented by him as a situation before it is a doctrine, and the situation is dated. In the essay which opens *Postmetaphysical Thinking*, ‘The Horizon of Modernity Is Shifting’, the break with the tradition is characterised by four themes: ‘Four themes characterize the break with the tradition. The headings are: postmetaphysical thinking, the linguistic turn, situating reason, and reversing the primacy of theory over practice — or the overcoming of logocentrism.’ The first names the decline of ‘the tradition’s strong concept of theory, its grasp of the totality, and its claim to a privileged access to truth’; the second is the passage from the philosophy of consciousness to that of language, in which world-constituting accomplishments ‘are transferred from transcendental subjectivity to grammatical structures’ and ‘the reconstructive work of the linguist replaces a kind of introspection that cannot be readily checked on’; the third is the situating of reason in ‘historical embodiments’, in action, language and the body; the fourth is ‘the reversal of the classical relationship of theory to practice’, credited ultimately to ‘the honing of a Marxian idea’ and found corroborated by pragmatism, developmental psychology and the analysis of the lifeworld.[^1] The four are not offered as a system but as the motive forces of twentieth-century philosophising ‘in spite of the boundaries between schools’.

The fourth theme carries a word which must be fixed at its first use, since it is used in another sense later in this chapter. The logocentrism Habermas would overcome is a fixation of reason upon one of its dimensions: philosophy ‘has pinned reason down to only one of its dimensions, at first ontologically, later epistemologically, and then even in linguistic analysis’, to the fact-stating discourse ‘that specializes in only one dimension of speech, the truth of assertoric sentences’, so that questions of justice, of taste and of truthful self-presentation are ‘excluded from the sphere of the rational’, and ‘Contextualism is only the flipside of logocentrism’. The release is a change of position and not a further theory: philosophy ‘reverses this perspective and looks back upon the thicket of the lifeworld’, where it finds ‘a reason that is already operating in everyday communicative practice’.[^2] Derrida’s word is the same and his sense is not: for him logocentrism names ‘the metaphysics of phonetic writing’, the privilege of presence and of the voice, and it is not set aside by a turn from theory to practice, since on his account practice too proceeds by iterable marks. The two senses are held apart throughout; that the two charges nonetheless share a form, each convicting a confinement of reason or of sense to one of its dimensions, is shown at §2.3.2, where the argument from iterability on which the second rests is engaged on its own terms.[^3]

The metaphysics the situation is said to follow must be stated in Habermas’s own terms before the chapter regroups it, since the regrouping is a reading and the terms are not. In ‘Themes in Postmetaphysical Thinking’ the word *metaphysical* designates, ‘In a rough simplification that neglects the Aristotelian line’, the philosophical idealism which runs from Plato to Hegel, and three aspects of it are singled out: ‘identity thinking, the doctrine of Ideas, and the strong concept of theory’. The first relates the many to a one which is ‘both axiom and essential ground, principle and origin’; the second makes the order underlying appearances itself conceptual, so that being acquires ‘the further attributes of universality, necessity, and supratemporality’; the third embeds theory in an exemplary form of life, the *bios theoretikos*, which ‘demands a renunciation of the natural attitude toward the world and promises contact with the extra-ordinary’. The philosophy of consciousness is not a fourth aspect beside these but the modern refraction of the three, in which the ideal essences become ‘the categorial determinations of a productive reason’ and reason ‘is active as a simultaneously totalizing and self-referential reflection’; its terminus is a theory which wants ‘to satisfy the demand for justifying all premises from within itself’ and so sublimates the independence of the theoretical life ‘into a theory that is absolute and self-justifying’.[^4] Four developments, ‘socially conditioned’ in the last analysis, are said to have problematised this thinking from without: procedural rationality, which shattered philosophy’s cognitive privilege; the historical consciousness which set a detranscendentalisation of the inherited concepts in motion; the criticism of reification which carried the paradigm shift to language; and the mutual dependence of theory and practice which undid the classical precedence of the former.[^5] They are the four themes of the opening essay in another order, and the correspondence is what licenses reading the two essays as one account. The passage from the constituting subject to post-metaphysical thinking is accordingly bound to the overcoming of three commitments held together, the identity of thought and being surveyed from a standpoint above the world, the idealism which makes the order of things conceptual, and the precedence of a theoretical life which assigns the other disciplines their place; what displaces them is examined below as one operation with three faces, and the operation is named once the tenets have been stated.

What the break renounces is stated by Habermas in three respects, and the three are the measure of everything the chapter will call post-metaphysical. In the essay addressed to Henrich, philosophy after Kant is said to operate ‘under conditions of rationality that it has not chosen’: it can reclaim no access to essential insights privileged over science, morality or art and ‘now disposes only over knowledge that is fallible’; it must forgo the doctrinal form which intervenes in socialisation and ‘must remain theoretical’; and it can no longer rank the totalities of lifeworlds, which present themselves only plurally, but ‘is limited to grasping universal structures of lifeworlds in general’. ‘These are three respects in which there can no longer be, after Kant, a metaphysics in the sense of “conclusive” and “integrating” thought.’[^6] The renunciation is not a proscription of the questions, and the preposition carries the point, since *nach* says both *after* and *according to*: the thinking is conducted after metaphysics and by the measure metaphysics failed to meet.[^7] Nor is it the anti-metaphysical reflex Habermas separates from it at the opening of the essay on its themes, where positivism’s furore against metaphysics is said to betray a scientistic motive of its own; a philosophy which has taken its place among the sciences ‘need not by any means completely surrender the relationship to the whole that had distinguished metaphysics’, since the lifeworld is present to it as a totality which is ‘unproblematized, nonobjectified, and pretheoretical’.[^8] What is retained of metaphysics is named by Habermas as a residue and its status is fixed: the moment of unconditionality preserved in fallibilist concepts of truth and morality ‘is not an absolute, or it is at most an absolute that has become fluid as a critical procedure’, and ‘Only with this residue of metaphysics can we do battle against the transfiguration of the world through metaphysical truths’.[^9] The residue is a procedure and not a content; that it is a procedure is what §2.3.4 will find to be the source of the aporia.

The Introduction described the position post-metaphysical thinking vacates in three forms, a principle, a faculty and a standpoint, and the chapter regroups Habermas’s renunciations under three tenets of the philosophy of consciousness whose negatives its three commitments are; the regrouping is entered as a reading, and each of its members is drawn from his text while the enumeration is not. The first tenet is methodological solipsism: the position of a subject which constitutes its self-understanding without recourse to anyone and, in the words of the essay of 1981, ‘confronts objects and becomes reflective only by turning itself into an object’. The Introduction fixed the term as Apel’s, and as a thesis about the theorist’s position and not about other minds; Habermas uses it in his own voice, of the ‘monological action’ an exit from discourse would require and of the ‘solitary observer’ of a radical-interpretation scenario, and its displacement by the rule-following argument is examined at §2.1.4, where the second commitment is entered; what the tenet holds about meaning, which the phrase does not itself say, is stated in the paragraph which follows.[^10] The second is the extramundane standpoint, which Habermas calls, in the essay on the unity of reason, ‘the extramundane perspective of a God’s-eye view’ and finds surviving in negative metaphysics as ‘the perspective of the radical outsider’; in the essay on Putnam the same phrase names what metaphysical realism ‘chases’, ‘a fictitious view from nowhere, an external God’s-eye point of view on an uninterpreted world’; and the volume of 2012 states the whole thrust of post-metaphysical thinking as the deconstruction of ‘the divine standpoint which by definition has the last word’, whose place is taken by ‘the impartiality of the practice of argumentation among participants to which there is no alternative’.[^11] The third is foundationalism, the theory which justifies its premises from within itself and secures, in the words of the essay of 1981, ‘a cognition before cognition’, a domain between philosophy and the sciences from which it can show the sciences their place; its incompatibility with the fallibilism of the sciences is stated at the point where ‘Every comprehensive, closed, and final system of statements must be formulated in a language that requires no commentary’.[^12] Each tenet is the negative of one commitment. Reconstructivism is what the linguistic turn becomes when its object is a competence exercised between speakers rather than a grammar read off from formations ‘as if from something lying before one’, and it displaces the first tenet because its datum is public; immanence is what the situating of reason becomes when the reason situated is the theorist’s own, so that no standpoint beyond the practices remains from which their conditions might be secured, and it forecloses the second; fallibilism is what the deflation of the strong concept of theory becomes when philosophy holds a place open for the reconstructive sciences instead of assigning them theirs, and it is the refusal of the third.[^13] The correlation is a reading of the method and not Habermas’s ordering; so far as it holds, the three commitments are not additions to post-metaphysical thinking but the form it takes in a method, which is why a deficit derived from their conjunction is a finding about post-metaphysical thinking as Habermas practises it, and not about one theory conducted under it.

The first tenet requires a statement the phrase does not supply, since what it names is commonly taken for the philosophy of consciousness as such, and it is narrower. Methodological solipsism is not the thesis that there is consciousness before there is language, nor a thesis about the existence of other minds; it is a thesis about where meaning is constituted, and it holds that meaning is constituted in the intentional acts of a subject before it is shared. Its most exact form is Husserl’s, and the *Ideas* of 1913 state it in three steps. All real unities are ‘unities of meaning’, and unities of meaning presuppose ‘a sense-giving consciousness, which, on its side, is absolute and not dependent in its turn on sense bestowed on it from another source’; sense is bestowed in the noetic phases of the intentional experience, by an ‘“animating”, meaning-bestowing stratum’ laid over the sensile data, the noema being the correlate of that bestowal; and expression, when it comes, adds nothing to what has been constituted, since the stratum of expression, ‘apart from the fact that it lends expression to all other intentionalities, is not productive’.[^14] On those three steps language is posterior to meaning: a public medium serves the transmission of a sense already had, and the question how a sense constituted in one consciousness becomes the same sense for another is posed afterwards and from within. The *Crisis* poses it under the title of a paradox, that of a human subjectivity which is at once a subject for the world and an object in it, a world which ‘takes its ontic meaning entirely from our intentional life’; and the work is not to be read as though Husserl had overlooked the difficulty, since it insists that the constituting accomplishment ‘is not that of the isolated subject’ and that ‘meaning is never anything but meaning in modes of validity’. What it retains is a priority of method. The epoché ‘creates a unique sort of philosophical solitude which is the fundamental methodical requirement for a truly radical philosophy’, and justice is to be done to the ego’s ‘central position in all constitution’.[^15] Methodological solipsism, so fixed, is the methodical priority of the constituting ego in the order in which meaning is constituted, and not a denial that meaning is intersubjectively valid; the adjective is Husserl’s own in Carr’s rendering, and the position is the one Apel’s phrase names and dates.[^16] Habermas’s diagnosis is directed at that priority and at nothing wider. In the Gauss Lectures of 1970–71 the concept of intentionality is said to have been won ‘am Modell des einsamen, in bedeutungsverleihenden Akten auf einen Gegenstand gerichteten Subjekts’, on the model of the solitary subject directed upon an object in meaning-conferring acts, and to stand in need of reformulation in the theory of language; the conclusion drawn is that ‘Identische Bedeutungen bilden sich nicht in der intentionalen Struktur eines einsam seiner Welt gegenüberstehenden Subjekts’, identical meanings do not form in the intentional structure of a subject standing alone over against its world, but only in the identical validity of an expression for different subjects.[^17] The essay of 1988 on the theory of meaning repeats the diagnosis in one sentence: ‘Husserl spoke in this context of meaning-conferring acts.’[^18] Apel’s formulation is the same and is sharper on the point at issue: the epoché of methodological solipsism was to secure ‘the irreducibility of the sphere of the pure noetic-intentional meaning-constitutive ego-consciousness and its noematic act-correlates’, and a philosopher so placed must claim ‘any possible meaning to be a virtual correlate of his intentional act’, a claim Apel calls legitimate and unavoidable for the philosopher and, in Husserl, blind to the public meanings through which alone the philosopher’s self-understanding is possible.[^19] What is displaced is therefore a thesis about the constitution of meaning, and the displacement is executed where meaning is constituted, in a rule the speaker follows with others and did not institute alone; the argument is Wittgenstein’s as Apel takes it, and it is examined at §2.1.4, where the second commitment is entered.

The displacement has a name in Habermas’s own vocabulary, and the name designates the core of the position rather than one of its results: detranscendentalisation, the operation by which the transcendental subject is replaced, its questions retained, by socialised subjects whose cognition is entwined with speech and action. The word enters his self-description in 1980, where the strategy followed since 1968 is said to have taken up ‘die universalistischen Fragestellungen der Transzendentalphilosophie, bei gleichzeitiger Detranszendentalisierung des Vorgehens und der Beweisziele’, the universalistic questions of transcendental philosophy under a simultaneous detranscendentalisation of the procedure and of the aims of proof; what is detranscendentalised is the procedure and the proof-goals, and what is retained is the questions.[^20] The definition is given in 2001: ‘Detranscendentalization leads, on the one hand, to the embedding of socialized subjects into the context of a lifeworld and, on the other hand, to the entwinement of cognition with speech and action.’[^21] The two halves of the sentence are the two faces under which the operation must be read. Its pragmatic face concerns the knowing subject. The theoretical ideas of reason ‘step out of the static “intelligible world” and unleash their dynamics within the lifeworld’, and where the transcendental subjectivity of consciousness stood there now stands ‘die detranszendentalisierte Intersubjektivität der Lebenswelt’, the detranscendentalised intersubjectivity of the lifeworld; four idealisations performed in communicative action — that an objective world is shared, that the participants are accountable, that the validity claims they raise hold unconditionally beyond their context, and that argumentation makes demanding presuppositions of its own — are presented as the successors of Kant’s ideas of reason, the last of them pairing with reason as the faculty of principles and yielding ‘rational discourse as the unavoidable forum of possible justification’, and the four are said to ‘refer to one another and form aspects of a desublimated reason embodied in everyday communicative practice’.[^22] Its practical face concerns the acting subject. The discourse-ethical explanation of a morality of reason is declared, in the history of philosophy of 2019, ‘ein Vorschlag zur Detranszendentalisierung des kantischen Begriffs der Autonomie’, a proposal for the detranscendentalisation of Kant’s concept of autonomy, and what the detranscendentalisation of Kant’s moral theory costs in the way of grade is stated in the sentence.[^23] The operation is neither a reduction nor a deletion. It changes the concept of the transcendental itself and it ‘leaves the core of Kant’s philosophy untouched; what I have in mind here is the self-understanding of rational subjects as finite and autonomous beings for whom reasons “count.”’ It takes from Kant’s world-producing subjectivity ‘den Panzer des apriorischen Wissens’, the armour of a priori knowledge, and the situated reason which remains is to be grasped from two perspectives at once, ‘gleichzeitig aus der Perspektive des Teilnehmers zu rekonstruieren und aus der Perspektive des Beobachters in ihrem jeweiligen historischen Einbettungskontext zu beschreiben’, reconstructed from the participant’s perspective and described from the observer’s in its historical context of embedding.[^24] That last sentence carries the chapter’s thesis about the method in Habermas’s words, though not under his ordering: the three commitments named above are three faces of this one operation. Reconstructivism is its method, the participant’s reconstruction of a competence; immanence is its site, the lifeworld into which the subject is embedded and from which no reflection escapes; fallibilism is its grade, the surrender of the a priori armour, so that what the reconstruction yields is universal only presumptively and inescapable only de facto, in the formula §2.1.4 displays. That the three are faces of one operation, and not three theses which happen to be held together, is what §2.2 argues under the name of their co-originality, and it is why an enquiry into their conjunction is an enquiry into detranscendentalisation itself. The commitments are entered below at the points where the exposition of the method reaches them: the first at §2.1.1, the third at §2.1.2, the second at §2.1.4.

The situation so described is a stage in a learning process and not a settled theory, and Habermas describes it as such in every phase. The break is dated to ‘the first generation of Hegel’s disciples’, since when ‘there has been no alternative to postmetaphysical thinking’; the volume of 2012 restates the claim as the judgement that ‘today there is no alternative to the postmetaphysical mode of doing philosophy’ and assigns the genealogy which accompanies it a critical rather than a justificatory office, that of fostering ‘reflexive awareness of the contingency of the context of emergence’ of the premises a fallibilist thinking claims for the present; the thinking so reached ‘operates “without a ground” insofar as it creates the building blocks of its “ground of validity” from its own resources, instead of borrowing them from some traditional authority’.[^25] The genealogy is then extended to a history of philosophy, whose introduction describes the process it traces as one which can be followed from within though it is not steered from within alone: ‘als einen intern nachvollziehbaren, aber nicht als einen ausschließlich intern gesteuerten Lernprozess’.[^26] A learning process has no standpoint outside itself from which its result might be certified, and Habermas draws the consequence for his own recommendation. The preface to the second volume states that the recommendation to philosophise henceforth only in the post-metaphysical mode could not be justified ‘without at the same time arguing for the concept of “communicative reason”’, and that ‘Anyone who wants to engage in the business of clarifying philosophy’s proper role must actually do philosophy. This reflexive circle is unavoidable’.[^27] The self-understanding is therefore self-critical in a determinate sense: the account of what philosophy can be is itself a philosophical claim of the kind the account permits, and it can be sustained only by the practice it describes. Its interlocutors are fixed by the same circle. Kant and Hegel are addressed directly, as the two modes of justification, transcendental and dialectical, which the essay of 1981 follows through their own traditions of self-criticism, and the concept of communicative reason is said to free thought ‘from the dilemma of having to choose between Kant and Hegel’. Adorno, Husserl and Heidegger are addressed as stations of an incomplete detranscendentalisation: Adorno for a negative dialectics which aims at ‘a negative metaphysics’ and keeps the outsider’s standpoint the second tenet names; Husserl for the German university tradition in which the methodical attitude ‘was inflated into the internally justified precedence of theory over practice’; Heidegger for an existential rooting of the world-projecting subject which leaves the problem of intersubjectivity ‘unsolvable’ and then, in the later work, makes ‘the prejudicing force of linguistic world-disclosure’ devalue every learning process within the world. The verdict is the same for all: ‘All these attempts to detranscendentalize reason continue to get entangled in the prior conceptual decisions of transcendental philosophy’.[^28]

A critique of metaphysics conducted as a claim about what philosophy can be is itself a claim of the kind the vacated court used to certify, and the difficulty is one Habermas states against others before it is put to him. Against critical rationalism and the negativism of Adorno the essay of 1981 observes that both ‘reject transcendental and dialectical means of cognition while at the same time using them in a paradoxical way’, and adds, under a hedge which is his, that the two attempts may also be viewed as showing that the two modes of justification ‘cannot be abolished except on penalty of self-contradiction’; against the totalising critique of reason from Nietzsche to Derrida the lectures of 1985 hold that it ‘gets caught in a performative contradiction since subject-centered reason can be convicted of being authoritarian in nature only by having recourse to its own tools’; and against relativism the essay on the unity of reason holds that the thesis granting every view of the world its perspectival right ‘cannot be put forth without a performative self-contradiction’.[^29] The instrument is the one §2.2.2 fixes in a bounded sense, and what it convicts in each case is a universal claim advanced by a position which denies itself the right to universal claims. The recoil is evident. A thinking which declares metaphysics finished, and declares it of all philosophy, advances a thesis about the totality of philosophical claims; and Rawls presses the recoil in its sharpest form. Habermas’s position, he holds, ‘is a comprehensive doctrine and covers many things far beyond political philosophy’, and it is metaphysical in a stated sense: ‘His logic is metaphysical in the following sense: it presents an account of what there is. And what there is are human beings engaged in communicative action in their lifeworld.’ The premise of the charge is entered in a note, that metaphysics is ‘at least a general account of what there is’, so that ‘To deny certain metaphysical doctrines is to assert another such doctrine’.[^30] Henrich had reached the same point from Kant, quoting against Habermas the sentence of the first preface on the indifferentists who, in so far as they think at all, fall back into the metaphysical assertions they affected to despise.[^31] Both charges are sound against one reading of the word, and the reading is not Habermas’s. If a position is metaphysical whenever it holds a view about what there is, then no philosophical position can be otherwise, and the predicate marks nothing. A criterion is therefore wanted which sorts positions by their manner and not by their having a matter.

The criterion is the one the vacated court applied to itself. Kant distinguishes the dogmatic procedure of reason, which a science cannot forgo, from dogmatism, which is that procedure pursued by a pure reason that has not first examined what it is able to do, and declares criticism the opponent of the second alone.[^32] Habermas’s is the same criterion transposed from a faculty to a practice, and it is stated twice in his own voice. Against analytic materialism, in the essay addressed to Henrich, a position is called metaphysical which ‘sticks to what is universal when the real issue is carrying through an abstractly posed program with scientific means’: on that sentence the fault lies not in the universality of the claim but in its exemption from the enquiry which alone could earn it. Against Rawls, in the reply of 2011 whose concession on weak transcendental arguments §2.1.4 reads, the decisive impulse of post-metaphysical thinking is said to be Kant’s critique of metaphysics, and the mode of thinking is defined by what its statements may and may not do: ‘we no longer think that we are in a position to make unqualified statements about the whole of nature and history, about the totality of being, at least not without referring to our reflexively analysed means of representation’, while ‘a discursively redeemable validity claim’ attaches to every philosophical proposition which remains within that frame, so that ‘Fallibilism and continued controversies on all fronts are the inevitable price to be paid for metaphysical abstinence.’[^33] The criterion has two limbs and both are needed. The first is reflexive: a claim about the whole is admissible only through an analysis of the means by which the whole is represented, which is Kant’s antecedent critique of capacity conducted upon the medium rather than the faculty. The second is procedural: every such claim carries a validity claim redeemable in argument and is thereby exposed to correction, which is the tenet foundationalism denies. On this criterion the second tenet is not renounced by abstaining from a view of the totality but by relocating the place from which the view is had, since ‘No “observer perspective”, however neutral, can provide the appropriate meta-level for testing the premises of postmetaphysical thinking further, but only the horizontal exchange of pro and contra arguments’.[^34] The practice of argumentation thereby inherits two properties of the court it replaces: it is the place at which claims are certified, and it is said to be a place to which there is no alternative. The first property is what makes the thinking critical of its own tenets, since its premises are tested where every other claim is tested, in the exchange of reasons, and are exempt from nothing. The second property is a claim of a different kind, about the standing of the practice itself, and it is the claim over which Apel and Habermas divide; Chapter Three conducts the dispute, and nothing about it is settled here.

The dispute with Henrich is the test of the criterion, since Henrich meets it. Habermas reads him as the advocate of ‘a metaphysics that might be capable of enduring after Kant’, one which begins from the subject’s relation to itself and returns to world-constituting subjectivity ‘as the definitive horizon of self-interpretation’; he declines the terminological quarrel over the word, which ‘does not lead any further into the matter itself’, concedes that ‘The reconstructive tasks of philosophy … are not in dispute’, and locates the disagreement in ‘the enlightening role of philosophy in the strict sense, directed toward the whole of life practices’. He also concedes the fallibility point in Henrich’s own words, since Henrich’s second thesis holds that ‘every theory of the series of natural numbers is in fact fallible’, and Habermas replies only that what holds of algebra ‘is all the more valid for ethics’.[^35] Henrich’s theses accept the constraint and turn it. A metaphysics of the elementary, which examines the presuppositions of object-determining and normative knowledge, is distinguished from a metaphysics of resolving closure, whose thoughts are ‘not elementary thoughts, but thoughts of a resolving closure’ (*Abschluß*), and the latter is exempted from no criticism: the fallibility of every theory of the number series ‘is no argument against the spontaneous generation of the series itself’, and the justification of metaphysics ‘does not require the claim to possess an infallible theory’. What Henrich charges is not that Habermas is fallibilist but that he stops short of the reflection which fallibilism should have forced: the theory of communicative action, he holds, ‘opts — albeit unwillingly — for immediacy. It regards the resources of the lifeworld, without further enquiry, as ultimately reliable and valid.’[^36] On the two-limbed criterion Henrich’s metaphysics is not dogmatic, since it claims neither infallibility nor exemption from argument; what excludes it is not the first of Habermas’s three respects, which it satisfies, but the second and the third, since it seeks in a doctrine of closure an orientation of conscious life which the theoretical form cannot supply and a conclusive whole which post-metaphysical thinking declines to rank; and Dews is right that on Henrich’s definition the enterprise violates none of the constraints Habermas sets, so that the dispute is not one the criterion can decide. What the criterion can do is turn Henrich’s charge into a question with an address. If the lifeworld’s resources are taken as ultimately reliable without further enquiry, the immediacy lies in the categorial premise on which the reconstruction of those resources rests and in the standpoint from which the reconstruction is conducted (§2.2.). A second observation Dews records is adopted as a limit on the whole section: a critique of answers which have become implausible is not a demonstration that the domain of questions is obsolete. Nothing in what follows treats the renunciation of the three tenets as a proof that the questions they answered badly have lapsed.[^37]

One further distinction is required before the method is examined, since the criterion has assigned to post-metaphysical thinking two critical tasks which do not have the same form: to be critical of the sciences with which it cooperates, and to be critical of its own tenets. Habermas separates the two procedures in the introduction to the essays of 1971, where he records that he had ‘not adequately distinguished posterior reconstruction [*Nachkonstruktion*] from reflection on oneself’. Rational reconstructions deal with anonymous rule systems with which any competent subject can comply, and so ‘do not encompass subjectivity, within the horizon of which alone the experience of reflection is possible’; self-reflection, by contrast, makes conscious what was unconscious ‘in a manner rich in practical consequences’, whereas the knowledge a reconstruction yields ‘has no practical consequences. By learning logic or linguistics I acquire theoretical knowledge, but in general I do not thereby change my previous practice of reasoning or speaking.’[^38] The task of being critical of other disciplines is discharged by reconstruction, the method §2.1.1 expounds, which cooperates with the sciences by supplying hypotheses about competences and submitting them to the tests (§2.1.2); it is a task in which the theorist’s own subjectivity is not in play. The task of being critical of its own tenets is not discharged by that method, since the tenets are not anonymous rules a competent speaker follows but commitments of the theorist, and what is turned upon them is reflection in the other sense, the argument turned upon its own conditions which §2.3.1 names philosophical self-reflexivity and whose three resources it costs. The two are the Kantian and the Hegelian forms of critical reflection respectively, the transcendental enquiry into conditions and the self-reflection which dissolves a false consciousness of itself, and Habermas’s programme, on the reading §2.3 pursues, requires both while its method is only the first. A third form, the elenctic, which convicts a denial by its own performance and derives nothing, is not Habermas’s and is introduced by Chapter Three as the instrument of these chapters, under the licence argued there; the question this section leaves is whether the second task can be discharged at all by a thinking whose only method is the first, and §2.3 returns that question with its cost.

### §2.1.1 · C1 · Reconstructivism: Reconstruction against explanation: object, method, the two theses Hypothetical status, the two loci of corrigibility, the silence of reconstruction

C1 holds that philosophy can lay claim to the structures of communicative rationality only by reconstruction: by taking as its data the competences speaking and acting subjects exercise, and rendering explicit the implicit know-how those competences instantiate. What is reconstructed is neither an empirical regularity open to inductive generalisation nor a structure of pure consciousness, but the operative competence exercised whenever a speaker acts with an orientation to reaching understanding, and the reconstruction accordingly renders a knowing-how as a knowing-that and does not record a regularity. Habermas states the connection and its ground in one movement: ‘There is then a fundamental connection between understanding communicative actions and constructing rational interpretations’, the connection being fundamental because a two-stage procedure, grasping the course of an interaction first and measuring it against an ideal type afterwards, is unavailable.[^39] The thesis governs the interpretation of communicative action, and it is read in this chapter as reaching the reconstruction of a competence by way of the datum: what formal pragmatics reconstructs is a competence exercised in actions, and an action is identifiable as communicative only under an interpretation which has already taken a position upon the claims it raises, so that no stage of the reconstruction is carried out upon a datum secured independently of such a position; the further step, to a procedure itself single-staged, belongs to the reading and is not a consequence of the manner in which the datum is had. Because the competence is one the speaker already commands, its reconstruction must be confirmed from within, by the maieutic confirmation, and that confirmation is not sovereign, being one of three mutually constraining criteria, alongside convergence with developmental enquiry and explanatory power across the cognate domains.[^40]

The place philosophy occupies after the renunciations is named in the essay which opens the discourse-ethics volume: philosophy, ‘while well advised to withdraw from the problematic roles of usher (*Platzanweiser*) and judge, can and ought to retain its claim to reason, provided it is content to play the more modest roles of stand-in (*Platzhalter*) and interpreter’. The place is held for ‘Empirical theories with strong universalistic claims’, whose chance of emerging ‘is greatest in the reconstructive sciences’, which, ‘Starting primarily from the intuitive knowledge of competent subjects … and secondarily from systematic knowledge handed down by culture’, ‘explain the presumably universal bases of rational experience and judgment, as well as of action and linguistic communication’. Reconstructive science thus enters as the discipline for which philosophy holds a place, the modality of what it explains fixed by the definition’s own adverb, *presumably*; the method and the grade of its results are expounded.[^41] Philosophy relinquishes the usher’s warrant, the authority to assign each science its place, whose assignments are final because the authority which draws them certifies them itself and exempts them from correction by the sciences it assigns, and takes up the stand-in’s, which holds a position open for theories whose results it neither anticipates nor certifies, and whose own results are put up for discussion as theirs are. On the reading adopted here it is the fallibilism, and no retreat from the claim to reason, which distinguishes the two offices: what has been renounced is a tribunal.[^42] After metaphysics philosophy ‘surrenders its extraordinary status’, is ‘no longer in possession of an affirmative theory of the good life’, and the lifeworld to which its gaze reverts is ‘now present only as horizon and background’ and ‘evades the grasp of theoretical objectification’;[^43] a discipline whose subject-matter is present to it only as horizon has no vantage upon that horizon, and the constraint this places upon its self-reflection is drawn out at §2.3.1.

The method by which that reconstructive science proceeds is best taken against its nearest rival, and Habermas separates the two by their object and not by their technique. Reconstructive procedures, he writes in 1976, are not characteristic of sciences developing nomological hypotheses about domains of observable events but of sciences which ‘systematically reconstruct the intuitive knowledge of competent subjects’; their data gathered by ‘a well-thought-out maeutic method of interrogation’. The essay of 1983 fixes the object in the same terms, formal-pragmatic analysis ‘focuses on the general and necessary conditions for the validity of symbolic expressions and achievements’ and concerns ‘rational reconstructions of the know-how of subjects who are capable of speech and action’, subjects who are credited with the ability to produce valid utterances and to tell, at least intuitively, valid from invalid ones.[^44] The theory of argumentation ‘does not have to provide a derivation for such a system in the sense of a transcendental deduction’, since ‘a reliable procedure for testing corresponding reconstructive hypotheses suffices’; the 1981 work opens by surrendering the ‘transcendental-philosophical hopes for an aprioristic reconstruction of the equipment of a nonempirical species subject’, and records that the theorist’s interpretation is exposed to the same criticism as the utterances he interprets. [^45]

Therefore, the identification of pragmatic presuppositions, ‘for which the search for performative contradictions provides a guide, relies upon a maieutic method’. It serves to make a sceptic ‘aware of presuppositions he knows intuitively’; ‘to cast this pretheoretical knowledge in an explicit form that will enable the skeptic to recognize his intuitions in this description’; and ‘to corroborate, through counterexamples, the proponent’s assertion that there are no alternatives to the presuppositions he has made explicit’, labelled (2a), (2b) and (2c).[^46] Of the last two he says that they ‘contain unmistakable hypothetical elements’: the description is ‘a hypothetical reconstruction that can provide only a more or less correct rendering of intuitions’, and ‘it needs maieutic confirmation’, so that it is a proposal about the competence and not a transcript, its correctness qualified in advance as approximate, its warrant lying in elicited assent; and, hinged to the third, in the English of record, by ‘Similarly’, where the German has *Und*, the assertion that no alternative to a given presupposition exists, ‘that it is one of the inescapable (i.e., necessary and general) presuppositions, has the status of an assumption’, which, ‘like a lawlike hypothesis’ — *Gesetzeshypothese* — ‘must be checked against individual cases’.[^47] Habermas glosses inescapability as comprising necessity and generality, so that what falls under the status of an assumption is a conjunction with necessity as a conjunct. The test he prescribes licenses a narrower result. A lawlike hypothesis is checked against individual cases, and the individual cases of a presupposition of argumentation are argumentations, actual or attempted; such a check can establish that no argumentation has been found lacking the presupposition, not that none could have lacked it, since a presupposition holding of every arguer as a matter of fact would survive it as intact as one no arguer could lack. The necessity conjunct is thus hypothesised without being testable by the procedure assigned to it, and a cognate admission stands in 1976: ‘Every reconstruction of a basic conceptual system of possible experience has to be regarded as a hypothetical proposal that can be tested against new experiences’.[^48] The proviso of 1983 is stated in a single passage:

‘To be sure, the intuitive knowledge of rules that subjects capable of speech and action must use if they are to be able to participate in argumentation is in a certain sense not fallible. But this is not true of our reconstruction of this pretheoretical knowledge and the claim to universality that we connect with it. The certainty with which we put our knowledge of rules into practice does not extend to the truth of proposed reconstructions of presuppositions hypothesized to be general’.[^49]

The concessive (‘To be sure’) marks the non-fallibility as granted, not argued; the hedge (‘in a certain sense’) withholds the sense in which it holds. The concession is then withheld from two constructions: from ‘our reconstruction of this pretheoretical knowledge’, and from ‘the claim to universality that we connect with it’. The hypothetical elements were lodged, a few sentences earlier, in substeps (2b) and (2c). The reading that the two sentences answer to the two substeps one apiece — the description-and-confirmation sentence to (2b), the ‘Similarly’-sentence to (2c) — is a reading, and it is the one the sentences’ own subject-matter invites: the first speaks of the description and its confirmation, the second of the assertion that no alternative exists and its check against cases. What is added here is the consequence. The German verb of the last sentence is *sich übertragen*: the certainty of practice ‘überträgt sich nicht auf die Wahrheit von Rekonstruktionsvorschlägen für hypothetisch allgemeine Präsuppositionen’, does not transfer itself to the truth of reconstruction proposals for presuppositions hypothesised to be general. The passage of a practice’s certainty to the truth of a reconstruction proposal is named a “transfer”, a notion explicitly repudiated by Habermas. He contends that such a transposition of certainty is untenable, given that a reconstruction is inherently provisional and subject to discursive validation, much like a logician’s description.[^50]

Description and generality-claim are advanced, warranted and tested separately, and separately liable to fail; both are propositional. Substep (2b) stands or falls by a speaker’s recognition of the description offered him, a first-personal acknowledgement; (2c) by the search for a deviant case, a third-personal check upon a generalisation. A reconstruction could render correctly what competent speakers do while the claim that all speakers must do it remained unwarranted. Habermas enumerates the failure-modes elsewhere in the same terms — a false choice of examples, the obscuring and distorting of correct intuitions, the overgeneralising of individual cases — the second answering to the description, the third to the generality-claim.[^51] Of any formal-pragmatic result it may be asked which of the two things it says — that this is the rule, or that the rule is general — is claimed.

Nor does the demonstration of performative self-contradiction escape the silence. Habermas makes the search for performative contradictions the guide of step (2) as a whole, and what the demonstration proves, that the rules have no alternatives, is the assertion (2c) is to corroborate; but the proof runs by showing that each attempted alternative makes use of what it would replace, and its yield, on the warning Habermas reports from Schönrich and endorses, which Chapter Three §3.2.4 displays, is no more than quasi-empirical. Whatever modal reach it carries is the reach of an exhibition, which convicts a denial on the occasion of its performance and derives nothing, and the licence for it is sought at Chapter Three §3.3.1.

Reconstruction is therefore silent at both tests, and silent by construction: each is directed upon content or extension, and the modality of what is enacted is neither. A counterfactual variation of the practice must be put to a speaker for acknowledgement or surveyed as instances, falling back into one of the two; that the method has no third resource is a claim made here about it, and §2.1.3 gives the demarcation of the data on which it rests, the exclusion of a third test being the premise just entered. What would settle the question between a transcendental and a naturalistic reading must arrive from outside reconstruction, as a premise about modal standing the method neither furnishes nor certifies. The requirement is identified here and is not a finding of the programme under examination. Pedersen registers the difficulty as an unclarity about how the reconstructive hypothesis is to be tested, without naming its source; the tests are not indeterminate in application but silent in subject-matter.[^52] Nor does that silence license the inference that the enacted inescapability is itself in doubt; the objection which presses it, and its answer, stand below, where the third commitment is entered.

### §2.1.2 · The demarcation of data and the three exclusions

The definition of 1983 names a second point of departure, the systematic knowledge handed down by culture, and the demarcation concerns tests and not points of departure: what a culture transmits in its logics and grammars supplies candidate reconstructions, and a candidate is confirmed or defeated by the same two tests as any other, to which indirect corroboration adds no third. The first of three exclusions is of the sub-personal: what is reconstructed is not the grammatical competence isolated by generative linguistics, much of which a speaker exercises without being able to acknowledge its rules, and Habermas separates linguistic from communicative competence by their objects.[^53] The most exacting objection to that exclusion can be drawn from Apel’s treatment of the Chomskyan material it invokes, written against Harman and Ryle. Apel accepts the description of reconstructive science and denies the epistemic settlement attached to it. He divides the reconstructive process in two, an *a priori* ascertainment of features of discursive rationality, taken to be ‘normative conditions of the possibility of the empirical-historical reconstruction of human competencies in the social sciences’, and that reconstruction, a division he draws while setting aside, by his own statement, whether there are areas of non-discernibility between the two, and he places the self-accountability he postulates among what makes the reconstructive sciences possible and not among what they could establish, an exemption stated in his own words: the self-accountability of discursive rationality ‘does not belong, I consider, to the possible empirical results of the reconstructive sciences but to the conditions of their possibility’.[^54] The rules of grammar are ‘nevertheless internalized as a component of social norms, i.e. in the sense of a normative consciousness’, every competent native speaker ‘can make explicit his ability to follow rules correctly, to a certain extent’, and Chomsky is right to refuse confinement to ‘Ryle’s disjunction between “knowing how” and “knowing that”’.[^55] At the first level, which compresses what Habermas divides between know-how and a first-level know-that, stands the intuitive rule-knowledge of the competent speaker, a knowing-how, exercised in the production and assessment of utterances, and directed *intentione recta* upon the world spoken of, upon the object and not upon the rule by which the speaking proceeds. At the second level stands the theorist’s articulation of those rules, a knowing-that, propositional, corrigible, and directed upon the rules themselves; the interpreter who moves to the second level turns from the surface of what is said towards the rule governing it, so that, in Cooke’s rendering of Habermas’s sentence, ‘she no longer looks through it *intentione recta* to the world’.[^56]

The exclusion therefore requires an argument, and the argument is one of object before it is one of criterion: what formal pragmatics reconstructs is the competence to reach understanding, and a rule of grammar belongs to that competence only where its following is answerable to a speaker’s acknowledgement, what is demanded at that margin being not that a speaker state a rule but that he acknowledge as his own the rule offered him.[^57] Habermas works the demarcation out against Searle by name, taking over the ‘essential rules’ and the ‘sincerity rule’ rather than refusing them, and an institutional account of speech-act competence may accordingly be granted in full without disturbing the exclusion.[^58] Apel’s qualification ‘to a certain extent’ concedes the criterion and marks its threshold; granted in full, it shows some grammatical rules to lie within reach of acknowledgement, which places them under the reconstruction of another competence and supplies no case of a rule of communication followed while no acknowledgement is available.

The strong forms leave the evidential ground and contest the reconstructive relation itself. The charge against presence, that to explicate a competence is to posit a plenitude awaiting articulation, delivers, pressed to its conclusion, the thesis of this chapter rather than displacing it: the thesis that the enactment is reachable only under a corrigible articulation of it, which is the *Begründungsdefizit* in its first interval, and the further claim the charge would need, that the enactment is nothing apart from its articulation, falls under the analysis of denial at §2.2.2; Derrida’s form of the charge, from citational repeatability, reaches a requirement the elenctic exhibition makes on its own behalf, and is quoted and costed at §2.3.2.[^63] The charge from genealogy, that the decentred subject who holds validity apart from acceptance is no competence discovered but a subject-form produced, is met by the discipline of §2.1.3 only in its genetic form, where the passage from provenance to title is blocked; what is conceded is that the corroboration drawn from developmental psychology is indirect and partial, and where power reaches not the genesis of communicative structures but their constitution, the objection must be met upon other ground, and the question is the one Chapter One §1.4.3 enters among those the Conclusion holds open.

Naturalist pressure arrives at the second source of data. Speakers acknowledge rules they do not follow and follow rules they cannot acknowledge, and a programme resting on acknowledgement would be one about what speakers believe; Rorty’s case against the universality of the practices Habermas reconstructs runs here, treating such self-descriptions as a particular culture’s contingent vocabulary.[^64] The objection would be decisive if acknowledgement were asked to certify the generality of a rule, or were sovereign over its description; it is neither, since the two tests are held together and each corrects the other: an acknowledged rule which the instances show unfollowed is defeated by the instances, a rule followed which no speaker can acknowledge falls by the first exclusion outside the competence reconstructed, and what an acknowledgement settles is that the description offered to a speaker is a description of his competence, its warrant for the content being defeasible and its silence upon the extension complete. The second exclusion is of the anthropological. The genealogical objection, that a historically particular argumentative culture has been mistaken for a species-universal, misidentifies the level at which invariance is asserted, the structure of positions occupiable by anyone who argues and not the incidence of arguing; a sharper form reaches past that reply, since a speaker can be asked what rule he was following, and his acknowledgement count, only where reason-giving already has authority, so that the evidential base is internal to the argumentative culture whose universality is at issue. Availability of the data only from within a reason-giving culture shows that the investigation is situated, not that its object is: the study of arithmetical competence only among calculators shows something about the enquiry and nothing about number, and whether the structure of positions an argumentative practice commands is like number in that respect is what the genealogist must argue. What the genealogist would need, and the objection does not supply, is the further claim that a situated investigation can reach only situated objects, a claim which, advanced in argument, claims for itself the unrestricted reach it denies to situated investigation as such, and whose standing falls under the analysis of denial §2.2.2 gives; the strong forms of the charge are located at §2.1.5. The exclusion leaves standing a claim indexed to a practice, and §2.1.4 shows the indexing to be Habermas’s own throughout the dated series. The third exclusion is of the statistical, the one most readily taken for evasion: the complaint that the idealising presuppositions are rarely satisfied measures a claim indexed to a practice against a criterion it never answered to, since the presuppositions are made rather than met, the object is the structure of positions a competence commands, and not a regularity, the data are acknowledgements and instances and not measurements, and the check against cases is a search for a defeater and not an accumulation of support. Each exclusion removes a kind of datum from the evidential base; what the base leaves is a relation between positions, and its leaving no other is a finding about the base and not about the world.

A more serious objection comes from the transcendental-pragmatic side, and its seriousness is owed to its coming from the ally rather than the opponent. Apel’s charge against the reconstructive programme has three limbs: that it declines to distinguish ‘the transcendental-pragmatic reconstruction of the ultimate presuppositions of argumentation’ from ‘an empirical-hypothetical reconstruction of cultural evolution or concrete history’; that the proposal to test the presuppositions empirically, by questioning competent speakers, is incoherent, ‘for in order even to understand what “empirical testing” is supposed to mean, one must at least presuppose the validity of those presuppositions of argumentation’; and that the programme’s appeals to modesty, which the chapter identifies with the offices of stand-in and interpreter, conflate methodological with sociological or psychological categories.[^65] The responses submitted regarding the three distinct points are presented on behalf of the programme rather than derived from it, with the second point addressed initially. This concession is granted strictly within the specified limitations: no empirical finding could confirm or disconfirm the presuppositions *as enacted*, and the procedure Habermas describes asks none to. What the three criteria certify is the articulation, the hypothetical elements being the two he himself lodges in substeps (2b) and (2c), and no empirical finding bears upon the presuppositions as enacted in either direction, not because corroboration is weak but because an enactment is not the kind of item a finding confirms or refutes; the criteria so construed are no test of the presuppositions but a constraint upon their statement, and the incoherence Apel alleges does not arise. What hypothetical status amounts to in practice is stated most plainly where the reconstruction is most ambitious. Of the three ways a reconstruction may fail, the one Habermas marks as the most frequent is overgeneralisation, so that the corrigibility he concedes bears most frequently, on his own ranking, upon the scope of the reconstruction;[^67] and of the system of rights he writes that no one can claim access to it in the singular apart from the interpretations history has already made available, the system ‘does not exist in transcendental purity’, and what can instruct a generalising reconstruction of the intuitions guiding the practice of self-legislation is two hundred years of European constitutional law.[^68] On one reading of that passage, reconstruction works from historically available models, whose provenance is neither concealed nor apologised for; what the admission does not concede is the inference the provincialism charge draws from it.[^69] Discussing the moral point of view he grants that actually entering discourse belongs to the idea of it, since ‘otherwise the solipsistic character of an examination of norms undertaken “in the privacy of individual reflection” would reassert itself’, and he names what must be avoided, the isolated subject who takes upon himself, as ‘custodian of the transcendental’, the examination of norms for everyone else. The *what* remains a linguistic-cognitively mediated interpretation answerable to the practice it renders as reconstructive; and it anchors otherwise than the second, since the denial of the conditions, performed in argumentation, is held to be self-defeating in the deed, whether or not any theory has correctly described what is denied. The cost is this: the position claims to secure the conditions against scepticism, a claim whose title is examined at Chapter Three §3.3, and does not secure the account given of them, and that is the *Begründungsdefizit* in its first interval, as §2.1.5 named it, the cost of the position and not an incidental consequence of it.

Apel’s defence is addressed to this difficulty, and his distinction is taken while his conclusion is refused. He holds his transcendental-pragmatic argument untouched by the ‘de-transcendentalization’ he attributes to Rorty, on the ground that, unlike Strawson’s, it concerns not the propositional content of arguments nor the categorial schemes of knowledge but the performative part of the argument by which validity claims are raised, adding that even were every transcendental argument about categorial schemes relativised, the speech act of raising that question would lay claim to the presuppositions at issue.[^71] Nevertheless, what is not secured is the propositional claim that the presuppositions so exhibited are the presuppositions of argument as such, a claim made in a proposition, by a theorist, which stands under whatever fallibility propositions stand under. The asymmetry between an enacted *Dass* and its propositional articulation therefore tells, in this application, against the philosopher from whom it is taken. Nothing in this shows the reconstruction false; the conclusion is that its warrant cannot be of the kind the reconstruction itself supplies, since a claim about what cannot be repudiated in performance is not established by a description of performances, however competent, and what an exhibition which the one addressed carries out establishes is of another kind, so that the claim about the type is warranted on neither route. The exhibition so named shares its grammar with Apel’s *Aufweis*, the exhibition he sets apart from demonstration, and parts from it at the yield: what the addressee’s own performance establishes is operative and indexed to the occasion of its enactment, and the elenctic route claims nothing propositional from it in the theorist’s person, where the transcendental-pragmatic route reads a *Letztbegründung* off the same exhibition. Nor is that reading rescued, so far as reconstructive science reaches, by taking the propositions of strict reflection for the enactment’s own self-explication rather than a description of it, since an explication put to the tests of recognition and counterexample stands under them whatever its parentage, and neither test discriminates the modality claimed for it (§2.1.2); whether an explication reached by strict reflection may claim an exemption from the tests is the question C3 enters. The elenctic route is the one this argument leaves standing, and Chapter Three takes it up at §3.3 as a route in its own right and not as a repair offered to Habermas

### C2 · Immanence

C2 generalises into a thesis about the standpoint of philosophical reflection what C1’s features jointly enforced: no such reflection occupies a standpoint beyond history from which the conditions of communicative rationality might be secured more firmly than in the practices where they are operative, an undertaking the programme makes and not a limit imposed upon it from without. Its textual centre is the programme of detranscendentalisation, in which the transcendental subject is replaced by socialised subjects whose cognition is embedded in a lifeworld, and the conditions so recovered are necessary in the weak sense: what is stripped away is, in a summary which is not Habermas’s wording, the apodicticity, the worldlessness and the finality the Kantian transcendental had claimed,[^72] and what is not stripped away is the necessity itself, since a presupposition which cannot be set aside by anyone engaged in the practice whose presupposition it is remains necessary relatively to that practice.

The philosophy-of-language ground of the commitment is Wittgenstein’s, and Apel takes a negative result from it first: the impossibility of a private language, which he calls a ‘truly revolutionary insight for all philosophical thinking’ and restates as the thesis that ‘one cannot follow a rule all by oneself, guided perhaps by introspectively accessible standards’, since a solitary rule-follower would possess no criteria for the correct use of language.[^73] What the argument defeats is methodological solipsism, the doctrine that a subject’s self-understanding is constituted independently of communication, the public medium serving only for transmission; Apel dates the doctrine, in the notes to his Wittgenstein chapter, from Descartes’s *Meditationes* to Husserl’s *Cartesian Meditations*, and holds that participation in a language game ‘replaces the methodological solipsism of attaining understanding through empathy’. The refutation is not an argument that private constitution is impossible, and its force lies in the difference; it is an observation about what the solipsist offers: ‘the self-understanding which the methodological solipsist seeks to offer … is itself mediated through the public rule of a language-game and the “life-form” that is interwoven with the latter’, so that the starting-point is already public, had under a rule the solipsist did not privately institute, what is shared being the public rule and not the experience had under it;[^74] the dating, on the reading proposed, locates the doctrine’s home in the phenomenological tradition rather than in the analytic one, and Chapter One §1.3.2 follows its lifeworld consequences.

Three questions must be put to the displacement before it is called an overcoming, and the answers are of unequal strength, the first resting upon a contested reading:[^75] whether the plurality (intersubjectivity) installed in place of the solitary subject is constitutive or merely aggregative; whether the theorist can reach that plurality from outside it; and whether the theory’s own procedure repeats the structure the charge condemns. The first is answered by the rule-following argument itself: were the displaced subject replaced by a sum of subjects each of whom might in principle have managed alone, solipsism would merely have been multiplied, whereas following a rule admits of mistake, and the correction of a mistake requires a standard whose application the follower does not himself certify, so that the virtual division of labour between practitioner and critic belongs to the concept of rule-following, the middle step being a reading, which the individualist denies; the second person is thus constitutive and not cumulative. The second is answered by the participation requirement, and the answer is where immanence shows itself a commitment of method. If meanings are accessible only from within a practice, the theorist of meaning cannot occupy the observer’s position without losing his object: an interpreter who would understand an utterance is, on Habermas’s account, a *virtual participant*, drawn at least potentially into negotiation over the meaning and validity of what he interprets, and because he must assess the reasons he reconstructs, the standards by which he assesses them are the general structures of communication he shares with those he interprets, structures which ‘provide the critical means to penetrate a given context, to burst it open from within and to transcend it’, so that a capacity for critique is recovered from the immersion which was thought to preclude it.[^76] Apel’s own formulation converges: the standpoint from which a third person is spoken about is a partial and not a complete objectivisation, and radical interpretation on the Quinean and Davidsonian model is to be refused, for proceeding as though a subject not yet a member of any communication community could begin framing hypotheses about others.[^77]  The third is answered at the close of this section, by the self-application the incoherence charge forces; before it, Apel’s step beyond Wittgenstein must be taken up, since it is where the displacement is first called an overcoming. From the impossibility of a position outside the language game Apel infers, in a reconstruction of two passages, the standing of that game as transcendental: ‘One cannot decide to affirm or negate the norms of the transcendental language-game from a position outside the language-game’, and ‘The negation of this constitutes the basic error of methodological solipsism’; where the *Investigations* disperses the public into a plurality of incommensurable language games, Apel asks in which game the thesis of that plurality is advanced, and answers, ‘I will call this institution the transcendental language game’ language is on his account not only the normatively binding institution of institutions but ‘the “meta-institution” of all dogmatically established institutions’.[^78] The inference conflates two claims, and only one of them is carried by the argument from which they are drawn. The claim that there is no position outside the language game from which its norms might be affirmed or denied is established by the rule-following considerations so far as they reach, what they exclude being a standpoint answerable to no practice whatever and not a standpoint taken within some other practice; it is a claim about the *unavailability of an exterior*; the claim that the game whose norms cannot be denied from outside is therefore *transcendental*, its rules stating conditions of possibility rather than the constitutive features of one historically achieved practice, is a further claim, which the argument from the impossibility of a private language does not reach. Nor is it carried by the question in which game the thesis of plurality is advanced: that question convicts the pluralist of claiming a reach his thesis denies, an exhibition within the practice in which he argues, and what it yields is a result in the deed, not the proposition that the game so exhibited is transcendental. A practice may be inescapable and still be a practice; what excludes the alternative is not shown by the impossibility of getting behind the practice in order to look. The distinction between an inescapability of that kind and a transcendental necessity is what the whole modal dispute turns upon, and it is drawn here, in the philosophy of language, before any question of ethics arises. Habermas refuses the further step, and the refusal is one form detranscendentalisation takes: he takes over Peirce’s triadic conception of the sign as it stands and orders the plurality of games by the double structure of the speech act, expounded at Chapter One §1.2.1, rather than by a game of higher order, declining not the unavailability of an exterior, which he affirms, but the inference from that unavailability to a transcendental standing for what is exhibited within it.[^79] A further inheritance, Heidegger’s, Apel contests rather than assimilates, holding the constitution of meaning distinct from the justification of validity.[^80] The most challenging question, which cannot be addressed here, is to what extent intersubjectivity is constitutive of meaning. If it is constitutive, this implies that the discursive redemption of the validity of meanings is not external to the intersubjective process. In essence, meaning can be attain to the extent that a dialogic-dialectical process is performed by co-subjectivity, whose conceptual determination is sedimented towards the pragmatic use within a communication community. Nevertheless, the validity of meaning—its apprehension and the validity of utterances in general—although implicitly operative within a community, is not at the disposal of an individual or multiple subjects; rather, it is intrinsically bound to discursive practices as an ongoing process. Thereby, it is not merely a matter of the external evaluation of different subjects in dialogue, which would ultimately result in a surreptitious reintroduction of methodological solipsism at the level of incommensurable language games.

What detranscendentalisation *does*, as against what it gives up, Habermas states in two sentences: ‘With validity claims that transcend all merely local standards of evaluation, the tension between transcendental presuppositions and empirical facts now moves into the facticity of the lifeworld itself. The theory of communicative action detranscendentalizes Kant’s realm of the Intelligible by revealing the idealizing force of anticipation in the unavoidable pragmatic presuppositions of speech acts, that is, at the heart of everyday communicative practices themselves’.[^81] The tension between the transcendental and the empirical is not dissolved by detranscendentalisation and is not shown to have been illusory; it is relocated, and the site to which it is relocated is the practice whose reconstruction the programme is. Immanence is accordingly a thesis about where a tension is borne and not a thesis that there is none to bear, and the bearer is the lifeworld, a domain the programme reaches only through fallible reconstruction, so that C2’s relocation delivers the tension to C1 and C3 to hold; what Habermas describes as a movement inward the chapter takes for the deficit’s provenance, a reading which is not his.

The dilemma of long standing attends any argument of the ambitious form, and it is Stroud’s: a transcendental argument can establish at most that a subject must *believe*, or must *presuppose*, that such-and-such is the case if experience or discourse of a certain kind is to be possible, never that such-and-such *is* the case, and the premise which would license the passage from what must be believed to what obtains is verificationist or idealist, conceding in the licensing what the argument was to have won.[^82] The reply is one Habermas does not make, and it confines the objection rather than contesting it. Stroud’s objection has purchase wherever a transcendental argument is construed as a probative inference of the shape just given, and none against the operative claim, which is no inference of that shape: the exhibition that the denial of a presupposition of argumentation is performatively self-defeating establishes no conclusion of the form *Q* at all, and where no such conclusion is drawn there is no interval between what must be believed and what obtains, and so nothing for the objection to divide. What the exhibition brings about is that a certain speech act is not executed by the one who attempts it, the result being his, in the order of the deed, while the theorist’s report of it stands under whatever fallibility reports stand under. The confinement accepts the second of the two alternatives such an argument faces: either the passage from what must be believed to what obtains is made, and made upon a premise which concedes in the licensing what the argument was to have won, or no such passage is attempted and nothing is established about what obtains. An exhibition which convicts a denier of enacting what he denies employs no transcendental claim of the form ‘q is a condition of the possibility of p’ and draws no conclusion about what obtains; it is an argument from self-defeat, and the title *transcendental* is not claimed for it in these chapters, where the word is reserved to Habermas’s predicate and its five restrictions. The cost is stated rather than hidden: what the exhibition yields is that a certain speech act is not executed here, not that the presupposition obtains. Against the critical rationalist, for whom a justification which stops has chosen its stopping-point, the arbitrary break-off of Albert’s trilemma, the burden rule of §2.2.2, on which a denial that miscarries settles where the burden of proof lies, is no break-off chosen but a distribution the arguer has already made in arguing; that the exhibition of it is itself corrigible is the categorial premise applied to itself, and why the corrigibility opens no regress is argued at Chapter Three §3.3.3, and the confinement is completed at Chapter Three §3.3.2 from the side of signification.[^83] The confinement carries a consequence the position must not suppress, and it is where the aporia §2.3.4 states is first located. Stroud’s gap, confined at the conditions of argumentation, stands *unconfined* wherever the programme is tempted to convert the operative inescapability of a presupposition into a propositional truth about the world, and it stands with full force at the orientation towards truth: the presuppositions of argumentation are conditions the deed enacts, reachable by an exhibition conducted at the level of the deed, whereas the orientation to truth points beyond the practice to a world obtaining independently of it, and no exhibition confined to the deed can reach what by hypothesis transcends it. The exception is accordingly a corollary of the reply and no exception to it, since the confinement held because the conclusion drawn was about a deed and not about a world; followed to the point at which its own ground is absent, Stroud’s objection locates the aporia exhibited at §2.3.4, of which the present disanalogy is the anticipation and not the proof.

Immanence in this sense is neither that of historicism, which relativises validity to particular cultural formations, nor that of Rortyan contextualism. It is a commitment of method and no thesis in the sociology of validity, and one distinction sorts it from both misreadings: the genesis of a reconstruction and the reach of what it reconstructs are separately assessable. The conditions are claimed to be universal in their operative reach, their reconstruction is immanent in its genesis, and the compatibility of the two is the substance of the commitment; what would defeat it is not the demonstration that the reconstruction has a history, which is conceded at the outset, but the demonstration that the reach of what it reconstructs is bounded by that history, a further claim the misreadings assume rather than argue. The contextualist objection, that the distinction begs the question by helping itself to the standpoint-independence immanence had renounced, fails because declining an inference, from the provenance of the models to the boundedness of what they instruct, requires no vantage outside the practice in which the inference is offered. The refusal invites a charge of incoherence: the reconstructing philosopher’s self-description is itself a move within communicative practice, so that the theory either generates a regress of reconstructions without end or falls under its own verdict. The reply is that the self-implication is the self-application of a single commitment, and an application of that shape does not defeat what it applies, since what the verdict imposes upon the self-description is corrigibility and not falsity; the charge depends upon a failure to distinguish a series of reconstructions, each a fallible assertion about the level below it, which does regress, from the series of enactments in which those assertions are made, which does not, and why a regress of that shape is not vicious is argued at Chapter Three §3.3.3. Apel names the requirement the *Selbsteinholungsprinzip*, the self-recuperative principle: a critical-hermeneutic reconstruction of history must be able to account for its own presuppositions of rationality as a possible and factual result of the history it reconstructs, and the principle ‘follows from the principle of performative self-contradiction, given the further premise that the reconstructive sciences are themselves a fact of history to be accounted for’;[^84] he states it of the reconstructive sciences in his own voice, which must reconstruct progress and regress in human history ‘in such a way that the possibility of their own enterprise becomes intelligible as a result of history’.[^85] The principle states a constraint the reconstructive programme can be read as also accepting, while the standing Apel claims for the presuppositions so recovered is declined, on the ground this section has given.

### §2.1.4 · Weak transcendentalism: the dated series and the reservation grid

Do the presuppositions constrain a speaker as what he must do to be arguing at all, as a condition holding of any rational being in the strict Kantian sense, or as what beings of a certain constitution cannot in fact avoid?

Habermas answers that question with a qualified predicate, and the qualification governs three things at once: the modality, the presuppositions being necessary relatively to a practice and not absolutely; the scope, the sociocultural forms of life so far known offering no functional equivalent for the practice; and the epistemic standing of the theorist’s specification, a fallible reconstruction certified by no result of the enquiry. The first two are stated together in 1991 and again in 2020, the third is drawn from the pages examined at §2.1.2, and taking the qualification to bear upon the modality alone misplaces it; nor does relativity to a practice weaken the compulsion upon the arguer within that practice, which is a claim of another order, made of the participant and not of the predicate.[^86] Dating the occurrences shows the predicate in continuous service from 1976, where Habermas states the weakened conception the analytic reception of Kant makes available and declines the word, to 1992, where the necessitation it names is again distinguished from the prescriptive force of a rule of action.[^87] In 1983 the demonstration of performative contradictions is itself called ‘this weak form of transcendental analysis’, and in 1991 the communicative agent is subject to a ‘“must” in the sense of weak transcendental necessitation, without encountering the prescriptive “must” of a rule of action’, a formulation which stands again in 1992.[^88] The statement of 1991 of what such arguments achieve is the most explicit: lacking an equivalent for Kant’s transcendental deduction, ‘we are left with weak transcendental arguments in Strawson’s sense’, which ‘can establish only the factual inescapability’ of the presuppositions of a non-replaceable discursive order, and two lines below, ‘Because we do not in fact have a functional equivalent for rational discourses, we are left with no choice: we unavoidably accept the pragmatic presuppositions of this demanding form of communication because there is no alternative.’ Cronin’s ‘factual inescapability’ renders *faktische Nichtverwerfbarkeit*, non-rejectability, and a presupposition which cannot be rejected is not thereby one which could not have been otherwise; what an argument of Strawson’s kind can establish has been contested since Stroud.[^89] The vocabulary of 1999 states the restriction twice, the concept of the transcendental itself changing with detranscendentalisation and the ‘epistemisch unhintergehbare Zusammenhang’ of truth and justification being not to be stylised into a conceptually indissoluble one; the essay of 2011 declares all post-metaphysical approaches ‘“freestanding”, even those which employ weak transcendental arguments’; and in 2020 the modality of 1991 is restated as the absence of a substitute, the discursive examination of criticisable validity claims being ‘only “ineluctable” or “unavoidable” in the weaker sense that there is no functional equivalent for this practice’.[^90] A counter-instance stands on the page following the Strawson passage of 1991, where ‘the weak transcendental proof, if we can indeed carry it out as I assume in agreement with Apel, suffices to ground the universalistic validity claim’ of a procedurally conceived moral principle, which might be ‘deduced from the normative content of factually inescapable presuppositions’;[^91] the constancy claimed for the predicate and its restrictions is withheld from that sufficiency claim, whose adjudication against the statement of 2020 stands at §2.2.3.

Inescapability is borne by a genitive in the sentence of 1983 which introduces the arguments, ‘aimed at demonstrating that the presuppositions of relevant practices are inescapable’, the presuppositions being individuated from the outset as the presuppositions of a practice, whose boundary the clause of 1983 quoted at Chapter One §1.5.3 marks from outside; it is restricted a second time to discourses ‘so general that it is impossible to replace them by functional equivalents’; a third time in the passage which names the *Faktum der Vernunft*, where the conditions are ‘inescapable *for us*’; a fourth time temporally, in Habermas’s gloss on what makes Putnam’s indispensability argument weak, transferred by the chapter to the presuppositions of argumentation, where what is claimed of a presupposition is not that it is ‘unrevisable or necessary, but that it is — for the time being — unavoidable’; and a fifth time where the demonstration is most exposed, what the weak transcendental argument establishes being ‘indeed conditional on the constancy of this life-form’. Indexed to a practice, to a practice without substitute, to a form of life, to a time, and to the constancy of that form of life, the predicate is executed across the dated series with a single exception, the sentence of 1991 adjudicated at §2.2.3; and a presupposition unavoidable for the time being is not a *Faktum* in Kant’s sense, which is indexed to no time.[^92]

Because modal status is to be adjudicated, the modal vocabulary is reserved. *Unvermeidlich*, unavoidable, is reserved to Habermas’s weaker modality; *unhintergehbar* and its cognates to the contested apodictic thesis of Apel and Kuhlmann, and none of them is used of Habermas’s position. The reservation is a rule of usage, and what it reserves is the unqualified predicate and not the word, since Habermas writes the word, with the qualifier *epistemisch*, in the sentence of 1999 quoted above, and his own formula recommends the rule: transcendental analysis, after the pragmatic deflation of the Kantian approach, seeks ‘presumptively universal, but only de facto inescapable, conditions’, every qualifier of which is a restriction. The claim on which the rule rests, that Habermas’s own predicate is, from 1976, everywhere the restricted one, is falsifiable, and the three candidates the note examines, of 1974, 1999 and 2019, each carry the first restriction, the earliest hedging its predicate besides and preceding the weakened conception it would falsify; none falsifies the rule.[^93] A second pair requires the same discipline: ‘quasi-transcendental’ and ‘weakly transcendental’ are not synonyms. The one occurrence of ‘quasi-transcendental necessity’ in Habermas’s own voice in the discourse-ethics volume renders *eine gleichsam transzendentale Nötigung*, an as-it-were transcendental necessitation, a hedge in the German having become a term of art in the English, and the hedge is no single occurrence: in 1974 Habermas assumes for every speech act ‘eine nicht hintergehbare, gleichsam transzendental nötigende Geltungsbasis’, in 1980 he holds that participants in discourse are ‘in einem gleichsam transzendentalen Sinne genötigt’ to suppose the demands of discourse sufficiently met, and in 1973 he had undertaken the burden of proof for the quasi-transcendental character he ascribed to the rule system of universal pragmatics. Where the word stands unhedged in his own voice it characterises a principle, an approach or a set of conditions, Davidson’s principle of charity having ‘a quasi-transcendental status’ and Peirce’s indefinite community supplying ‘the quasi-transcendental enabling conditions for criticism of claims to truth’; it is absent from *Between Facts and Norms*; and it grades a necessitation, in Cronin’s English, once, in the sentence of 2020.[^94] Apel uses the word in both directions, approvingly of Peirce and of the preunderstanding of the lifeworld, pejoratively of the *Tractatus*;[^95] in neither author does the word, where it stands beside a modal predicate, grade the modality without a hedge, save in the English of 2020, which the translation alone cannot settle. *Weakly transcendental* is accordingly written wherever the modality is predicated, while *weak transcendental argument* is retained as Habermas’s own name for the argument-type of Strawson’s kind, and the one sentence of 2020 in which the prefix bears argumentative weight is read at §2.2.3. What Kuhlmann states, and Apel presses with him in the passage Habermas displays in order to refuse it, is not a higher degree of certainty within the reconstructive mode but a claim that something falls outside it, placed by Apel among the conditions of the reconstructive sciences (§2.1.1), and taken up at Chapter Three §3.2.3.[^96] The modality so fixed is one term of a deficit not yet derived, designated in anticipation the *Begründungsdefizit*, the justificatory deficit. The word is Habermas’s twice over in the volume of 1983: of the shortfall which opens ‘from the perspective of transcendental philosophy’ when the transcendental philosopher, turned modest, tests his reconstructions against counterexamples and acts, in the English, ‘like a hypothesis-testing scientist’; and of the point at which Tugendhat’s chain of argument breaks off.[^97] The application of the word to both intervals §2.2.3 reaches, the first answering to the former use and the second to the latter, is the thesis’s.

### C3 · Fallibilism

C3 holds that the propositional yield of any reconstructive enterprise, formal pragmatics not excepted, remains in principle revisable. Its textual registration against Apel is the point at which Habermas confronts Apel’s demand: the presuppositions cannot be denied from within argumentation, since any attempt to deny them enacts them, yet the demonstration of this inescapability is not the justification of the presuppositions, what is proved being, in the sentence §2.1.2 displays, that the rules of argumentation have no alternatives, while the rules themselves are not thereby justified.[^98] The discrimination the chapter needs is carried in the sentence’s grammar, and the German is the safer ground for the first clause, the second being fixed by the sentence which follows it: what is there said to be proved is *die Alternativenlosigkeit dieser Regeln für die Argumentationspraxis* — a state of the rules, and so the sort of item a proposition states, and one already indexed to the practice of argumentation; what is said not to be justified is *diese selbst*, whose antecedent the next sentence, on the participants’ having accepted *diese Regeln* as a fact of reason, fixes as the rules themselves, as followed, and rules as followed are not items a proposition states. The two governing verbs are thus given objects of different categories within a single sentence, and upon that assignment the withholding is intelligible rather than merely stipulated, since justification is a relation in which a norm stands to grounds, and the demonstration that a norm has no available alternative furnishes no ground for the norm but reports upon its standing; the reading of the grammar is offered as a reading, and nothing in the argument requires that Habermas drew the distinction under those terms, only that the sentence he wrote observes it.

C3 is thus not the thesis that the conditions of rationality are uncertain, the misreading the language of hypothetical status invites, but the thesis that the philosopher’s articulation of those conditions is corrigible, even where the conditions themselves lie operatively beyond denial. Three items are run together in that misreading, and separating them fixes what the commitment concedes and what it does not: the standing of the conditions, which C3 leaves untouched; the undoing, on each occasion of denial, of the denial by its own performance, which occurs without the mediation of any formulation and is no item of knowledge, since the denier defeats himself whether or not a theorist has described what he denies, and any report of the undoing stands under the fallibility reports stand under; and the correctness of any given statement of their content, where alone fallibility is conceded. A reading of C3 conceding the first would leave the instrument of these chapters nothing to defend; one conceding the second would leave the exhibition nothing to exhibit; one declining the third would be *Letztbegründung* under another name. C3, so read, concedes the third alone.

An objection runs upon that concession: If the theorist’s reconstruction of the presuppositions is fallible, then the elenctic force of exhibiting a performative self-contradiction is fallible too, and the sceptic is not caught; the presupposition may be misidentified, described in terms the sceptic may reasonably reject, or mistaken for a parochial feature of one argumentative culture, and none of these possibilities is denied. The reply turns upon what the sceptic’s denial undoes. What it undoes is not a proposition of the theorist’s but the meaning of his own performance: he denies, in argument, a presupposition without which his denial would not be grasped as an argument, and the undoing occurs *in actu*, in the performance and for its performer, whether or not any theorist is present to describe it and whether or not the description, if offered, is correct. A better description would not make the undoing more secure, there being no degrees in a performance not performed; nor would a worse one make it less secure, since what a description is a description of is not brought into being by being described. The theorist’s failures are failures to identify which presupposition is at work, and not failures which restore a vacated position to the sceptic. The sceptic would have to show not that the theorist’s description is inaccurate, but that there is a way of arguing which does not undertake what the description was attempting to state, which is the possibility substep (2c) exists to test, and it concerns the extension of the presupposition and not the standing of the compulsion where the presupposition holds; a sceptic who produced such a case would have narrowed the claim, not shown that arguing without the presuppositions of argument is something one might do badly rather than something one does not do. Operative irrefutability does not, therefore, depend upon descriptive infallibility: the term is used of an enactment in the sense that no performed denial of it succeeds, and not in the sense in which a proposition resists refutation, refutation being, upon the criterion §2.2.2 states, an operation upon claims. The cognate objection against the asymmetry itself, that a distinction reachable only under corrigible description cannot be occupied, is met at Chapter Three §3.2.2. The concession this requires is nevertheless real, and it falls upon the argument of these chapters and not upon Habermas: what the argument establishes is negative, that descriptive fallibility does not defeat operative irrefutability, and it does not establish that operative irrefutability holds, nor what its title would be if it did, nor whether the exhibition supposed to display it succeeds in displaying anything. Those three questions are handed to Chapter Three §3.3, where the elenctic *Aufweis* is developed and the debt so entered is discharged or is not; and the deficit named at §2.2.3 does not depend upon the discharge, since its second interval would stand even if the *Aufweis* failed entirely.[^99]

The coherence of the programme has been contested from two opposed directions. The circularity charge holds that a reconstruction of the presuppositions of argumentation is itself an argument, and so presupposes what it sets out to exhibit; the concession is the reply, since the reconstruction *enacts* the presuppositions it articulates, and enactment is not entailment. The second charge runs in the contrary direction, holding the programme to be empiricism in transcendental dress, and is met by the demarcation of data; whether a restriction of that shape is principled or expedient is the question C3 must answer, and the answer is sharper than it appears, because the demand for a restriction is Apel’s and the *form* of the restriction is Apel’s too. The self-application of the fallibilism principle, he argues, leads to a paradox on the pattern of the liar: ‘If the “Fallibilism” principle is itself fallible, it is to just that extent not fallible and vice versa’; what the argument concludes is not that fallibilism is false but that the principle of fallibilism, and with it the principle of criticism it underwrites, are meaningful and valid only under a restriction entered at the outset, so that at least some evidence is withheld from possible criticism, namely the evidence upon which the principles are themselves grounded.[^100] A restriction is therefore required by both, so that the charge of *ad hoc* exemption cannot be levelled on the ground that a restriction is drawn at all, but only against the principle upon which each restriction is drawn. Apel withholds a class of *evidence*, ‘at least some philosophical evidence’ ‘excluded from possible criticism’ from the beginning. The withholding has two parts, and the reading given here keeps them apart: the certainty is placed first in the arguer’s performative self-knowledge, since, in a sentence of 1986, ‘I do not need to presuppose a fallible speech-act theory in order to know that I make an affirmation or put forward a question’, and it is then extended to the explication which states what is so known, so far as the explication is adequate.[^101] The first part is common ground with the position argued here; the disagreement is with the extension, which keeps the exempted item inside the category to which fallibility could intelligibly attach, and so owes an account of how a proposition comes to be immune.

The commitment is examined at length in the essay where Apel submits critical rationalism to metacritique, under a disclaimer which governs how the dispute must be read: his purpose is not to question the principle of critical examination but to examine the inner connection Albert asserts between the impossibility of a fundamental grounding and the programme of rational criticism, which the chapter takes in the form of a connection between criticism and unrestricted fallibilism.[^102] A discrimination follows which Apel’s own statement leaves implicit, and which is the chapter’s: the exemption the paradox forces need be no exemption from criticism, only from the assumption of possible falsity, which a defender of critical examination may withhold without withdrawing that principle.

What the metacritique targets is not the fallibilism C3 holds, and the point is documented on Habermas’s side before it is pressed from Apel’s. In 1983 Habermas records that Apel ‘has subjected fallibilism to an illuminating metacritique’, and he reconstructs the result himself: Apel ‘uncovers a performative contradiction in the objection raised by the “consistent fallibilist,”’ since ‘even the consistent fallibilist has already accepted as valid a minimum number of unavoidable rules of criticism’.[^103] The fallibilism C3 states is accordingly not the pan-critical fallibilism of Albert, Bartley and Radnitzky, which both parties reject in the same terms; it is the restriction of fallibility to ‘our reconstruction’, in the sentence displayed at §2.1.2, and what remains in dispute between them is whether that restriction is stable. Apel’s own line is drawn in 1975 and held thereafter: at the level of philosophical reflection one may formulate ‘a fallibilism proviso as virtually universal doubt with regard to the paradigmatic evidence of all possible language games, except for the philosophical language game of doubt’, and in 1992 the principle of fallibilism, which is said to hold ‘for all empirical and categorial knowledge-claims’, ‘reaches its limits, when we reflect on the necessary presuppositions of argumentative discourse’.[^104] The charge against Habermas is dated between 1986 and 1989. In a note of 1986 the thought of an empirical testing of the presuppositions is declared unintelligible, since any test would presuppose them, so that a correction of their explication ‘kann daher nur den Charakter einer Selbst-Korrektur haben’, can only have the character of a self-correction, and the radicality of the transcendental question is not to be relaxed ‘zugunsten halb empirischer und insofern schwacher transzendentaler Argumente’, in favour of half-empirical and to that extent weak transcendental arguments;[^105] in the same year the principle determining the rules of discourse is denied ‘the status of a discursive hypothesis — only provisionally without alternatives, but essentially fallible’, and Habermas is charged with ‘a false modesty, understandable indeed in view of dominant relativism and skepticism’;[^106] in 1989 the charge is generalised, Habermas having, ‘in spite of apparent reservations’, ‘*de facto* accepted the unrestricted and thus self-applicable principle of fallibilism held by the late Popperians’, the presuppositions being ‘necessary and universally valid only insofar as they have proven themselves up to now to be without alternatives’.[^107] The sharpest statement stands in a note of the same essay: the philosophical statements which explicate fallibilism can be corrected as explications, ‘But they are infallible precisely insofar as they state necessary presuppositions of the principle of fallibilism’; and of Habermas’s sentence of 1986 that ‘There are no meta-discourses in the sense that a higher discourse is able to prescribe rules for a subordinate discourse. Argumentation games do not form a hierarchy’, Apel asks: ‘Might he have noticed that this sentence claims exactly what he denies and thus that he falls into a performative self-contradiction?’[^108]

Habermas’s reply, in 1991, proceeds by redescription and not by refutation. Under Apel’s transformation of philosophy, transcendental reflection is said to uncover a realm of knowledge ‘which, itself infallible, accounts for the presuppositions of fallibilism and thereby satisfies the conditions of an ultimate grounding’; the talk of ultimate justification ‘suggests a residual foundationalism limited to the analysis of transcendental conditions’, and philosophy becomes ‘the final haven of certainties that are otherwise unavailable on the assumptions of a generalized fallibilism’ — in the German, ‘unter dem allgemein gewordenen Fallibilismusvorbehalt’, under a fallibilism proviso become general, the proviso being Apel’s word of 1975 before it is Habermas’s. The refusal rests on one ground, that ‘there is no hierarchical gradation between discourses and corresponding metadiscourses’, and it concludes that an ultimate justification of ethics ‘is neither possible nor necessary’.[^109] The ground is aimed at a hierarchy Apel had disclaimed in the sentence of 1975 quoted above, what he claims being that two classes of statement differ in principle and not that discourses form a ladder, so that the reply of 1991 meets the note of 1989 at one point only, the no-hierarchy sentence which the note charges with self-contradiction, and meets it by restating the sentence. The parties, both having restricted fallibility, divide over whether the restriction Habermas draws, to the reconstruction as against the practice reconstructed, survives the submission of universal-pragmatic statements to the two tests §2.1.2 examines. Apel’s answer is that it does not, since the tests presuppose what they would test, and the note of 1989 gives the answer its strongest form: a statement which explicates a necessary presupposition inherits the standing of what it explicates, and a fallibilism which withholds that standing from its own statements says of them what it denies of them. The answer C3 gives, on the reconstruction offered in this chapter, is that the inheritance is the point at issue and not a premise: a statement which explicates a presupposition is put up for discussion as any statement is, and what the exhibition of a denial’s self-defeat secures is the enactment and not the statement, on the categorial ground §2.2.2 supplies. Whether an explication reached by strict reflection can be exempted from the tests without being exempted from argument, which is what Apel’s ‘insofar as’ requires, is the transfer question of Chapter Three §3.2.3 and §3.3.3; the charge is entered as Apel states it, its target is the propositional statement of the refusal and not the refusal as enacted, and its adjudication is Chapter Three’s.

## §2.3 · Habermas’s Response, the Cost of the Categorial Premise, and the Aporia as Limit

### §2.3.1 · Self-reflexivity, and the three resources with their costs

The tensions the three commitments generate from within cannot be resolved while the programme is retained: a resolution reached by weakening any commitment would defend the programme by abandoning it (§2.2.3). If the commitments are to be retained, the tensions must be sustained, and the discipline of sustaining them — each objection met not by refutation but by a bounded concession whose boundary then determines the tension’s shape — is philosophical self-reflexivity, argument turned back upon the conditions of its own possibility. Two properties of different orders meet in that phrase: the self-referentiality of argumentation, a property of the object, since every argument instantiates the conditions under which arguments are made, and the self-reflexivity of philosophy, the consequence at the level of the discipline, since an enquiry whose object is the conditions of argument, and whose instrument is argument, has no exterior from which to specify what it studies. The warrant is Habermas’s own diagnosis of what the tradition had to surrender, which §2.1 expounds, and the consequence, which the chapter draws and he does not, is that the reversal of the gaze cannot be executed from outside: a discipline which has renounced the objectivating stance towards its own conditions cannot reoccupy that stance to describe its renunciation without withdrawing the renunciation, and a philosophy to which the lifeworld is present only as horizon (§2.1) has a position within the horizon and no vantage upon it. The discipline’s turn upon its own conditions must therefore itself be conducted from the participant’s perspective, and this is a constraint upon the argument conducted here and not merely a thesis it reports: a self-reflexivity which borrowed an observer’s standpoint would reoccupy, in its method, the position its own premise forecloses.

The first resource is the *Faktum der Vernunft*, the Kantian term Habermas borrows analogically for a compulsion given and not derived; it stands in his text concessively and by analogy, and the immediacy Henrich charged against the theory of communicative action (§2.1) has its address in this resource, a compulsion the programme admits it can clarify and not ground, so that the charge is met by a concession and not by a refutation; the use to which it is put, sustaining irrefutability at the operative register, is the chapter’s. The commitment §2.2.3 records, in the form C3 gives it, is this: whoever argues that no presupposition of argumentation is beyond denial defeats his own performance in the arguing, and the defeat occurs in the deed, whatever any theory has said of what he denies (C3), the licence for the result falling to Chapter Three. At MCCA 95 the demonstration is first delimited, in the sentence §2.1.2 displays; only then is the concession entered, concessively: ‘True, the participants must have accepted them as a “fact of reason” in setting out to argue.’ The denial follows adversatively: ‘But this kind of argument cannot accomplish a transcendental deduction in the Kantian sense.’[^110] The three moves are printed in that sequence, and the sequence carries the reading: the concession stands between the delimitation and the denial and names what the delimited demonstration presupposes and does not justify, the rules themselves, which the participants are said to have *accepted* as a fact of reason. The concession also narrows the object of that acceptance and fixes its mode. The sentence of Kuhlmann’s which Habermas has just displayed has participants who have always already accepted ‘the propositions and rules’ belonging to the presuppositions; his rejoinder, introduced in the German by a corrective which the translation softens to an equivalence, names the rules alone, and has them acknowledged solely by the participants’ taking up argument, a restriction carried in the German by *allein* and dropped by the translation.[^111] The acknowledgement is thereby placed in the deed and withheld from the propositions Kuhlmann had joined to the rules, and the sentence distributes its predicates across the categorial boundary §2.2.2 states without remarking on the alignment. The restriction is stated again thirty-five pages later, in the title essay of the same volume: the presuppositions ‘always already underlie the communicative practice of everyday life and that cannot be picked or chosen like makes of cars or value postulates’, and the fact of reason itself ‘cannot be deductively grounded, but it can be clarified’ by conceiving argumentative speech as a special case of action oriented to reaching understanding, and a privileged derivative of it.[^112] What the resource secures is accordingly indexed to performance from the outset. Its office against the sceptic is one thing; its office against an argument about the conditions of a sign’s recognisability is another, and the cost of the second is the amendment §2.3.2 makes to the individuation of the *Dass*.

The second resource is the confutative method itself, post-metaphysical philosophy’s characteristic *via negativa*, establishing results by exhibiting the self-defeat of denials rather than by deriving them from self-evident premises. It meets Apel where §2.2.1 leaves him standing. Apel does not dispute that the presuppositions bind; what he disputes is the status of the binding, and his charge, stated at Chapter One §1.6.3 and expounded by Kettner, is that Habermas can decline strictly reflexive arguments only at the price of inconsistency. What his objection reaches, on the chapter’s reconstruction from that intervention and Kettner’s exposition of it, is the reach the programme claims for the method’s results. The confutative exhibition operates within discourse: its verdicts fall upon performances of denial, and a performance of denial is available only where someone argues. The priority of communicative over strategic action in its general form, which Chapter One §1.4.1 bounds, is a thesis about language at large, and the openly strategic actor, using language without even a pretence of an orientation to reaching understanding, performs no denial within discourse upon which the exhibition could operate; Habermas’s own answer to the actor who declines communicative action, the factual observation Chapter One §1.6.2 records him offering in place of a conceptual argument, concedes that what closes that exit is not the exhibition. The denier of the priority is another matter, since whoever denies it in argument argues; what his defeat secures, upon the categorial premise §2.2.2 states, is the operative result and not the proposition he denies. This is the point beyond which, on Apel’s conclusion as Chapter One §1.6.3 displays it, universal pragmatics on its own cannot reach, the strict reflection that conclusion requires being conducted, in Kettner’s exposition, from within argumentative discourse, and by an argument Habermas’s method does not employ.[^113] The refusal carries a cost. The priority thesis stands, on Apel’s own terms, undischarged by confutation alone, a debt which recurs wherever the *Selbsteinholungsprinzip* requires a reconstruction to render its own possibility intelligible as an outcome of what it reconstructs.[^114]

Apel does not situate the boundary of that compulsion where Habermas situates it, and the difference concerns where the floor lies, which presuppositions still bind a speaker who has left argumentation. He holds the presupposition of an unlimited communication community to be undertaken not in argumentation alone but ‘in any argument, indeed in any human word’, and, in his own qualification, ‘with any action that is to be intelligible’.[^115] On that account the openly strategic actor leaves argumentation and remains bound by its presuppositions, and the debt just entered would be discharged by the wider scope rather than owed. Where Habermas places the floor is reported at §2.3.3 from MCCA 99–101 and displayed at Chapter One §1.6.2; which of the two boundaries holds is a question Chapter Three states at §3.3.6 and declares open there. Neither the reply §2.3.2 gives to Derrida’s form of the charge, which rests upon a thin requirement of recognisability, nor the parasitism thesis as Chapter One §1.4.1 bounds it, which establishes a dependence case by case, depends upon the debt: what Apel’s objection leaves undischarged is the general priority of the communicative over the strategic, which neither of them asserts or needs. Nor is the debt one-sided, since Apel discharges the office by a reflection whose availability is the matter in dispute between the programmes (§2.2.3); a debt which one party cannot discharge and the other discharges by a means the first does not accept as discharging it marks a disagreement about what discharge would be.

The third resource answers the objection, stated at §2.1.5, which Habermas enters against his own programme in the first volume of *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie*.[^116] It answers it by a shift Habermas states in his own words, from the validity to the genesis of convictions, the genesis being itself understood as rational: only the patterns of argument formed in learning processes which can be rationally retraced (*rational nachvollziehbar*) need, he writes, no further justification, and the steps must have appeared as learning to the participants themselves, and not only in retrospect. The genealogy is, on the chapter’s reading, where the programme meets the requirement Apel names the *Selbsteinholungsprinzip*, that a reconstruction render its own possibility intelligible as a result of what it reconstructs (C2, §2.2.3), and it meets it as a genealogy of learning and not by a reflection. The proviso is entered in Habermas’s own voice: a genealogical consideration of learning processes would be a contradiction in itself, he writes, were it not framed so that the independence of the validity of statements from their genesis can be preserved at the logical-semantic level.[^117] The resource vindicates, by the chapter’s account and not by Habermas’s, a practice’s acquisition and the adequacy of its self-understanding, and not the validity of its statements, which the proviso holds apart from their genesis at the logical-semantic level; what Habermas claims for the rationally retraced acquisition is that it frees the presuppositions of the modern understanding of self and world from a contingency otherwise asserted or denounced,[^118] which is the removal of an objection to their validity and not the supply of a ground for it, and the limitation is legible in the shape of the move itself, which answers a genealogical question with a solution no longer genealogical in the sense that posed it. The taxonomy under which the move is made is threefold — subversive, problematising, vindicatory — and the move Habermas makes is of the third kind, against a charge of the first; the author to whom Habermas credits the taxonomy advances it against the genealogy he undertakes, and not as an instrument offered to it, a contest recorded and not entered.[^119]

### §2.3.2 · The constancy requirement, and the concession it forces

Derrida’s charge, taken at the point where it bears upon the requirement the exhibition makes on its own behalf, is not the general charge against presence which §2.1.5 states and §2.2.2 answers. It is a specific objection to a specific requirement: that participants in an exchange use expressions with constant meaning. The requirement can be found in the catalogue of rules of argumentation Habermas adopts, at the logical-semantic level, as rule (1.3): ‘Different speakers may not use the same expression with different meanings.’ It cannot be staged upon that rule, and the reason is on the same page. Habermas takes the catalogue over from Alexy ‘for simplicity’, and in the sentences immediately following the rule he sets the whole level aside: the presuppositions of argumentation at this level ‘are logical and semantic rules that have no ethical content. They are not a suitable point of departure for a transcendental-pragmatic argument.’[^120] A concession extracted from rule (1.3) would therefore be extracted from a requirement the programme has declined as a point of departure for the argument to (U), on the ground that it has no ethical content. What the objection reaches is not the catalogue but the exhibition. For a performative self-contradiction to be exhibited at all, the denial a speaker performs must be recognisable, by him and by his interlocutor, as the denial of the presupposition his denying enacts. The recognisability so described is a requirement of constancy which the *Aufweis* makes on its own behalf, whatever the catalogue says, and which Chapter Three §3.2.2 receives in the form this section gives it. How much constancy it requires is not settled by stating it: an identity of content holding across every context of use would answer to it, and so would the weaker condition that a denier and his interlocutor take the expression of the denial for the expression his performance presupposes. It is that requirement, and the question of its strength, which Derrida’s argument tests.

Derrida’s argument against it begins from the mark, which is what it is only in being repeatable: a written communication ‘must be repeatable — iterable — in the absolute absence of the addressee or of the empirically determinable set of addressees’, and a mark ‘is constituted, in its identity as a mark, by its iterability in the absence of whoever, and therefore ultimately in the absence of every empirically determinable “subject.”’ The argument is generalised from writing to every element of spoken language: the unity of a signifying form ‘is constituted only by its iterability, by the possibility of being repeated in the absence not only of its referent, which goes without saying, but of a determined signified or current intention of signification’, and ‘given this structure of iteration, the intention which animates utterance will never be completely present in itself and its content’.[^121] Derrida’s target is Austin’s exclusion of the non-serious, the uses of language Austin sets aside as ‘not seriously’ meant and ‘in ways parasitic upon its normal use — ways which fall under the doctrine of the etiolations of language’, and his complaint is that what is thereby excluded is what Austin ‘nevertheless recognizes as the possibility to which every utterance is open’. The conclusion is drawn on the side of success and not of failure: ‘a successful performative is necessarily an “impure” performative’, and the ‘relative purity’ Austin allows ‘is not constructed against citationality or iterability, but against other kinds of iteration within a general iterability’. In place of the exclusion he proposes ‘a differential typology of forms of iteration’, within which ‘the category of intention will not disappear; it will have its place, but from this place it will no longer be able to govern the entire scene and the entire system of utterances’.[^122] Near its close the essay enters a disclaimer: Derrida will not conclude that ‘there is no effect of the performative, no effect of ordinary language, no effect of presence and of speech acts’; these effects ‘do not exclude what is generally opposed to them term by term, but on the contrary presuppose it … as the general space of their possibility’.[^123]

Habermas contested the argument in print, in the form Culler gives it. In the excursus on the levelling of the genre distinction between philosophy and literature he replies that Derrida ‘obviously already presupposes what he wants to prove: that any convention which permits the repetition of exemplary actions possesses from the outset not only a symbolic, but also a fictional character’. ‘Only the actually performed speech act is effective as action; the promise mentioned or reported in a quote depends grammatically upon this’; Derrida ‘makes no attempt to “deconstruct” this distinctive functional mode of ordinary speech within communicative action’; and the illocutionary binding force Austin discovered places normal speech ‘under constraints different from those of fictional discourse, simulation, and interior monologue’, constraints which ‘define the domain of “normal” language’. The burden he lays upon Derrida is stated in one sentence: ‘it must first be shown that the conventions of a game are ultimately indistinguishable from norms of action’.[^124] The reply is considered and is not empty, though the note records what it engages and what it does not; it does not restore what the objection removes. What it defends is the asymmetry of the ordinary and the parasitic, the priority thesis, whose separate cost §2.3.1 has entered on Apel’s charge; and where the excursus goes on to the second argument Culler draws from Derrida, that meaning varies with context, it answers with idealisations, the constraints which define normal speech being analysable, in the sentence which follows, as ‘the kinds of idealizing suppositions we have to make in communicative action’. The objection targets identity of meaning across contexts, and neither a defence of differential constraints upon use nor an idealising supposition of shared meaning delivers identity of content across uses; the second concedes that the identity is presupposed and not given (Chapter One §1.3.2), which is what the concession below keeps. The two exchanges pass each other, since nothing in Habermas’s reply claims the context-invariance a thick constancy requirement would need.

The concession this forces is prepared by a sentence Habermas writes against Apel, in the reply of 2003, and is not made by him. There he records that the universal validity of (U) is ‘derived’, the verb standing in his own quotation marks, from the content of the presuppositions of argumentation, and, as Chapter Three §3.2.3 displays, that in this he follows Apel’s nondeductive model of justification by the uncovering of a sceptic’s performative contradictions; and he locates the disagreement in one sentence: ‘The point of contention between Apel and me is not this justification move but its status in a nonfoundational game of justification.’ Nine pages later the status is fixed. The transcendental-pragmatic argument, to which Apel accords the role of an ultimate justification, ‘has in reality the status of a presumptively infallible, and at any rate discursively nontestable, reassurance. If it were an argument, it would be located in a linguistic context that offers as many opportunities for criticism as it has facets.’[^125] What Habermas denies the transcendental-pragmatic argument, so long as it is accorded Apel’s role, is the standing of an argument at all: what remains is a reassurance, presumptively infallible and discursively untestable, and no such reassurance was claimed for the exhibition in these pages. The conditional which follows states what is kept below, that were it an argument it would stand in a linguistic context and open to criticism at every facet. The sentence is directed at Apel and says nothing of Derrida, and the step from it to what Derrida’s argument needs, that an exhibition conducted in argument is conducted in iterable marks and nowhere else, is the chapter’s and not his.

The categorial premise of §2.2.2 cannot then be defended unchanged, and does not need to be abandoned; it needs the downgrade §2.1.4 recorded at the modal register, from an unconditional to an indexed necessity, applied now to meaning itself. The premise as §2.2.2 stated it was silent upon how finely the *Dass* is individuated across exchanges, and the argument had so far proceeded, wherever it spoke of the type of which a performed denial is the token, upon the reading on which the item enacted is identical in content wherever it is enacted; it is that reading, and not the criterion of individuation, which is surrendered. What the *Aufweis* requires from constancy of meaning is thin: that the content of a denial be recognisable, within a given exchange, as of the same citational type as what the denier’s performance enacts, sufficient for the miscarriage of denying-while-enacting to register between these interlocutors, here. The requirement is participant-indexed. A prohibition upon different speakers using the same expression with different meanings, read at face value, is thicker: a demand for a stable identity of meaning across speakers, which is what iterability puts out of reach, the identity it constitutes being that of the mark and not of a signified stable across its uses. The deployments of the instrument in this chapter — the analysis of denial at §2.2.2, the confinement of Stroud’s objection at C2, the reply to the fallibility objection at C3 — draw upon no more than the thin requirement, since each concerns what a denial performed within one exchange undoes, and none what is identical across exchanges. Nor is the thin requirement one Derrida’s argument excludes: a recognisability sufficient for incoherence to register is one of the ‘forms of iteration’ which the differential typology he proposes would have to sort, a typology whose tenability as an exhaustive programme he expressly holds off from affirming, and the place he assigns to intention within it, from which intention no longer governs the entire scene, is a place the thin reading can leave it, since the thin reading asks of intention nothing the typology denies it.

The individuation of the *Dass* is thus amended, and the premise stands: the *Dass* is individuated no more finely than by the citational recognisability sufficient for incoherence to be exhibited, and not by a self-identical meaning surviving the exchange intact. What §2.2.2 fixed — that the *Dass* is individuated by what its denial, performed, would undo — is unaltered; the amendment fixes the reach of the undoing, which is the exchange in which the denial is performed. The cost is that a thin, citational *Dass* is individuated only relative to a given exchange, and nothing guarantees its stability beyond it; the interval between what is operatively secured and what any reconstruction can state opens earlier than the thick reading suggested, though it does not close. The two intervals §2.2.3 reaches are reached with the amended premise and not the thick one: the first premise of that account is the programme’s commitment to an irrefutability Habermas himself indexes, and an individuation relative to an exchange is an index of the same form, applied to the identity of what is enacted and not to the necessity with which it binds. What the amendment alters is the width of the interval and not its existence. Derrida himself later supplies the nearest counterpart to the illocutionary bond, in the sworn faith of 1996, a pledge of promising at work in every address and guaranteed by nothing, to which the thin *Dass* stands nearer than the thick reading stood; what separates the two, that the pledge is held answerable in these chapters to the practice of giving and asking for reasons and by Derrida to nothing that practice could supply, is drawn in the note and recurs at §2.3.3 as the difference between an exhibition and a founding.[^126] What is owed is the passage from an individuation bounded by the exchange to an irrefutability which no exchange bounds, that is, whether exchange-relative recognisability can carry irrefutability *simpliciter* or only irrefutability within this exchange, a question the amendment states more sharply than the unamended premise could; Chapter Three receives it at §3.2.2 and attempts the passage at §3.3.2, by the retorsion — the turning of a denial upon its own performance — and within the limit declared there.

One consequence of the amendment strengthens an objection which the chapter frames in the terms of Wellmer’s critique of the idealisations, and does not attribute to him in this form: that the presuppositions of argumentation are counterfactual idealisations which participants project, and not conditions a performance enacts. On that objection a *Dass* individuated thinly is harder to distinguish from a projected idealisation than a thickly individuated one was. The distinction nevertheless holds, by the criterion §2.2.2 fixed: the *Dass* is individuated by what its performed denial would undo. An idealisation’s fulfilment can be denied without self-undoing, since a participant who says that the conditions of this exchange fall short of the ideal still argues; what cannot be denied without self-undoing is the anticipation itself, and the anticipation is then no projection at a participant’s disposal but a condition the performance enacts. A condition enacted in the performance is not given up but attempted against, and the attempt undoes itself. Thinning the individuation alters how widely the undoing reaches, within this exchange rather than across all exchanges, and alters nothing about whether there is an undoing to be had; what the amendment surrenders is scope, and not standing.

### §2.3.3 · Internal critique, its outside, and the decisionist challenge

A further thesis stands upon the second resource and upon the record of the architecture’s amendments: that standing critiques of the architecture are *internal* to the self-reflexive practice, amendments within it and not defeaters of it. The thesis has two components, differently secured, and the charge that it converts the programme into an unfalsifiable dogma is answered by keeping them apart. The categorial component, that a critique advanced in discourse enacts whatever conditions argument has, is secured performatively on each occasion of its advancing, and no case history defeats it there; which conditions those are is a reconstruction, and a case can narrow it. The historical component, that the architecture in fact answers such critiques by amendment rather than by relabelling, is falsifiable in the ordinary way, since a single sustained objection met by relabelling would defeat it; it is illustrated by one instance, the retraction of the consensus-theoretic conception of truth, which Habermas himself attributes to the pressure of named critics (Chapter One §1.5.6); that the retraction is an amendment and not a relabelling is an interpretive construal, and the component is carried no further than the instances examined carry it.[^127] A charge of unfalsifiability pressed against the instrument rather than the thesis is met on firmer ground: §2.2.2 states two determinate conditions under which the categorial premise would be defeated, both checkable by anyone, and the instrument, turned upon the premise of the argument deploying it at §2.3.2, returned the verdict that the premise could not be defended unchanged and cost the *Dass* its thick individuation, a cost which distinguishes an amendment from a re-description. The programme’s own self-understanding supports the operative–propositional asymmetry so far as certainty is concerned, and in Habermas’s own voice, in the sentence §2.1.2 displays in which he withholds from ‘our reconstruction’ of pretheoretical knowledge the non-fallibility he grants, in a certain sense, to the intuitive knowledge of rules.[^128] The thesis has, lastly, a determinate outside, which is named: the decisionist’s refusal to argue, which is not an objection raised in discourse and falls under neither component.

The tension of the standpoint takes a further form: not an objection which would ground communicative reason in something further, but a challenge whose content assigns the standing of any ground to a decision, and Apel’s own texts supply its shape. Fundamental evidence, on the position he reports and rejects, ‘can neither be denied without self-contradiction nor be grounded without *petitio principii*. Therefore, it is said, the *fundamental-grounding must be superseded by a fundamental-decision*’ — a solution he ascribes to Stegmüller and to Popper, and which ‘clearly corresponds again to the purely logically deduced “Münchhausen-Trilemma” of fundamental-grounding in the sense of Hans Albert’.[^129] Against a challenge of this shape no demonstration is available, and the unavailability is principled, since a demonstration would presuppose, as the court before which its own case is tried, the practice whose title is disputed. What can be exhibited is the challenge’s own performance: raised as a claim addressed to interlocutors, it enacts in the address the conditions of claim-raising its content denies. Reciprocity of address is all that is claimed for the enactment here, in the measure Chapter Three §3.3.1 allows the exhibition, and nothing of the symmetry or equal entitlement that chapter withholds from it; this secures a compulsion and no refutation, and forecloses the challenge only as something arguable. The decisionist position licenses a reply which changes the dispute’s shape: not to deny that arguing enacts the presuppositions, but to exit the practice, refusing to argue and demonstrating in conduct what could not be asserted in speech.

At this point the parties divide, and not where a summary would place the division. Habermas concedes the exit from argumentation: the consistent sceptic who will not enter into argumentation leaves the transcendental-pragmatic argument without purchase and voluntarily quits the community of those who argue; the concession is at once bounded, since the sceptic remains bound, in the everyday communicative practice he cannot leave, to presuppositions he has not shaken off. Apel, in his own earlier voice, denies the exit from argumentation within a stated bound: the defence of irrationalism refutes, by the performative act which accompanies it, the attempt to refuse argumentation, and one who decides to leave presupposes the principle he has denied so long as he takes his leaving for a decision. Both passages are displayed at Chapter One §1.6.2, which also reads Apel’s later reply to Habermas, and the dropout, the sceptic who quits argumentation, is examined as the figure in which the question of the exit reaches the legal-political architecture at Chapter Four §4.5.1. The parties therefore agree that some floor admits of no exit, and divide over where it lies, at argumentation as such or at the everyday practice of communication beneath it: Habermas lets the sceptic leave the community of arguers and holds him within the lifeworld, and Apel holds that the one who leaves presupposes, in understanding his leaving as a decision, what he would quit. The division is one of scope in the placing of the floor, and not the categorial difference C3 locates in the kind of item each party exempts; and the two axes cross.[^130]

The challenge has a form stronger than either party’s statement of it, and Derrida supplies it where he asks what founds the authority of law. The founding and justifying moment which institutes law implies, he writes, ‘a performative force, which is always an interpretative force’; the operation which amounts to founding law ‘would consist of a coup de force, of a performative and therefore interpretative violence that in itself is neither just nor unjust and that no justice and no previous law with its founding anterior moment could guarantee or contradict or invalidate’. The challenge can be turned upon the exhibition, though Derrida’s target is the founding of law: ‘No justificatory discourse could or should insure the role of metalanguage in relation to the performativity of institutive language or to its dominant interpretation.’ Since the origin of authority ‘can’t by definition rest on anything but themselves’, the founding acts ‘are themselves a violence without ground’, ‘neither legal nor illegal in their founding moment’, and they ‘exceed the opposition between founded and unfounded, or between any foundationalism or anti-foundationalism’.[^131] Read in the terms of this section, this is the decisionist’s reply raised to a structural claim: not that a sceptic may leave the practice, but that the practice itself rests upon an act which no discourse conducted within it can adjudicate, and that the same limit ‘will reappear at the supposed origin’ of whatever conditions or conventions the practice invokes.

The reply available is bounded, and its boundary is the one already drawn. What Derrida’s sentence denies to any justificatory discourse, the office of a metalanguage over the performativity which institutes a practice, is nothing the exhibition claims: the exhibition grounds nothing, displays within an exchange that a denial performed there defeats itself, and forecloses the exit only as something arguable, the concession already made to the decisionist. Where his argument reaches past that concession is at the founding of the practice, and there no verdict is entered: a verdict upon the founding would have to be returned from the standpoint §2.3.1 forecloses, and one returned from within would be a justificatory discourse of the kind his sentence excludes. The founding’s being uncertifiable from within does not show that there is nothing to certify, a distinction the Introduction draws and this chapter observes. What Derrida calls the mystical limit is accordingly accepted as a limit upon certification and not as a finding that there is nothing to certify; it is the place at which *the legitimacy question*, whether philosophical discourse, confutative, self-referential and without grounding external to itself, is entitled to the verdicts it returns upon its own conditions, is put in its strongest form, whether a discourse without exterior is entitled to declare an exit foreclosed, and the question is handed on in that form. For Derrida the limit is a limit of justice, ‘an experience of the impossible’, at which ‘the decision between just and unjust is never insured by a rule’; the limit reached in these chapters is one of grounding, and §2.3.4 draws the distinction.[^132] Habermas’s own answer to the paradox of inauguration is examined at Chapter Four §4.3.4.

Whether the floor holds where Apel places it or where Habermas does governs where the deficit named at §2.2.3 has its practical face: if the exit is available, between the compulsion binding the participant and the *Sollgeltung* the agent owes; if it is not, between a compulsion reaching the agent as such and any statement of what binds him. Both outcomes locate one structural finding, that modal status does not pass between the registers, and neither refutes nor vindicates it. What the placing of the floor adds, and Chapter Three §3.3.6 and Chapter Four §4.5.1 record, is that the deficit’s practical face rests upon Habermas’s own concession of the exit, and is therefore charged to his architecture rather than entered as a neutral datum so long as the second-stage question, whether the norms of reciprocity are among the conditions of determinate signification (Chapter Three §3.3.6), is open; were Apel’s floor to hold, the finding would be charged to no party’s architecture in particular, the compulsion then reaching the agent as such.

Adjudicating between them is the recurrence the resources of this chapter generate, the legitimacy question named above. It has arisen at four points in this chapter, in the definition-or-description dilemma of §2.1.5, in the framing objection to the yield-question at §2.2.1, in the third of the questions C2 puts to the displacement of the solitary subject, and at the boundary of internal critique drawn in this section, where it takes its sharpest form, whether such a discourse may declare an exit foreclosed. Its adjudication is the Apel–Habermas dispute over the presuppositions’ irrefutability, which Chapter Three conducts.

### §2.3.4 · Two circularities, and the aporia as limit

The instrument used throughout, the operative–propositional asymmetry, sorts a family of circularities recurring at different levels and depths, and the sorting is itself an exercise of the self-reflexivity §2.3.1 named: a method under which every circularity charged against it is found benign owes a demonstration that the verdicts are reached by argument and not by definition, and the sorting supplies it only where it shows that the item charged is an exhibition, and not merely that an exhibition would be benign. Two circularities are distinct, and five formulations reduce to them. The first is the reconstructive circle, charged at three levels. At the level of the object it is Apel’s charge that the empirical testing of the presuppositions must already presuppose their validity in order to understand what such testing would be (C1); at the level of the discipline it is the charge C2 states in the genealogist’s name and in a formulation which is no one critic’s, that the philosopher’s self-description as reconstructor is itself a move within the practice reconstructed; at the level of the architecture it is a formulation, likewise no critic’s, on which the reflexive claim is warranted by the practice it examines. It is benign at each level: a reconstruction which enacts the presuppositions it articulates has inferred nothing from them, enactment being not entailment (C3); the regress the second level alleges is one of assertions while the irrefutability is operative, on the ground Chapter Three §3.3.3 supplies; and the warrant the third level alleges is exhibited in the practice and not inferred from it.[^133] The second is the circle of the test, charged in two adjacent forms: the hermeneutic form, the immanent test moving within the circle it tests, which no critic presses in these terms and whose sufficiency-debt is met where Chapter Three §3.1 absorbs the hermeneutic-priority objection Kompridis presses; and Wellmer’s justificatory form, which he names in one sentence and does not pursue, and whose shape Habermas concedes of his own derivation of 1983, recording there a *petitio principii* (Chapter One §1.5.3): that the central content of the principle of universalisation is introduced into its derivation by a supplementary semantic premise, so that the justification presupposes its own result;[^134] generalised, the charge reaches any justification of that shape. Read as a derivation the step invites the charge, as Habermas’s own admission for 1983 shows; read as an exhibition it derives nothing, which is the deficit this chapter reaches and Chapter Four adjudicates. The two charges answer to two construals of one step, and Habermas’s own description of the move as nondeductive, with ‘derived’ in his quotation marks (§2.3.2), assigns it to the second. In both circles the circularity is vicious only if the exhibition is misread as a grounding; the showing that the item charged is an exhibition is made in this chapter for the first circle at the level of the object and for the second in Wellmer’s form, and rests for the remainder upon results Chapter Three §3.1, §3.2.3 and §3.3.3 supply.

A circularity is a relation of presupposition, a procedure turning upon a condition it also delivers, and the question it raises is whether the turning is vicious. The aporia of the meaning of truth is a relation of orientation: the practice is directed towards something which, being by hypothesis beyond every context of justification, it cannot deliver, and no reading of the direction as exhibition rather than grounding relieves it, because what is at issue is not how the practice warrants its conditions but that it reaches beyond every warrant it can give. This is the form in which the residue §2.1 recorded survives the renunciations: the moment of unconditionality is preserved, on Habermas’s own account, at most as a critical procedure, and a procedure can test every claim to the validity it intends without being able to deliver that validity, so that the transcendental illusion whose shadow he sees still falling upon communicative reason is cast where the orientation outruns the procedure. The aporia is accordingly not a third circularity, and the difference is categorial rather than one of degree, anticipated by the confinement’s own disanalogy at C2 and resting upon the distinction just drawn, which the argument enters as its own and derives from no further result. The criterion sorting the circularities accordingly has no jurisdiction here, on its own terms and not by an external rule, since a criterion built to ask whether presupposition is misread as grounding has nothing to say about an orientation which outruns every warrant the practice can give. Habermas’s own answer, which lodges the justification-transcendent reference of truth in the certainties of action and returns discursively vindicated beliefs to it, is examined at Chapter Three §3.4.2–§3.4.4.

The word carries two other senses in the traditions drawn upon, and its own sense is fixed against both. For Aristotle the difficulty of thought points to a knot in its object, to be surveyed before it can be untied, in the methodological doctrine of *Metaphysics* Β.1 which Chapter Three §3.3.1 displays; for Derrida the word names a nonpassage, ‘the experience of what happens and is fascinating in this nonpassage, paralyzing us in this separation in a way that is not necessarily negative’, distinguished expressly from an apparent antinomy, from a dialectisable contradiction and from a transcendental illusion.[^135] The aporia of this chapter is neither: not a knot, since the orientation to a context-transcending validity is constitutive of the practice and would survive any resolution of the difficulties it generates, so that a survey reports it and unties nothing; and not a nonpassage endured, since a speaker who claims truth meets no obstacle in the claiming. The word is declined in one sense, endurance as a subject’s experience of an obstacle; the sense in which the Conclusion speaks of the aporia as endured, as the form of a result which admits of no transition, is another, and is not declined. The limit is visible in the reflexive turn alone, to one who makes, from within the practice, the turn §2.1.1 assigns to the interpreter, no longer looking through what is said to the world but upon the rules by which the speaking proceeds, and from no standpoint outside the practice, which §2.3.1 has foreclosed; it belongs to one competence turned upon itself and not to a second person, and the answerability §2.1.1 fixes runs in one direction still. The aporia so reached is a limit of grounding in a sense kept apart from a deficit: not a warrant the practice owes and withholds, which is the condition Chapter Four §4.1.4 sets upon a deficit, but the determinate terminus which fixes what the practice’s orientation intends, exhibited within the practice, grounding nothing and, in the sense just declined, endured by no one. One formulation is nevertheless taken from Derrida, and declared taken: among the places of aporia he lists is iterability, glossed as ‘the conditions of possibility as conditions of impossibility’.[^136] The gloss names the shape of the aporia of truth: the orientation to a validity which transcends every context is the condition of there being criticisable claims at all, since, upon the cautionary use of ‘true’ which Chapter Three §3.4.4 defends against the inferentialist, a claim which could not outrun the agreement of the practice as a whole, however far pursued, could not be mistaken in the sense that use requires, and the same orientation is the condition of the claim’s never being discharged by any agreement reached within the practice, since what outruns every agreement cannot be discharged by one. The shape is taken and not the doctrine: Derrida’s aporia is the condition of responsibility and decision, ‘a sort of nonpassive endurance’; the aporia entered here is the condition of a practice’s being answerable to something it cannot supply.

This is the terminus the resources of this chapter reach by a route other than the semantic one of Chapter Three §3.3.4 and §3.4.4. The semantic route brings to its sharpest form a pressure upon whether the aporia is intelligible at all, a pressure which falls upon the aporia and not upon the instrument by which it is reached, and which Chapter Three discharges, at §3.4.4, only upon the strong reading of the validity claim to intelligibility for which §3.3.2 argues without establishing it beyond contest. All three resources examined in §2.3.1 presuppose an answer to a question none of them poses: the *Faktum* secures a fact of reason and says nothing about what makes a claim true, Habermas’s own disjunction at MCCA 130 offering clarification where grounding is refused, and clarification of a practice is not an account of what the practice answers to; the confutative method exhibits self-defeat and is silent upon what a defeated claim was defeated against; the genealogy accounts for the acquisition of convictions and refuses, by its own proviso, to speak to their validity. Two routes, distinct in their instruments though sharing the categorial premise of §2.2.2, one through semantics and one through the three instruments of self-reflexivity examined at §2.3.1, arrive at what is argued to be the same limit, which is evidence, so far as two routes sharing a premise can supply it, that the limit is not an artefact of the instruments proper to either; a third diagnosis, Levine’s, which the chapter maps onto the junction of C2 and C3, corroborates without carrying anything.[^137]

## §2.4 · The Cost Stated, and What Is Handed On

What has been shown is that the method by which formal pragmatics is reached is silent upon the modality of what it reconstructs, and that the silence is the cost of three commitments each separately defensible (§2.1); that the three are co-original, presupposing one another without derivation and forming neither a circle of entailments nor a chance conjunction, the space the entailment-reading denies being Apel’s (§2.2.1); that the categorial premise on which the argument rests is stated with its two consequences and declared falsifiable under stated conditions, its ground drawn from the behaviour of denial and the criterion of individuation argued at Chapter Three §3.2.1 (§2.2.2); that the three, held together, generate three tensions, one for each commitment, each reached in its gap, two of them in the intervals of the deficit and none resolved by weakening a commitment without surrendering the programme (§2.2.3); that each of the three resources with which Habermas sustains the tensions carries a determinate cost, that the premise survives Derrida’s argument amended and not defended, that critiques of the architecture fall within the self-reflexive practice on the categorial component, and are amendments within it on the historical component so far as the one instance examined reaches, while the decisionist’s refusal to argue stands outside it, and that the circularities charged against the programme are benign where exhibition is not mistaken for grounding (§2.3.1–§2.3.4); and that what remains beyond them is an aporia, in which the practice is oriented to a validity it cannot discharge, which none of the three resources addresses, and whose own intelligibility as an aporia rests upon Chapter Three’s strong reading of the claim to intelligibility, argued at §3.3.2 and left contestable (§2.3.4). Two things are kept apart in the claim: the structural finding, that no modal status passes between the registers, which is neutral between the parties; and its practical face, which rests upon Habermas’s concession of the exit and is charged to his architecture while the second-stage question is open (§2.3.3). The response available to the programme, as the chapter reconstructs it, is therefore real and is not a dissolution. It costs something statable, the enactment’s individuation narrowed from thick to thin, a cost borne by the instrument these chapters use on the programme’s behalf, and it relocates the tension rather than closing it; a programme which holds the three commitments on purpose cannot meet the cost of the tensions by weakening any of them without ceasing to be the programme, and can only sustain them, conducting its self-reflection from the participant’s perspective and declaring at each station what has been conceded.

What is handed on goes to three destinations. To Chapter Three go the first interval and the legitimacy question: the interval is the tension of the relation stated at §2.2.3, closed on both routes reconstruction commands and open, if at all, to the access the counter-programme claims at the reflexive face of the performative; the legitimacy question, whether a discourse which has no exterior may declare an exit foreclosed, is sharpened at §2.3.3 by the form Derrida gives it and taken up at Chapter Three §3.3.6 as the second-stage question, together with the question §2.3.2 narrowed, whether recognisability relative to an exchange can carry irrefutability beyond it, received at Chapter Three §3.2.2 and answered at §3.3.2 within the limit declared there; the defence of the categorial premise against the inferentialist and the meaning-critical objections, in the individuation §2.3.2 amends, goes with them. To Chapter Four go the deficit in its second interval, reached at §2.2.3 and inscribed in Habermas’s own statement of 2020, the objections to it, and the dropout, whose two readings §2.3.3 has set against each other; the question which follows is where, if not in the presuppositions of argument, the bindingness of a norm is constituted, and whether the architecture built upon the discourse principle in *Faktizität und Geltung* supplies from the legal form what the presuppositions cannot supply from themselves. To Chapter Three §3.4, and from there to the Conclusion, goes the aporia.

The chapter has not claimed that the presuppositions of argumentation are contingent, nor that they are necessary, but that reconstruction cannot tell which and that the counter-programme’s claim to tell rests upon a reflection which, if it is available at all, is not available to reconstruction; not that discourse ethics fails, but that its programme of justification requires a passage its infrastructure cannot make, a requirement Chapter One located and whose two intervals §2.2.3 reaches, the first adjudicated in Chapter Three and the second in Chapter Four; not that Apel’s route succeeds where Habermas’s stops, but that it answers the yield-question by an access whose existence Chapter Three adjudicates; not that Derrida’s argument is answered, but that it reaches a requirement the exhibition makes on its own behalf and that the premise survives the concession it forces at a stated cost; and not that the tensions, or the deficit they name, are a defect of Habermas’s theory, but that they are the cost of the commitments which make the theory post-metaphysical, met, if at all, by a method which declares what it has conceded; the deficit’s practical face is excepted until the second-stage question is settled (§2.3.3).

## §3.2 · The Categorial Premise Defended, and the First Interval Localised

The modal charge is adjudicated with an instrument, and the instrument is owed a ground before it is used. Chapter Two §2.2.2 sets the categorial premise down under a declared debt, which is discharged here: first the ground, then the objections, then the conditions under which the premise may be falsified, and then the premise in the form Derrida's pressure left it. On that footing the first of the tensions Chapter Two hands forward is localised, and what survives of it is placed at the one point where the two programmes divide. Whether the access claimed at that point exists is not settled here; it is put to the elenctic test at §3.3.

### §3.2.1 · Three modal claims, the criterion of individuation, and the burden rule

Three claims are in play whenever Habermas writes of the presuppositions of argumentation, and the argument requires that they be kept apart. There is the necessity of presupposing, conditional on entry into the practice: whoever argues must presuppose, and whoever leaves argumentation may shake the compulsion off (Chapter One §1.5.3). There is the non-necessity of obtaining: that the presuppositions are unavoidably made entails nothing about their satisfaction, and Habermas writes of the idealising and in many cases counterfactual content of 'unavoidable' pragmatic presuppositions, the scare-quotes being his.^[MCCA 86 for the compulsion shaken off outside argumentation; *Postmetaphysical Thinking II*, trans. Ciaran Cronin (Cambridge: Polity, 2017), ch. 9, §III, n. 22, for the idealising and counterfactual content; MCCA 97 for the status of an assumption. The three-way division is not marked as such by Habermas.] And there is the modal standing of the theorist's ascription of the first claim, which is given 'the status of an assumption' to be checked against cases (Chapter Two §2.1.2). The first governs a participant, the second a state of the world, the third a proposition.

The criterion Habermas enters for the first claim is reflexive. The defeasibility which attaches to the supposition of rationality is said not to apply to idealisations in the domain of cognition, even where these too take the form of pragmatic presuppositions: the supposition of a shared objective world is '"transcendentally" necessary in the sense that it cannot be corrected by experiences that would not be possible without it', whereas the supposition of rationality in communicative action holds 'only pro tempore'.^[BNR 41.] The page divides the field by a criterion stated for a class: one supposition is protected, another is not, and which further items fall within the protected class is not settled there. The criterion is Habermas's; its extension to the presuppositions of argumentation is not, and whether they fall on the protected side is what the counter-programme asserts and what reconstruction, on the result of Chapter Two §2.1.2, cannot decide.

The three behaviours of denial which hold the two registers apart are set out at Chapter Two §2.2.2 and are invoked from there: negation, which alone stays inside the order it operates on, and which is the behaviour proper to a proposition; miscarriage, a performative compulsion being not negated but failed; and the withholding of compliance, a norm's bindingness being not negated but refused. Two things are owed to them which that chapter does not supply. The first is the criterion under which they individuate at all: an item's kind answers to the treatment it admits of, so that negation is the operation by which propositions, and only propositions, are disputed. The second is the standing of the first behaviour's own report. That a denier's assertion reaches the description of a compulsion and not the compulsion is not derived from the behaviour of denial but exhibited in it, in the denier's act, and §3.3.1 supplies the form of that exhibition. The one apparent exception, the principle of non-contradiction, whose negation can be written down but not asserted, is not a counterexample but the paradigm case, and Aristotle treats it as such in *Metaphysics* Γ.4.

The vocabulary of the two registers is fixed at Chapter Two §2.2.2 and is invoked and not re-fixed: the operative register of enactment, with its two faces, the operative face, the enactment as performed, and the reflexive face, the enactment turned upon itself; the propositional register of description; and between them the operative–propositional asymmetry, which, stated as a premise, is the categorial premise. Three things follow from the ground just given, and each is used below. The difference between the registers is one of kind and not of grade, grades being what is in dispute. It rests on the behaviour of denial and therefore on no claim about the reliability of reflection, which distinguishes the ground offered here from the school which first drew the line. And it is turned against that school, since a proposition inheriting the standing of the performative evidence would have crossed the line, as §3.2.4 shows of Kuhlmann's inference.

The burden rule Chapter Two §2.2.2 draws from the behaviour of denial is what the positive result comes to, and the negative is regularly taken for the whole. Habermas states the negative half and its limit in one sentence: the demonstration of performative contradictions establishes that there are no alternatives to the rules of argumentation, and the rules are not thereby justified.^[MCCA 95; Chapter Two §2.1.2 displays the sentence.] The passage from *not coherently deniable* to *provisionally acceptable* is not a logical inference. It is pragmatic, holding within a practice which distributes burdens of proof, and it is the operative–propositional asymmetry applied to itself, since what is enacted is a distribution of burdens and a proposition about the distribution is corrigible like any other. Nor is the result an affirmation in disguise, since no second negation has been performed: a denial which cannot be executed is a speech act that failed, not the negation of a negation from which an affirmation might be read off on the mathematical model.^[The figure is Brunkhorst's, describing philosophy's doubly negativistic office: 'double negation is not the same as affirmation on the mathematical model (i.e., "minus times minus equals plus")', in Hauke Brunkhorst, Regina Kreide and Cristina Lafont (eds), *The Habermas Handbook* (New York: Columbia University Press, 2018), 356. The inferential principle stated here is the chapter's.] A provisional standing so distributed is not a deontic bindingness, since a claim which stands until contested has not thereby obliged anyone to anything beyond the discourse in which the burden lies. The rule therefore sharpens the second interval, between provisional standing and bindingness, instead of crossing it, and it does so on the side of the counter-programme as much as on Habermas's: an *Aufweis* which exhibits a condition by defeating its denial secures the standing described and nothing beyond it. Where more is claimed below, at §3.3.3 and §3.3.6, the claim is to be measured against this rule.

The premise so grounded may be restated in the form the derivation of Chapter Two uses. An enactment is individuated by what a performed denial would undo, and not by what would make a proposition about it true. Nothing in it answers to an assessment of content as got right or got wrong, which is the assessability fallibility presupposes, and for that reason it is not the kind of item to which fallibility could intelligibly attach. Whether an interlocutor has taken an utterance rightly or wrongly, the criterion §3.3.1 works with, is an assessment of uptake and not of content, and the two are not run together. The property invoked is assessability and not truth-aptness. Habermas withholds the justification-transcendent sense of truth from moral rightness, which he calls an epistemic concept, and holds moral insight fallible notwithstanding; a criterion resting on aptness for truth in the justification-transcendent sense would therefore exempt every rightness claim from the fallibility Chapter Two §2.1.2 asserts of the reconstructions which state them.^[TJ 230 for rightness as an epistemic concept which has lost the ontological connotation of truth; TJ 258 for the fallibilist proviso under which agreement in moral discourse nonetheless stands.] The categorial line is confirmed in the predicates Habermas keeps to either side of it without remarking on the alignment. At MCCA 32 the presuppositions 'cannot be cast aside', a predicate of what a subject does or fails to do; on the same page, and introduced by 'For this reason', all rational reconstructions have 'only hypothetical status', a predicate of what a proposition has or lacks.^[MCCA 32. The relation asserted is one of sentence order on the page and nothing else.]

### §3.2.2 · The premise defended: two objections, two falsifiers, the cost, and the amended individuation

An objection is entered against the asymmetry itself. If the operative is reachable only under a propositional description, and every description is corrigible, then the grasp of what is enacted is corrigible too, and the asymmetry collapses into a distinction which can be drawn but not occupied. The inference fails three times over. It misses its target, since the claim is not that the enactment is known incorrigibly but that correction is not predicated of it; it generalises past its target, since on the same reasoning the fallibility of physics would infect the processes physics describes; and it begs the question, since the passage from *access is fallible* to *the item is fallible* is the operative–propositional conflation under another name. An inferentialist rejoinder, constructed here from Brandom's account of scorekeeping and not entered by him against this premise, denies that anything stands on the enactment side except statuses instituted by attitudes of attributing and undertaking, so that what is here called the operative register is the implicit half of one scorekeeping practice, made explicit without remainder by logical and modal vocabulary.^[Robert Brandom, *Making It Explicit* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1994), 595 and 600, for objectivity as a structural feature of each scorekeeping perspective.] The reply is the falsifier declared below. What is attributed can be withdrawn, and a status which exists only as attributed is undone when the attribution is undone; whereas the enactment of a presupposition of argument is not undone by any scorekeeper's withdrawing the attribution of it, since the withdrawal is itself a move in the practice whose presuppositions it would suspend. The categorial claim is advanced as a reconstruction of the ordinary practice of disputing and is corrigible as any reconstruction is. It is declared falsifiable: a case in which negating the description undid an enactment which is not constituted by being attributed would refute it. Determinacy under fallible description is necessary for what is claimed and far from sufficient, since a natural process also has a determinate character and does not thereby institute meaning; that the enactment falls within the constitution of meaning rather than outside it is Apel's argument, examined at §3.2.4 and, in its Peircean ground, at §3.3.4.

A charge of a different order alleges an ineffable residue manufactured and installed at the foundation of the account. It would be decisive if the enactment were placed beyond articulation, which it is not; the claim is the one §3.2.1 entered, that no articulation can be shown to have exhausted it. The disclaimer is Habermas's own. Having written that 'Apel uses the vivid image of the interlocking of the ideal and the real communication community; but this sounds almost too Kantian', he adds that 'The doctrine of the "two realms" has been completely overcome'.^[OPC 207, in 'A Reply' (1991). The unavailability of a two-realm architecture is drawn here against the interest of the argument rather than in its favour.] The categorial premise rests on the behaviour of denial and not on an appeal to two orders of being. It establishes that the first and third modal claims of §3.2.1 are two items and not one, so that the appearance of conflict between them rests on a premise the behaviour of denial does not support: that the modal status of what is presupposed transmits to the statement that it is presupposed. That no modal status crosses by any resource of reconstructive science is a further claim, argued at §3.2.3, which leaves open whether it crosses by any other means.

Against the premise an objection is entered from the transcendental-pragmatic side, and it must be taken in its strongest form. The weak form is a regress worry: the premise is a proposition, all propositions are fallible, so the premise is fallible and the protection evaporates. The strong form is meaning-critical, and it is constructed here from the position Apel and Kuhlmann hold rather than taken from a text in which either presses it. The premise is no hypothesis among others about the furniture of the world but states what kinds of item can bear modal properties, a claim about the conditions under which any argument is intelligible; claims of that kind are enacted in every argument in which they might be disputed, and hence belong to the class the premise says propositions cannot occupy.

The reply concedes without reservation that the premise is a proposition and is fallible, and rests on two grounds. The first is a disanalogy. A prohibition on formulation is self-undermining in the medium it governs, as the verification principle forbids meaningful assertion of a class to which it belongs; an assertion that one kind of item does not bear a modal property borne by another forms no forbidden sentence. The second is the reply Apel's own principle of self-recuperation (*Selbsteinholung*), which Chapter Two enters under the second commitment, demands rather than merely permits. What is enacted in every argument is not the content of the premise but the distinction the content states, so that a condition of intelligibility can be stated, its statement being a proposition and fallible, while what it states is enacted. That is not a paradox but the categorial premise itself. The premise is therefore self-instantiating in the sense the burden rule allows: its denial cannot be executed without the denier's performing and his saying coming apart in the act, which is the distinction the premise states, exhibited in the denial whether or not the denier draws it, the standing so conferred being provisional and intersubjective and not a demonstration.

The consequence is a cost, and it falls on the standing of the reading rather than on the premise. If the premise is self-instantiating in that way, the position so reached shares a reflexive criterion with the counter-programme and cannot present itself as an adjudication conducted from outside both. It stands closer to Apel than the language of stronger and weaker claims suggests, and Apel operates the same register-distinction. His criterion for what counts as ultimately grounded is of the same reflexive kind and not one of formal-logical demonstration: what cannot be denied without self-contradiction and cannot at the same time be demonstrated without *petitio principii* 'is not an insight into a formal-logical dilemma but an insight into an indispensable condition of the possibility of performative arguing'.^[SE2 99, Apel's own endnote 63 to 'The Question of Grounding'. The register he there contrasts with the performative is logical demonstration and not the propositional as such; that this narrowing strengthens the result of §3.2.1 rather than conceding to Apel is argued in the body.] The difference does not disappear. He takes the reflexive insight to yield a proposition immune to revision; the reading defended here takes it to yield an enactment whose every statement remains revisable. What divides the position from Apel's is therefore the single categorial question Chapter Two §2.1.2 located, which §3.3.6 states as the crossing of two boundaries.

Three concessions travel with the reply. The premise is falsifiable by a second route, stated with the restriction which makes it a route: it would be defeated by the exhibition of an item which is both a performance and truth-apt and whose truth-value is settled by the performing rather than by what is said, so that separating the utterance from the sentence uttered would leave one of the two without the property ascribed to the item. The nearest candidates, the explicit performatives, do not meet that condition, since there the utterance is a performance and the sentence uttered is truth-apt independently of it. That the performance/description distinction is not exhaustive of the field of items to which modal predicates attach would not defeat the premise, which asserts a difference of kind and not an exhaustion of kinds. And the premise is not category-neutral towards Apel's third-kind claim: it grants that the conditions are not objectifiable and that their individuation is not semantic, and declines only the further step by which a non-objectifying reflection is credited with yielding a proposition immune to revision.

One further step is owed before the premise may be used, since the chapters use it in the form Chapter Two §2.3.2 amended and not in the form first stated. Derrida's argument from iterability was taken there to reach what the exhibition requires on its own behalf, that a speaker's denial be recognisable as the denial of what his denying enacts, and the requirement was conceded in a thin form: recognisability holds between these interlocutors, on this occasion, and nothing is claimed for an identity of sense which would survive the occasion. The three behaviours stand as individuated, since what tells them apart is what each does to its object and not how far the doing reaches; the burden rule is undisturbed, having been drawn in the indexed form the concession requires; and the two falsifiers survive in a bounded shape, each falling within a single exchange. What the concession costs the grounding is reach and not standing. The question it leaves open is handed to §3.3: whether what two interlocutors can recognise between them will bear a result that no exchange bounds.

### §3.2.3 · The first interval: three routes across it, and the transfer fallibilism forbids

Suppose the standing of an enacted presupposition were to cross to the proposition reconstructing it. Three routes present themselves, and the programme's own resources permit none of them.

The first route is description: a true description of an unavoidable presupposition would itself be unavoidable. The passage of 1983 quoted at Chapter Two §2.1.2 closes it so far as certainty is concerned: the intuitive knowledge of rules has a certainty which the reconstruction of it does not inherit.^[MCCA 97.] That unavoidability is not inherited either follows only with the further premise that a description stands to what it describes as a proposition to an enactment, which is the categorial premise §3.2.1 grounds and not the passage. The passage of 1981 quoted at Chapter Two §2.1.1 closes it from the theorist's side: an interpreter who credits those he interprets with his own competence forfeits his methodological immunity and exposes his interpretation to their criticism.^[TCA I 119.] The generalisation drawn from that forfeiture, that a description inherits nothing from its object by being true of it, is not Habermas's, and neither is its extension to formal pragmatics, which his grouping of formal pragmatics among the reconstructive sciences licenses.

The second route is self-application. The theorist who reconstructs the presuppositions is himself arguing, and his assertion therefore enacts what it describes; might the enactment protect the assertion? The theorist's performance certifies a token: this assertion, in being made, enacts the presuppositions, shown in the act and not open to revision. What the reconstruction asserts is a type: that every act of the kind enacts them. The token-certification does not transmit to the type, since a singular enactment is as compatible with a contingent universal as with a necessary one; the reconstruction is thus indexically self-verifying and universally hypothetical at once. Habermas draws the same conclusion in 1991: 'the self-referentiality of the undoubtedly central analysis of general presuppositions of argumentation that can be made only by participants in argument does not secure the philosophical enterprise the independence and infallibility Apel associates with the notion of an ultimate justification'.^[JA 83. The token/type analysis has no counterpart in either author.]

Apel concedes the point in the case that matters most to him. Every attempt to formulate the principle of discourse ethics 'appears as revisable', and this 'appears to me to be the strongest argument for the hypothetical character, even of the results of transcendental reflection'; what is possible in their explication is, in the parallel statement of *From a Transcendental-Semiotic Point of View*, 'at most, only self-correction (i.e. through self-reference)'.^[SE2 198; FTSPV 88, where the concession 'all such explications are revisable' stands on the page with the condition that the fallibilism principle is coherent 'only if its presuppositions are coherent in their a priori certainty'.] He qualifies the concession on the page which enters it. The explications 'do not have their possible corrective in external, empirical evidence, but in the performative evidence of our argumentative practical knowledge', and their status is entirely different from that of empirical hypotheses; two pages later they are 'unquestionably true, i.e., certain' so far as they adequately explicate the performative evidence presupposed in them, and 'revisable explicative hypotheses only inasmuch as they may require a self-correction'.^[SE2 198 and 200.]

The third route is a different mode of access: a reflection which articulates the presupposition from within the act of arguing rather than describing it from outside, so that the articulation carries the standing of the act. That is Apel's route, and what this section settles of it is only that it is not one of reconstructive science. The method's tests are recognition and counterexample; they reach content and extension; a mode of access reaching modality would be, by the argument of Chapter Two §2.1.2, one the method does not possess. Apel says so in his own name, answering the proposal that the presuppositions be established empirically after Chomsky's manner: an adequate concept of argumentation, 'concerning its necessary conditions, can only be attained through strict transcendental reflection', his answer to a 'suspicion of a petitio principii' which he there anticipates against his own dismissal of the empirical proposal.^[SE2 197; and SE2 179 for self-accountability placed among the conditions of the reconstructive sciences' possibility and not among their results.] If the standing crosses, it does not cross by reconstruction.

The corrigibility reminder of 1983 is directed 'for this reason' against the inference from inescapability to ultimate justification. In 2003 Habermas records following Apel's nondeductive model and locates the point of contention not at the justification move but at its status within a game of justification which is not foundational; a page beyond the reminder, the essay of 1983 puts the same point positively: 'No harm is done, however, if we deny that the transcendental-pragmatic justification constitutes an ultimate justification. Rather, discourse ethics then takes its place among the reconstructive sciences concerned with the rational bases of knowing, speaking, and acting.'^[MCCA 98; BNR 88.] Habermas has not failed to notice a route by which the standing might cross; the fallibilism his programme adopts forbids the transfer.

What the three routes establish may be stated as a result, and the ground of the result is the silence of the method on modality rather than the number of the routes. Since the tests a reconstruction commands are recognition and counterexample, and these reach content and extension alone, formal pragmatics cannot from its own reconstructive resources transmit the modal standing of enacted commitments to their propositional reconstruction. Description will not do it, the description being corrigible on Habermas's own account; self-application will not, a token-certification not reaching a type; and strict reflection, which claims to, is placed by both parties outside the method. Whether a transfer is possible by any means is what divides the two programmes, and their reference point is common ground, which Habermas states in Apel's favour: in 'argumentation as such', he writes, 'Apel has gained a reference point that is as fundamental for the analysis of unavoidable rules as the "I think" or "consciousness as such" is for the philosophy of reflection'.^[MCCA 81.] The consequence is not drawn by him: the highest point of the deduction is occupied no longer by the 'I think' which, in Kant's words, 'must be able to accompany all my representations', but by a 'we argue'.^[Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, B131–32.]

### §3.2.4 · The other access, localised at the reflexive face, and the modal register of the threshold

Apel's claim is that the self-referential meaning of an explicit performative phrase can be transformed by any competent arguer into propositional knowledge by a kind of public reflection which belongs to the language game itself.^[FTSPV 21, in 'The impact of analytic philosophy on my intellectual biography'; and SE2 43: 'This a priori can be reconstructed, but not overlooked.'] Two features carry the weight: the reflection belongs to the language game, being a move within it and not a theory about it entered from outside, and it is public, an explication any arguer can be brought to share rather than an introspective certitude. The claim is therefore not that a description inherits an immunity, which the three routes exclude, but that at the reflexive face of the performative the difference between enacting and describing lapses.

Habermas refuses the claim, and his refusal has two formulations which are not to be run together. The first is a diagnosis of its ground, entered in 1983: the claim to ultimate justification rests, if he understands Apel correctly, on identifying propositional truth with the experience of certitude, an identification available only under the conditions of a philosophy of consciousness.^[MCCA 96, in a passage which credits Apel with the paradigm shift it charges him with abandoning.] The qualification is Habermas's, and Apel disowns the ground, seeing in his own procedure 'the possibility of renewing the problematic of philosophical (transcendental) self-reflection of thought in a non-psychological way'.^[FTSPV 21, continuing: 'By contrast Habermas has recently appeared to consider any renewal of the problematic of reflection with the aid of language pragmatics as a relapse into the obsolete paradigm of the transcendental philosophy of consciousness.'] The second is a diagnosis of the strategy, entered in 2020 (§3.1.5). The refusal is accepted here; the first diagnosis is declined on Apel's own testimony; and the ground of acceptance is neither diagnosis but the result of the three routes, taken within the reach that result has. Strict reflection is said to yield propositions which inherit the standing of what they articulate, and no resource of reconstructive science delivers that inheritance. Whether an ultimate grounding is excluded by the asymmetry itself is not settled at this point, since the asymmetry bars no route but the one reconstruction possesses; the transfer is declined at §3.3.3 and on the ground stated there.

The two faces of the operative register carry the dispute, and the theorist's proposition adds a third register. Apel's strict reflection operates at the reflexive face alone, and his stronger predicate is claimed there; the propositional register is where either face is described, and the revisability of what is recorded there is common ground. The dispute is not about the phenomenon, since both parties grant the inescapability at the operative face, nor about the revisability of a formulation, since both grant that any statement of the presuppositions may be improved. It is about what a correction answers to, for Habermas the discussion to which any reconstruction is submitted, for Apel the performative evidence of the act explicated; and about whether the coincidence of enacting and thematising at the reflexive face yields an insight of a different modal order, or only a token-certification no more transmissible than any other. Habermas declines the further issue where reconstruction must decline it, writing that 'the concept of "strict reflection" introduced by Wolfgang Kuhlmann raises further issues that I cannot address here', and assigns the transcendental-pragmatic argument, two sentences earlier, the standing of a discursively untestable reassurance.^[BNR 97; the status is examined at Chapter Two §2.3.2.] §3.3 puts the question, by asking whether an exhibition of the elenctic kind, the refutation convicting a denier of enacting what he denies, can display what a reconstruction cannot certify.

Kuhlmann draws a stronger conclusion from the presuppositions of argumentation, and Habermas displays it in order to refuse it. What cannot be meaningfully disputed 'because it is necessarily presupposed in a process of meaningful argumentation', and what 'for the same reason I cannot meaningfully justify by deriving it deductively', is on Kuhlmann's account a secure and unshakable basis; Habermas contests neither conjunct and refuses the inference, the argument form being what 'H. Lenk calls petitio tollendi', the denier's reliance on what he would remove.^[MCCA 95. Habermas's note sends the passage to Kuhlmann (1981); the page reference given for it in earlier drafts (57) cannot be squared with the pagination of Kuhlmann's article of that year (*Zeitschrift für philosophische Forschung* 35 (1981), 3–26) and must be taken from MCCA's own endnote before submission.] Classified by the distinctions drawn above, the first conjunct concerns the operative face and is conceded; the second concerns the propositional order, what a deduction of the item would cost, and is conceded on its own ground; the conclusion is a proposition about a type, and the 'therefore' which joins them carries it across the boundary the behaviour of denial fixes. Whether the crossing is licit is the reflexive-face question, and it is not decided by naming the inference. §3.3.3 examines it.

The limit is not an oversight. §3.2.3 closed each route by a commitment the programme holds on purpose. The route of description is closed by fallibilism, all rational reconstructions having only hypothetical status. The route of self-application is closed by the same commitment applied reflexively, the reconstruction of the presuppositions being itself an argument which claims no exemption. The route of strict reflection is closed by detranscendentalisation, with which the concept of the transcendental is itself altered and philosophy retains the office of a stand-in for reconstructive sciences and not that of a judge.^[*Wahrheit und Rechtfertigung* (Frankfurt am Main: Suhrkamp, 1999), 26, the sentence Chapter Two §2.1.4 displays in German; and Chapter Two §2.1 for the two offices.] The silence on modality is therefore the cost of post-metaphysical thinking as Habermas defines it; a programme which met it differently, by exempting one proposition or admitting a reflection that was not a reconstruction, would no longer be the programme.

What remains once the access is localised is the modal register in which the two parties state their claims, and it must be fixed from their own vocabulary before the elenctic test is applied. The five restrictions under which Chapter Two §2.1.4 fixes Habermas's register are not re-laid. What the sentence of 2020 adds is the clause which closes it: the discursive examination of criticisable validity claims is unavoidable 'only in the weaker sense that there is no functional equivalent for this practice of giving and asking for reasons in any of the sociocultural forms of life that we know of'.^[PSC 646. The next sentence calls the modality 'a detranscendentalized conception of de facto unavoidability for our species'. Habermas's own term for the register in the essay is 'quasi-transcendental' (PSC 635, 636, 645); 'weakly transcendental' is this thesis's designation for the grade Chapter Two §2.1.4 fixes from his statements of 1991 and 2003, and is not his phrase in 2020.] The phrasing indexes the claim to a speaker and to the forms of life known, and the indexing is his and not a construal placed on him. It is compatible with the weakly transcendental grade and does not by itself secure it; what it fixes is the bound of the claim and not its grade. What it leaves open is whether an absence of alternatives so indexed carries transcendental force or is an induction over the forms of life encountered.

The demarcation must not, however, be made to rest on the lexicon, since *Nichthintergehbarkeit* is not Apel's word alone. Habermas predicates it in his own voice of the communication-presuppositions of argumentation in 1999 and of the presuppositions of a research practice in 2019, glossing it there as what 'we can only reconstruct in the performance of an operation directed at something else'.^[*Wahrheit und Rechtfertigung*, 51; *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie*, 2 vols (Berlin: Suhrkamp, 2019; cited from the paperback edition of 2022), Bd. II, 755 ('wir können sie nur im Vollzug einer Operation, die sich auf anderes richtet, rekonstruieren'), where the page before expressly downgrades the modality, calling the suppositions not necessary in a logically compelling sense but unavoidable or irreplaceable (754). Translations are the chapter's.] No demarcation can be drawn from the presence of the word, and none is drawn from it. What the usage settles, in Habermas's favour, is that he possesses the concept of a presupposition which cannot be gone behind and is accessible only in performing an operation directed elsewhere: the operative side of the asymmetry in his own terms. What divides him from Apel is what the access is taken to yield, and Apel states the divergence himself: in point of content the position of a transcendental-pragmatic ultimate grounding of ethics agrees extensively with Habermas's in *Moralbewußtsein und kommunikatives Handeln*, 'wenngleich Habermas seine Begründung der Ethik als bloß hypothetisch und insofern ihr Prinzip als fallibel auffassen möchte'.^[Apel, *Diskurs und Verantwortung* (Frankfurt: Suhrkamp, 1988), 253 n. 6; and 117–18 n. 17, where the reconstructive theory of communicative action is said not to replace the ultimate grounding of ethics but to presuppose it, 'sondern setzt sie schon voraus'. Translations are the chapter's.] The demarcation defended in this chapter is how one of the parties describes it.

The modal question inherits the form Habermas's refusal took in 1983. What the demonstration of performative contradictions proves is that the rules of argumentation admit of no alternative; the rules themselves are not thereby justified. The argument-form he refers, after Lenk, to the *petitio tollendi*, and its yield he lets Schönrich state, introducing the statement as provocative and not adopting it in his own voice: implications 'wrung from the skeptic through cunning can have no more than quasi-empirical validity', since whether those who think will one day think otherwise is, on that view, an open question.^[MCCA 95–96. Apel's reply of 1989, that the charge establishes only the formal-logical non-rejectability of a claim within a language game which could as a whole be questioned, and that this is not available for the language game in which language games as such are spoken about, stands at Chapter One §1.6.2.] The refusal is accordingly not a denial that the presuppositions are, for whoever argues, not to be gone behind. It is that their not being gone behind is, on the construal this chapter places on the refusal, a fact of the kind an induction over thinkers reports. The answer these chapters give in their own name is the retorsion of §3.3.2, which turns not on whether thinking might one day go otherwise but on whether the claim that it might can be stated without enacting what it relativises.

The question so posed is the object of Niquet's analysis of transcendental modality: of what a condition of possibility can mean once the Kantian synthetic-constructive model is surrendered, and of the absence of alternatives (*Alternativenlosigkeit*) which transcendental arguments claim. His analysis separates an absence of alternatives which holds for what can be conceived from one which holds for what has been met with, and under that separation the formulation of 2020 can be asked whether it is more than a generalisation about the forms of life so far known.^[Marcel Niquet, *Transzendentale Argumente: Kant, Strawson und die Aporetik der Detranszendentalisierung* (Frankfurt am Main: Suhrkamp, 1991), §2.2.3 (61 ff.), whose section title gives the triad *Konzeptualschemata, Alternativenlosigkeit, Vorstellbarkeit*. The application is the chapter's.] The question cannot be settled by either party's preferred instrument. A survey of forms of life cannot establish that no alternative is enactable, since surveys reach only what has been encountered; and a derivation cannot establish it either, since any derivation is conducted in argument and would presuppose what it undertakes to secure. What is shown is not that no inference of any kind could reach the result, but that no inference conducted within argument can reach a condition of argument without presupposing it. If the result is to be reached at all, it must be reached by a procedure which exhibits rather than infers, and which reaches the conditions of contestation by way of the act of contesting. It is set out at §3.3, and the ground on which it is available, the categorial premise, has now been laid.

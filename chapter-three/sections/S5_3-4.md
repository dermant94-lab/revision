## §3.4 · The Aporia of the Meaning of Truth

Two routes to the governing aporia of the thesis terminate in this section. The route through the concept of truth was opened in Chapter One, where the question of validity is generated (§1.2.2), and narrowed at §1.5.6; the modal route, through the status of the presuppositions, was opened at Chapter Two §2.1.4 and grounded in §3.2. The office of this section in the thesis exceeds its office in the chapter, and it must be stated first. The three constitutive commitments of reconstructive science, reconstructivism, immanence and fallibilism, generate a tension which none of them may be surrendered to relieve. Habermas navigates it by drawing on what he calls a transcendence from within, a phrase he predicates of the unconditional moment in validity claims; its extension to philosophy's own procedure is not his. The navigation raises one question: by what title, and within what limits, may philosophy operate as critical self-reflection on the practice in which it is itself conducted? That question is the inherited question of irrefutability, and it is prior to any dispute over the derivation of a moral principle. The place at which it becomes visible as a threat rather than a limitation is the aporia of the meaning of truth.

### §3.4.1 · An avowal and a limitation

Two things about the standing of the argument which reaches this point are the conditions under which it is advanced. The first is an avowal. The reading the chapter has offered is an Apelian reading of Habermas, and it is entered as such rather than presented as a neutral adjudication. It takes over Apel's strong construal of the claim to intelligibility, his reflexive concept of grounding, and his conviction that the presuppositions of argumentation are the prior question, and it reaches its conclusions because it takes these over. The negative claim of this section holds only for one who accepts those premises. What is claimed for the premises is that they are argued rather than assumed, the intelligibility transit at §3.3.2 and the concept of grounding at §3.1.3, and that where they remain contestable the contest has been recorded.

The second is a limitation, the counterpart of the avowal. The reading's being Apelian does not make it a vindication of *Letztbegründung*. What the chapter establishes, for one who grants the avowed premises, is that weak transcendentalism cannot answer the prior question: an unavoidability indexed, as §3.2.4 finds Habermas's to be, to the forms of life so far known is on the argument of §3.3.2 relative to the forms of life over which it quantifies, and a self-reflexive philosophy whose authority rested on it would have its authority relativised in the same measure. It does not establish that Apel's apodictic answer succeeds, which §3.3.3 has declined. The result is a determinate negative claim together with a determinate question left open.

### §3.4.2 · The two desiderata and the Janus face

The retraction of the epistemic conception of truth is established at Chapter One §1.5.6 and is invoked here. Habermas credits it to 'discussions with Albrecht Wellmer and Cristina Lafont', which convinced him that the internal connection between truth and justification 'does not amount to a conceptual connection between truth and rational assertibility under ideal conditions. Otherwise we could not take truth to be a property of propositions that they "cannot lose."'^[TJ 37–38. The parenthesis credits discussions and not publications; the arguments they concerned are presumably those of Cristina Lafont, *The Linguistic Turn in Hermeneutic Philosophy*, trans. José Medina (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1999), ch. 6, 'Rational Acceptability and Truth', 283–314, and of Wellmer, *Ethik und Dialog* (1986), which Lafont herself credits (299 n. 24). The identification is the chapter's.] The mature account must therefore satisfy two requirements. Truth must remain internally connected to justification, since a claim's content is fixed by what its discursive redemption would require, and a truth wholly severed from those conditions would be a truth of which nothing determinate could be said. Truth must nevertheless transcend justification, since without a justification-transcendent point of reference the difference between the best-justified consensus and the truth cannot be marked, and with that difference goes the fallibilism on which the reconstructive programme rests. An account which secures the first at the expense of the second is the consensus theory Habermas retracted; an account which secures the second at the expense of the first is the correspondence theory his post-metaphysical commitment forbids. The mature position is the attempt to occupy the interval.

Habermas's proposal for occupying it has two levels. The orientation towards truth, 'as a property that a proposition "cannot lose"', 'acquires an indispensable regulative function for fallible processes of justification precisely if such processes can at best lead to decisions about the rational acceptability of propositions and not their truth'.^[TJ 91–92.] That orientation is not housed in discourse. The nonepistemic concept of truth, 'which manifests itself only operatively, that is, unthematically, in action, provides a justification-transcendent point of reference for discursively thematized truth claims'; the lifeworld 'furnishes a justification-transcendent standard for orienting ourselves by context-independent truth claims—a standard that is always already presupposed in action'; and the gap between justification and truth, which 'cannot be closed definitively within discourse', 'can be closed pragmatically by a rationally motivated transition from discourse to action'.^[TJ 39, 254 and 92.] The discursive conception is to be complemented by a pragmatic one if it is 'to do justice to the weak ontological connotations that we associate with the "grasping of facts"', and this reading 'salvages the moment of unconditionality'.^[TJ 252.] The two-sided concept is what Habermas calls 'a Janus-faced everyday concept of truth that mediates between lifeworld and discourse'.^[TJ 254; and 253 for 'a Janus-faced truth that establishes the desired internal connection between performative certainty and warranted assertibility'.]

That discourse cannot supply its own transcendence is therefore not an objection to Habermas; it is his premise, and the two-level architecture is built on it. What is pressed here is narrower. The two desiderata are secured in different registers and by operations of different kinds: the tether to justification is fixed discursively, the outrunning is borne operatively, in the certainties of a practice which is not itself an assertion and whose standing as a whole no act of the practice calls to account, however many of its certainties are thematised singly. The transcendence is real, but it is borne and not established; and a theory which needs the difference between an ideally justified consensus and the truth must then take that difference on the practice's word. Habermas states the cost himself: the transcending relation 'guarantees the difference between truth and rational acceptability, but puts the participants in discourse in a paradoxical position', since a truth claim can be made good by reasons alone while every reason stands under the reservation of fallibility.^[TJ 40.]

The counterpart on the side of rightness is a doctrine of the mature position and not an artefact of exposition. 'Truth' is 'a justification-transcendent concept that cannot be made to coincide even with the concept of ideal warranted assertibility', whereas 'the meaning of "rightness" consists entirely in ideal warranted acceptability'. The qualification Habermas enters on the same page must be carried with it: the construction of a world of well-ordered interpersonal relationships 'is subject to constraints that are not at our disposal; otherwise we could not talk about moral insight', and 'the absence of ontological connotations must not impair the claim to universal or unconditional validity'.^[TJ 247–48; and TJ 42: the rightness of moral judgments and norms 'lacks such a justification-transcendent point of reference'.] The measure therefore bifurcates. On the side of truth the cautionary use survives: a claim justified under the best available conditions may nonetheless be false, and the reservation is intelligible because there is something, the obtaining of the state of affairs, for the justified claim to fail of. On the side of rightness the reservation has no purchase, since there is nothing for an ideally accepted norm to fail to correspond to.

### §3.4.3 · Why the aporia is the modal fracture in another register

The justification-transcendence Habermas requires for truth is carried by the unconditional orientation built into the act of raising a claim and borne by the certainties of a form of life. Those certainties are placed here in the same modal position as the presuppositions §3.2.4 graded: the indexing Habermas enters for the practice of argumentation is carried over to them by this chapter and not by him, on the ground that a certainty sustained by a form of life is indexed no more widely than what sustains it. A transcendence borne by what is so indexed cannot be certified, on the account's own resources, as anything other than form-of-life-relative, which is what the account required it not to be. The aporia is therefore not a local difficulty in the theory of truth but the modal fracture of §3.1 in the epistemological register. Habermas refuses both familiar horns, the wholly epistemic concept and correspondence, and states a third course, one which does justice to 'the realist intuition that there is a world independent of us without representing propositions as corresponding to facts'.^[TJ 247.] The question pressed is not whether a third course has been stated, since the statement is his, but whether what is stated is a third course or the second conducted under a lifeworld description.

The pressure on the rightness side has its nearest statement in Lafont. On the side of truth her argument reaches the conclusion the mature account adopts; on the side of rightness it is the same argument denied the same satisfaction. If ideal rational acceptability is what constitutes rightness, then either the ideal conditions are construed so strongly that the participants would have to be infallible, which no discourse-theoretic specification of the conditions can deliver, or the procedure 'allows for error', in which case 'its results cannot be considered morally right per se', and meeting the procedural conditions 'does not constitute what "morally right" means'.^[Lafont, *The Linguistic Turn in Hermeneutic Philosophy*, ch. 7, 'Rational Acceptability and Moral Rightness', 315–60, at 333–35.] The cognitivist vocabulary the framework retains for morality, error, knowledge, learning, presupposes that an ideally formed agreement might nonetheless be mistaken, and that presupposition has no purchase on an account in which ideal acceptance is that in which correctness consists. Habermas retains a reservation for rightness which has the appearance of the one he retains for truth: discourses of application carry 'revisionary force', and the supposition 'that every moral question here and now can in principle be given a "single right answer" is risky, to say the least'.^[TJ 272.] What that reservation indexes, however, is the distance of an actual agreement from the ideal, not a possible failure of the ideal agreement. Habermas sees the pressure and names it: given the premise that 'rightness' reduces to 'rational acceptability', 'the binary decision, which must be unequivocal, somewhat acquires the character of a posit'.^[TJ 273; WuR 315.] Two pages on in the German he withdraws the idiom, holding that talk of *Entschluß* and *Festsetzung* 'weist in die falsche Richtung', since the sceptical exit from the language game of justified moral expectations exists only in philosophical reflection and not in practice.^[WuR 317.] The withdrawal removes the suggestion of an arbitrary act and leaves the reduction where it was.

Habermas answers Lafont, and the answer must be entered, since it is made with the instrument these chapters employ. He grants that 'the projection of a moral world and the presupposition of an objective world are functionally equivalent' in securing context-independence, and warns at once against 'assimilating the moral to the objective world', which is how he reads her grounding of cognitivism; the move, he objects, 'assimilates the participant perspective, from within which the required generalization of interests must be undertaken, to an objectifying observer perspective'.^[TJ 266 and 268.] The objection is well taken against the proposal it addresses, and no such proposal is adopted here. What is pressed is narrower and stated wholly within the participant perspective: the cognitivist vocabulary the framework itself retains requires that an ideally formed agreement be capable of being mistaken, and an account in which ideal acceptance is that in which correctness consists cannot supply the requirement. Habermas may decline the ontologising of moral objectivity and still owe the fallibility.

What the transfer of registers establishes may be stated as four claims, each resting on a commitment of Habermas's. (i) Justification has two moments, one context-bound and one context-transcending, and the account requires both. (ii) The rightness of norms is exhausted by ideal rational acceptability, while truth is not so exhausted. (iii) Discourse operates on an understanding of validity which it renders explicit rather than institutes: what it certifies of a norm is its worthiness of recognition, while the mode of validity in which a norm exists is not of discourse's producing (Chapter One §1.4.5), and the reconstructive method describes its own office in the same way (Chapter Two §2.1.1). (iv) No standpoint outside all discursive mediation is available from which that understanding might be secured. The conjunction is not Habermas's, and neither is the thesis that the four constitute an aporia rather than a set of adjustable difficulties. The distinction between the two is what the chapter's claim turns on. Four commitments constitute a set of difficulties if some adjustment to one of them would relieve the tension without costing the framework a commitment it cannot surrender; they constitute an aporia if every such adjustment costs it such a commitment. Abandon (i), and the distinction between inquiry and the ratification of convention goes. Abandon (ii), and either rightness acquires a justification-transcendent standing, which is moral realism, or truth loses its own, which is the retracted consensus theory; the intermediate branch, on which rightness answers to 'constraints that are not at our disposal', owes an account of what those constraints answer to, and on any answer short of a standing beyond ideal acceptability it collapses into no abandonment at all. Abandon (iii), and the method is no longer reconstructive, since a method which generates the competence it describes does not reconstruct it. Abandon (iv), and immanence goes, and the post-metaphysical constraint with it. The practice is thereby shown to rely at its centre on an understanding of validity which it can neither generate from within, by (iii), nor receive from without, by (iv), and which is therefore borne by a practice whose certainties no act of the practice can call to account. What is claimed is that no relieving adjustment has been found, not that none could be.

### §3.4.4 · The exits, the transcendental-semiotic resolution, and what it does not claim

The conjunction is shown to be an impasse and not an unexplored option by what each of its exits costs. Six are occupied. Rorty's abandons the context-transcending moment. The moral realist's accepts discourse-independent moral facts at the cost of the metaphysics whose refusal defines the post-metaphysical situation. A third withdraws the cognitivist claim for rightness, and it is taken from two directions: Wellmer withdraws it while keeping the critical office, at the cost §3.3.3 stated, and a systems-theoretic redescription of validity as a code by which a subsystem reproduces itself withdraws it at the cost of critical purchase, since a description in which validity is a code cannot distinguish a legitimate order from a stable one.^[Niklas Luhmann, *Das Recht der Gesellschaft* (Frankfurt: Suhrkamp, 1993), chs 2 and 4; English as *Law as a Social System*, trans. Klaus A. Ziegert (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004). Cited at the level of the position.] A fourth grounds the presuppositions apodictically, as Apel does, at the cost of the *Letztbegründung* which §3.3.3 found unsecured. A fifth is Habermas's own, the pragmatic complement, whose price is modal and was stated at §3.4.3: a transcendence borne by a lifeworld owes an account of why it is not a form-of-life-relative unconditionality under another description. A sixth surrenders the reconstructive self-description and holds that the norms of discourse are instituted in the practice of giving and asking for reasons, as Brandom's inferentialism does. Its price, set out at Chapter Two §2.3.3 and not repeated here, falls where Habermas's reading of Brandom locates it: on one horn rational validity is assimilated to social validity, and on the other the surplus of content is secured by 'a conceptual realism that obliterates the distinction between the intersubjectively shared lifeworld and the objective world'.^[TJ 142 and 8; TJ 145 for 'a linguistic variation on an objective idealism'. Brandom holds the perspectival structure of scorekeeping sufficient for the thought that the whole community may be mistaken (*Making It Explicit*, 179); the objection concedes the result and asks what the availability of the thought is grounded in.] The instituted norms inherit the standing of the instituting practice, and the question of that practice's standing returns one level up, no longer askable in the theory's own vocabulary.

The resolution proposed is conditional, and its condition is named before it is stated. If the transcendental-semiotic condition of §3.3.4 holds, that assessability by some competent interlocutor is constitutive of propositionhood and not an added desideratum, then the justification-transcendence Habermas requires for truth is located neither in a form of life nor in correspondence but in assessability itself, the condition which constitutes a claim as a claim. The transcendence so located is not relative to the forms of life so far known, since assessability is not a feature of a particular scheme but the condition of there being determinate content to relativise; and it is not a correspondence inspected from outside discourse, since it is a condition of signification and not a relation between propositions and facts.

Two objections must be met, or the resolution will be misread. The first is that assessability by an unlimited community of interpreters is the Peircean final opinion under another name, so that the convergence theory declined at §3.3.4 has been adopted in another voice. The two come apart in both directions. Assessability is a modal property of a claim, that there is something it would be to assess it and someone for whom there would be; convergence is a normative postulate on inquiry, that assessment is to be pursued as though it terminated in agreement. A claim may be assessable and never assessed; it may be assessed without limit and never agreed; and a body of opinion may converge without assessment having done any of the work. The resolution requires assessability alone, and what it locates there is a condition on a claim's having determinate content, not a promise about what inquiry will deliver. On this account what the world withholds, when a practice fails, is the assessability the claim had anticipated: worldly resistance is registered as a failure of anticipated assessment rather than as a mismatch between a proposition and a fact.

The second objection is that rightness claims are assessable no less than truth claims, so that locating justification-transcendence in assessability erases the asymmetry on which §3.4.2–§3.4.3 depend. The objection would be decisive if assessability were being offered as what constitutes transcendence, and it is not. Assessability is a condition of any claim's having determinate content, and it holds of every claim; what differs between the two kinds of claim is not whether assessment is possible but what assessment answers to. The resolution therefore neither supplies the asymmetry nor abolishes it. It supplies the condition under which an asymmetry between two kinds of claim is statable and leaves the asymmetry where Habermas's doctrine places it. It follows that the resolution does nothing for the rightness side. The aporia is relieved, if at all, only on the side of truth, and the pressure of §3.4.3 on the cognitivist vocabulary for morality stands unrelieved. That limit is stated because concealing it would overstate the result.

What the resolution does not claim bears equal weight. It is not a settlement in Apel's favour: it secures its location of transcendence only on the strong reading of the intelligibility claim, which §3.3.2 argued and did not establish beyond contest, and on the minimal reading it is unavailable. It inherits the limit §3.3.2 declared on the retorsion, whose restriction was specified and not discharged. It is not a resolution Habermas could accept while retaining his own description, since to accept the semiotic condition is to accept that the presuppositions of determinate meaning are not unavoidable merely in fact. And it extends nothing to the reciprocal norms of discourse, the second-stage question being left open at §3.3.6. One question, turned on the thesis itself, must be answered rather than survived. If determining the unconditional moment exceeds the two offices Habermas's methodology sanctions, stand-in and interpreter, then the resolution exercises some office, and an account is owed of which. The answer is a third office, elenctic exhibition, defended at §3.3.1 and exercised at §3.3.2–§3.3.4, whose warrant is borrowed neither from the empirical sciences nor from mediation but exhibited in its exercise. The determination it delivers is formal and not material: it exhibits the condition under which a claim possesses determinate content and locates transcendence there, without saying in what truth consists. The office is avowed and not disclaimed, and it is self-applying under the discipline of §3.3.3, its propositional articulation, that of this section included, being fallible and revisable while what it articulates is enacted in every revision.

Avowal is not vindication. Declaring the office does not establish that it delivers what it claims, and whether an appeal of this kind vindicates anything is the question put at §3.1.4, on which the constructive proposal of the thesis turns and which is advanced, conditionally and with its cost stated, at the close of the thesis. What the chapter claims is not that the aporia has been dissolved but that its dissolution on the side of truth has been shown to depend on one determinate thesis, whose burden of proof is now locatable and whose price is now stateable. Chapter Four determines whether the same terminus is reached from the side of the discourse principle, by a route on which the meaning of truth is never in question.

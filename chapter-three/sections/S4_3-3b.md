### §3.3.4 · The transcendental-semiotic a priori: why there is no proposition without assessability

The reciprocity of meaning on which §3.1.4 and §3.3.2 turn rests on a premise which must now be stated, traced to the warrant it is given in the counter-programme, and bounded, since the pre-moral reading holds only so far as the premise holds: a putative content which no competent interlocutor could in principle assess, for truth where the content is assertoric, is not a determinate content but a mark. This is the transcendental-semiotic a priori, and its warrant is Apel's transformation of Peirce.

For Peirce the meaning of a conception is exhausted by the conceivable practical bearings of its object, and truth is 'the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate', the real being what would be represented in that final opinion.^[Charles Sanders Peirce, *Collected Papers*, ed. C. Hartshorne, P. Weiss and A. W. Burks, 8 vols (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1931–58), 5.402, 5.407, 5.311.] Apel reads this not as a theory of scientific method but as a semiotic transformation of transcendental logic. In the paper given to the Third International Kant Congress in 1970 the 'highest point' of that logic is 'the "ultimate opinion" of the "indefinite community of investigators"', and the level at which the unity of knowledge is secured is fixed: 'The quasi-transcendental subject of this unity is the indefinite community of experimentation which is identical with the indefinite community of interpretation.'^[TTP 87–88. Chapter 3 of TTP is printed in Apel's own English, first published in L. W. Beck (ed.), *Proceedings of the Third International Kant Congress* (Dordrecht: Reidel, 1972).] Where Kant grounded the objectivity of knowledge in the transcendental unity of apperception, Peirce on Apel's reconstruction replaces 'the idea of transcendental consciousness as subject and warrant of objectively valid knowledge' with 'the idea of the indefinite community of sign interpreters whose consensus is the nontranscendable ultimate criterium, we can conceive of, for the intersubjective validity and thus for the truth of our knowledge'.^[Apel, 'C. S. Peirce and the Post-Tarskian Problem of an Adequate Explication of the Meaning of Truth: Towards a Transcendental-Pragmatic Theory of Truth, Part I', *The Monist* 63/3 (1980), 386–407, at 401; 'criterium' is the article's spelling. On 402 the real is conceived 'not as that which can be factually "known" but as the "knowable"', against the Kantian thing-in-itself. Apel names the position 'sense-critical realism' in Part II, *Transactions of the Charles S. Peirce Society* 18/1 (1982), 3–17, at 16 n. 50, where he defends it against the charge in *Knowledge and Human Interests* that Peirce's semiotic realism relapses into pre-Kantian metaphysics.] The substitution is a change in the kind of item which does the transcendental work: a synthesis performed by a subject is replaced by a relation obtaining within a community of interpretation, so that what was a condition on the unity of a consciousness becomes a condition on the determinacy of a sign.

The argument for the replacement is conditional, and its form carries what may be taken from it. 'If the human subject of truth-claims is conceived of not only in a Kantian way as subject of the pre-linguistic "synthesis of apperception," but moreover, in a transcendental-semiotical way, as subject of sign interpretation, then his truth-claims cannot, in principle, be sufficiently redeemed by the evidence of his own synthesis of apperception'; they must be redeemed by an intersubjectively valid synthesis of sign-interpretation, and 'this means that the methodical solipsism of modern epistemology from Descartes to Husserl has to be surmounted'.^[Apel, *Monist* I, 394; and 389 for the triadic structure of semiosis 'considered by Peirce as a semiotic equivalent of Kant's "synthesis of apperception"'.] The consequent is secure; the antecedent is what the programme has to earn, and Chapter Two §2.1.4 takes its warrant from the argument that a solitary rule-follower has no criterion.

The structural warrant beneath the substitution is the triadicity of the sign-function. Anything represented as something is represented by a sign, of an object, to an interpretant, and the interpretant is not a further sign appended to a self-standing dyad but that in virtue of which the dyad is a sign at all. An interpretant is in Peirce's account itself a sign, which calls for a further interpretant; the step from that series to a community of interpreters is Apel's transformation and not Peirce's semiotic alone. On that transformation the position of an interpretant is occupied, at the limit, by a possible assessor, and a representation without a possible interpretant is not an unassessed representation but no representation whatever. That is the semiotic form of the thesis the pre-moral reading requires.

Apel enters the restriction on this himself, in the sentence on which the present reading principally turns. From the postulate of the ultimate opinion, Peirce 'can deduce as transcendentally necessary not any propositions but the universal validity of synthetic inferences in the long run'; the propositions accepted at any given time are, on the same page, fallible and corrigible, and the convergence on which the construction depends is 'a convergence that must be postulated as normative'.^[TTP 88 and 105; TTP 90 for Peirce's logical socialism as 'a practical postulate of ethics'. These TTP loci lie beyond the portion of the Drive copy that could be checked, and are to be verified against the print edition.] Transcendental necessity is claimed for the validity of a method and withheld from the propositions the method yields. That is not yet the asymmetry between an enactment and a proposition about it, but it is its nearest Peircean analogue, and what the argument takes from it is that the counter-programme's founding text refuses transcendental standing to the propositions its practice yields. What follows for the counter-programme is a burden rather than a refutation: if the postulate secures the validity of the synthetic inferences of enquiry, the extension of the 'we' from those inferences to every validity claim, the normative with the theoretical, is a further step and not an entailment of the semiotic transformation. It is a step Apel takes, and Chapter Four examines what carries it.

The bearing on the premise is direct. If the sense of a validity claim, its being a determinate item an interlocutor may take rightly or wrongly rather than a noise, consists in its being the kind of thing an unlimited community of interpreters could in principle assess, then a putative claim withdrawn in principle from any such assessment lacks determinate content. It is not that its truth is merely undiscoverable; there is nothing determinate for the predicate 'true' to attach to. The point must be distinguished from verificationism, since the resemblance is superficial and the objection otherwise inevitable. The claim is not that a proposition is meaningless unless it can be verified. It is that a putative content withdrawn from any possible assessment by anyone at all is not a content on which verification could bear. The modality is 'in principle' and the community unlimited; nothing turns on the reach of any actual knowledge or on what any actual inquirer could establish.

From this the reciprocity of meaning §3.3.2 asserted is reached by a further step, on a route Γ.4 does not supply, and the step must be entered explicitly. The semiotic material yields the position of a possible assessor. That the positions so yielded are reciprocal, each member a potential assessor of any sign any other produces, is a claim about the constitution of the community of interpretation and not an entailment of triadicity, and it is on that claim, entered as a claim and not as a corollary, that a sign is held to have a determinate interpretant only under reciprocity. At the level of argumentative discourse that reciprocity of interpretive positions is the correlativity of speaker and hearer, and Habermas's 'recognition', on which the socio-ontology of the moral 'ought' rests, presupposes it.

Three notions must be held apart, since the secondary literature commonly conflates them and the argument requires only the weakest. The first is the community of interpretation as a condition of meaning: a sign has a determinate interpretant only relative to a space of reciprocal assessment. That, and that alone, is what the argument requires. The second is the ideal communication community as a regulative idea, the counterfactually anticipated community by reference to which real discourse is oriented and corrected, which does other work and is not examined here. The third is the ultimate consensus of inquiry as a theory of truth, the thesis that inquiry is fated to converge on a final opinion in which truth consists; it is not required, and it is expressly declined. The refusal of the third bears on the coherence of the thesis as a whole. Chapter One §1.5.6 established that the mature Habermasian epistemology rejects the epistemic conception of truth, and Habermas states the rejection in terms endorsed here: 'a proposition is agreed to by all rational subjects because it is true; it is not true because it could be the content of a consensus attained under ideal conditions'.^[TJ 101.] The transcendental-semiotic premise is compatible with that rejection and would be incompatible with the convergence theory it is commonly assumed to carry. One exegetical caution is owed against Apel himself. Peirce's final opinion, the regulative endpoint of inquiry, is not identical with the ultimate logical interpretant of the mature semiotic, the habit-change in which a chain of interpretants terminates, and Apel runs the two together in the loci consulted.^[CP 5.491 against 5.407; T. L. Short, *Peirce's Theory of Signs* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007).] The philosophical point survives the distinction, since both are constituted only relative to a community of potential interpreters, and it is that relativity, not the identity of the two notions, which the argument requires.

### §3.3.5 · The derivation question distinguished: Finlayson, the trilemma, and the order of grounding

The objection developed in these chapters must be held apart from the derivation objection which the anglophone literature has chiefly pressed against discourse ethics. Finlayson's charge has two limbs. The first is that the formal derivation of (U) which Habermas promised, from non-moral premises, cannot be executed as he sets it up. Finlayson reconstructs it from two premises, 'the normative, (but non-moral) preconditions of argumentation in general' and a 'weak idea of normative justification', observes that 'neither Habermas nor any of his followers have ever actually managed to derive (U) formally', and gives a reason why it could not be done: (U) is a biconditional which makes amenability to consensus on the basis of interests a necessary and a sufficient condition of validity, whereas (D) states a necessary condition only; and were the derivation produced from premises whose normative status is not that of moral obligation, it 'would itself appear to fall foul of Hume's law'.^[Finlayson, 'The Persistence of Normative Questions' (2013), 523–24; and 'Modernity and Morality in Habermas's Discourse Ethics', *Inquiry* 43/3 (2000), 319–40, at 320–21, for the biconditional argument and for the second limb.] The second limb is that Habermas's later recourse to justifying (U) abductively makes the moral theory dependent on an empirical theory of modernisation. The evidential verdict is shared so far as it is evidential: no formal derivation stands executed, and the abduction depends on modernisation theory at least to the extent Habermas's own supplement clause concedes (Chapter One §1.5.5). Whether the dependence exceeds that concession is not adjudicated here. What is not shared is the significance of the verdict, because the two objections are directed at different objects.

The derivation whose non-execution Finlayson reports would be the derivation of a principle from premises, the deduction of a sentence from sentences, and that is grounding in the sense Albert's trilemma presupposes. Apel's reconstruction of 1975 fixes what the trilemma requires in order to be deduced at all: it 'can only be logically deduced for sentences of an axiomatized sentence-system in the sense of the syntactic-semantical construction of a so-called "formal language"', a deduction 'only possible under prior abstraction from the pragmatic dimension of argumentative language use'; and 'the logical deduction of sentences from sentences is not itself the grounding of the validity of knowledge'.^[Apel, 'Fundamental-Grounding' (1975), 248–49.] The transcendental-pragmatic exposure of the presuppositions, by contrast, 'proves to be the philosophical fundamental-grounding in a nondeductive way insofar as its paradigmatic evidence is precisely of that kind which can neither be called into question by criticism without self-contradiction nor be grounded deductively without presupposing itself'.^[Ibid., 262; and 264: 'If I cannot challenge something without actual self-contradiction and cannot deductively ground it without formal-logical *petitio principii*, then that thing belongs precisely to those transcendental-pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation which one must always have accepted'.] The critique of deduction as a form of grounding is therefore Apel's, directed against formal-logical systems of grounding and not against Habermas.

The consequence for the derivation question is determinate. That (U) has not been deduced from the presuppositions is no finding about the presuppositions, since a presupposition of that kind is not, under the abstraction a deduction of sentences from sentences requires, the sort of premise from which such a deduction takes its warrant. A valid inference transmits the standing its premises already have; a derivation therefore grounds its conclusion only so far as that standing has been secured elsewhere, and the question of the standing is prior to the derivation in the order of grounding. Finlayson himself separates 'the broader question of the justification of (U)' from 'the narrower question of the formal-logical derivation of (U)', and grants for the sake of argument a satisfactory justification of his first premise.^[Finlayson, 'Modernity and Morality' (2000), 325 and 321.] What is added here is that the standing of the presuppositions is prior to both questions, and that it is decided, if at all, at the level §3.3.1–§3.3.2 occupy and not at the level of moral theory.

The independence runs in both directions. Were a valid derivation of (U) produced, counterfactually on Finlayson's argument, the pre-moral question would remain untouched, since it concerns not the passage from the presuppositions to a moral principle but the constitution of the presuppositions as validity-conferring, which any such derivation would already presuppose in order to count as an argument. Conversely, the derivation might fail for reasons having nothing to do with the constitution of validity, and its failure would not establish the pre-moral thesis. There is nonetheless a relation of another kind. Habermas holds that the moral 'ought' is not to be had from the presuppositions of discourse and must be imported from the lifeworld; once it must be imported, its principle must be recovered from the historical materials into which it was imported, which is what the genealogical supplement supplies and the abductive proposal requires. Finlayson reads the abductive turn as a retreat; this chapter reads it as a consequence of the architecture. Two disciplinary points bound what is claimed. The chapter does not claim to have solved the derivation problem or to have superseded Finlayson's result, which brackets the standing of the presuppositions as not in dispute between him and Habermas. And it keeps apart the three justifications which the literature runs together, Apel's ultimate grounding by strict reflection, Habermas's formal derivation of 1983, and the abductive proposal of 1996: an objection which defeats one does not thereby defeat the others, since they differ in their premises and in the modality each claims.

### §3.3.6 · The scope of the *Aufweis*: first stage and second stage

The chapter's position on what the *elenchos* reaches converts the dispute from a quarrel about strength into a question of scope. The term of art is *Aufweis*, an exhibition rather than a proof: a showing of what an act has already put into effect, as against a derivation of a conclusion from premises. The first stage is that no determinate signification is possible except under conditions which the denier enacts in denying; this the *elenchos* secures, subject to the retorsion of §3.3.2 and its declared limits. The second stage is that those conditions include the specific reciprocal norms the rules of discourse articulate, symmetry, equal entitlement and non-coercion, rather than some thinner requirement of assessability.^[Equal entitlement and non-coercion are the rules Habermas reproduces from Alexy at MCCA 89; 'symmetry' is his own word for the general conditions at MCCA 88.] This the *elenchos* does not secure, and the second stage is not an extension of the first but a distinct thesis with its own burden of proof. A terminological guard is needed: Apel describes discourse ethics as a *Zwei-Stufen-Ethik*, its first stage the philosophically grounded formal-procedural principle and its second the delegation of substantive norm-justification to practical discourses.^[DV 271.] His two stages and the two distinguished here are homonyms; his second begins where grounding ends, whereas the second here lies inside the question of what the grounding reaches.

The verdict is on what the Aristotelian base delivers, a base pitched at signification, and not on Apel's own route. His *Aufweis* proceeds from serious argumentation and claims the norms directly. Among the normative conditions of argumentative discourse in general, he holds, 'there are also the transcendental, and hence a priori valid, principles of discourse ethics', and the first of them is that 'All possible discourse partners must acknowledge each other as having equal rights in representing their interests by arguments'; the second, declared co-original with it, is equal co-responsibility for identifying and solving the problems of the lifeworld through argumentative discourse.^[ML 47–48. The same claims stand in German at DV 233 and 271, and in English at Apel, 'How to Ground a Universalistic Ethics' (1993), 22.] He also states the criterion the claim must satisfy: 'Letztlich muß sich erweisen lassen, daß jeder Versuch einer Bestreitung der angedeuteten Regel-Präsuppositionen notwendigerweise in einen performativen Selbstwiderspruch des Bestreiters führt'.^[DV 233. The modal verb announces a demonstration owed and not one executed, and the test is therefore Apel's own.] The test can be run at once.

Run on the denial made within discourse, it succeeds on the strong construal. A participant who asserts to his interlocutors that they lack equal entitlement to assess what he says solicits, in asserting it, an assessment he simultaneously declares them unentitled to render, and the withdrawal is performative and not a contradiction between propositions. On the minimal construal, on which assertion solicits understanding and not assessment, the test does not bite. Where the test stops is at the constitution of the class. It catches the contester who, arguing with given interlocutors, denies their entitlement; it does not catch one who restricts admission, maintaining of some third party that no argument need be had with him, since the restricter performs no speech act towards the excluded and a performative test has no performance on which to operate. Apel's formulations quantify over 'all possible discourse partners', and it is at that quantifier that the result stops: what the enactment secures is entitlement towards whomever one argues with, and that the class of possible partners is closed under something stronger than actual address is a further claim. Apel marks a boundary of the same kind at an adjacent joint, conceding that his refutation of the sceptic 'has only — indeed — shown that the a priori presupposition of argumentative discourse in philosophy is non-circumventible', and that this insight 'does not show that the paradigm of a discursive settlement of moral problems can be always applied to the life world'.^[ML 81–82. The concession concerns application and not admission, and the two joints are not identified; it establishes in Apel's voice that the reach of the reflective foundation and that of the discursive paradigm are distinct questions.]

The co-responsibility so secured must be classified before it is used. A requirement is moral without qualification only if it binds the agent as such, and not solely the occupant of a role the agent may decline. Within argumentative discourse the requirement is categorical in form, borne by the participant as participant and not on a revisable preference, so that Kettner's practice of calling the discourse-constitutive responsibilities moral marks something real; it becomes moral in scope only if occupancy of the participant role is itself unavoidable in the required sense, which is the second stage.^[Kettner, 'Discourse Ethics: Apel, Habermas, and Beyond' (2006), 303–4; Apel himself calls the pair 'fundamental moral norms' of an ideal communication community. The criterion applied here does not deny the predication; it restricts what the predication licenses.] It is in either case no instance of (U)'s requirement that norms be universalisable, which no part of the second stage asserts.

Read as a quarrel about strength, Apel claiming more necessity and Habermas less, the dispute is irresoluble, because the two boundaries in play are not drawn on one axis. Apel's boundary runs between grounding and application, placing the presuppositions and the moral principle on the apodictic side and the justification of situational norms on the corrigible side; the boundary drawn in these chapters runs between enactment and articulation and sorts by kind. A concession of extent by either party therefore leaves the disagreement where it stood. Both parties concede the phenomenon, that whoever argues stands under presuppositions he cannot coherently deny; what divides them is what a propositional articulation of the phenomenon can carry, and the strength-framing obscures that question, inviting only assertion and counter-assertion. Read as a quarrel about scope, it becomes a question with a determinate burden on each side. Apel must show that the conditions of determinate signification include reciprocal assessability in the rich sense, that to signify to another is already to address an assessor entitled to symmetrical standing. His burden falls into two steps: a quantificational step, that no specification of intelligibility adequate to individuate a content can be given without ranging over possible assessors; and an entitlement step, that the assessor position cannot be specified without entitlement. Quantification over an assessor is necessary for the rich condition and not sufficient, and the weight falls on the second step, which Γ.4 does not supply. The demonstration belongs to the theory of meaning and not to moral theory, which is one reason the dispute has proved intractable in the venue it has occupied. Habermas's burden is of the opposite logical shape, existential where Apel's is universal: it would be discharged by the exhibition of a practice, actual or coherently describable, in which determinate content is individuated and assessed without symmetry, equal entitlement and non-coercion. Hierarchical and coercive practices of signification suggest themselves, but a candidate must survive the argument of Chapter One §1.4.1, that a coercive or deceptive use is parasitic on the serious use it counterfeits. That argument forecloses the easy candidate, a coercive practice offered as though it owed nothing to the infrastructure it exploits, and leaves open whether the infrastructure so presupposed contains the full reciprocal package or stops at bare intelligibility.

The objection that the reformulation defers rather than advances is the one on which the principal result of the chapter stands or falls: a dispute relabelled is not a dispute settled. The reply turns on one distinction. A question is underdetermined when its framing generates nothing on which an argument could bear; it is undecided when an argument of specified form and identified subject-matter could bear on it and has not yet been brought. The strength-framing was underdetermined. The scope-framing is undecided: Apel's burden falls to a result in the theory of meaning and Habermas's to the exhibition of a case, each an argument someone could set about constructing, and Apel's direct route has already been brought to a first test whose divided result shows what deciding looks like once it is begun. Two consequences follow, and Chapter Four inherits both. Apel is right that the first stage is secured, within the limits §3.3.2 declared, and that within discourse equal standing as assessor is secured for participants among themselves. Habermas is right that the second stage is not thereby secured, and right for a reason his own texts supply, since the minimal construal of the claim to intelligibility is a live alternative which the strong reading argues against but does not defeat. Neither is right that the disagreement concerns how strongly the presuppositions bind. It concerns how far the exhibition reaches, and the open question is not whether the norms bind those who argue, since they do, but with whom argument must be had, and in what modality that 'must' holds.

A final consequence passes to Chapter Four. So long as the second stage is neither secured nor refuted, the deficit in the practical form Chapter Four gives it, which rests on the availability of an exit from discourse, tells against Habermas's architecture and is not a neutral datum. Apel denies the exit by asserting the second stage, and his statement is scoped to the questioner: whoever enters the ground of discourse with a serious question 'unterstellt nicht nur automatisch die Gleichberechtigung aller virtuellen Diskurspartner, sondern auch so etwas wie eine Solidarität der Problemlösungsverantwortung'.^[DV 451.] Extending the scope from the questioner to the agent as such is what the dropout question turns on, the dropout escaping or being held according as the extension stands or falls. Whether the deficit is a feature of the framework or of the human situation therefore turns on the second-stage question, which is declared open here and not left open by inadvertence.

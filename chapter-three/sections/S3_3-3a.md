## §3.3 · Reflexive Irrefutability: The Elenctic Disclosure of the Conditions of Meaning

### §3.3.1 · Why the investigation must proceed elenctically and not demonstratively

If what is in question is the constitution of discursive validity as such, that constitution cannot be established by derivation, since any derivation would be conducted in argument and would presuppose the conditions at issue. Nor can it be established by reconstruction in Habermas's sense alone: a fallible reconstruction leaves the status of the presuppositions where the self-referential difficulty found it, and the correction it invites cannot reach the modal claim which does the philosophical work, the two tests a reconstruction commands reaching content and extension and not modality (Chapter Two §2.1.2). What remains is a third procedure, neither deductive nor merely reconstructive, and Aristotle assigns the first principles to a treatment of that kind.

The problem the procedure answers is older than the trilemma usually taken to state it. In the *Posterior Analytics* demonstrative knowledge proceeds from premises which are true, primary and immediate, prior to and better known than the conclusion, and Aristotle refuses the two positions which the impossibility of demonstrating everything seems to force: that there is no knowledge at all, since the premises would have to be demonstrated in their turn without end, and that everything is demonstrable, since demonstration may proceed in a circle. The regress is arrested at what is known otherwise than by demonstration, and circular demonstration is denied on the ground that it would make the same things at once prior and posterior to the same things.^[*Posterior Analytics* I.2, 71b20–33; I.3, 72b5–24, with the ground of the second refusal at 72b25–28.] The three positions so arrayed, regress, circle, and a halt at what is not demonstrated, are the horns of the trilemma Albert deduces against every claim to sufficient grounding, and the descent is not a construction of this chapter. Apel, reconstructing Albert's argument in 1975, holds that the trilemma 'can itself be understood as a complete explication of the problematic of axioms that Aristotle had pointed out and that, from the beginning, has posed the problem of fundamental-grounding', the problem having turned since Aristotle on the circumstance 'that logico-mathematical arguments can ground neither the truth of their premises nor the validity of the rules of proof'.^[Apel, 'Fundamental-Grounding' (1975), 244 and 243; his n. 15 (270) quotes *Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006a6–18, as the illustration of the classical problem, and his n. 17 refers to *An. Post.* I.2, 71b20 ff. Albert's three alternatives are rendered at 240.] The modern form differs from the ancient in one respect: where Aristotle arrests the regress at principles known by a faculty other than demonstration, the critical rationalist counts every such arrest as an arbitrary suspension of the demand for grounds.^[Hans Albert, *Traktat über kritische Vernunft*, ch. 1, §2. The older trilemma of dogmatism, infinite regress and psychologism, which Popper reports from Fries, stands beside Albert's and not within Apel's reconstruction: Karl Popper, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (London: Hutchinson, 1959), §25.] The refusal of that count does not require the faculty Aristotle invoked, since the halt it exhibits is a performance and not a piece of intuited knowledge.

Aristotle's assignment of the first principles to a procedure other than demonstration is his own statement and not an inference from his practice. Dialectic is useful, beyond training and beyond the encounters of ordinary discussion, in relation to the first principles of the sciences: since it is impossible to say anything about them from the principles proper to the science in question, the principles being first of all, it is by way of what is acceptable that one must treat of them, a task Aristotle calls most appropriate to dialectic and grounds in its capacity to examine.^[*Topics* I.2, 101a36–b4, in *Aristotle: Topics Books I and VIII*, trans. Robin Smith (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997), 2–3. The strength of the assignment is disputed; what is taken here is the weaker and undisputed reading, that the indemonstrable principles are approached examinatively and not demonstratively.] The office is examinative and not probative: what dialectic does with a principle is to test it, not to establish it. Γ.4 stands in that office and comes by its material otherwise than the *Topics* contemplates, not as an opinion the respondent concedes when asked but as an act he performs in speaking at all, and it is that difference in the eliciting which carries the escape from the trilemma. That the office survives the difference is not assumed: *On Sophistical Refutations* makes the art of examination a department of dialectic and takes its material from what an answerer supplies rather than from what a questioner has selected, so that a procedure drawing on the respondent alone remains within the office the *Topics* assigns.^[*On Sophistical Refutations* 5, 167a23–27, for the definition of a refutation as a deduction of the contradictory of the respondent's thesis from premises he has granted, the point at issue itself excluded; 8, 169b25–27, and 11, 171b4–6, for examination as a department of dialectic; quoted from E. S. Forster's Loeb rendering (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1955). The definition does not deny the procedure premises; it fixes their provenance.]

Three ancestries present themselves for the procedure, and only one discharges the office required. The Socratic *elenchos* of the early dialogues is an aporetic examination of an interlocutor which yields aporia and no principle, so that an enquiry which took it for its model would have to represent as an achievement what its source presents as an impasse.^[Enrico Berti, 'Ancient Greek Dialectic as an Expression of Freedom of Thought and Speech', *Journal of the History of Ideas* 39/3 (1978), 347–70, on the Socratic elenchos as exhibiting the contradiction between the thesis an interlocutor advances and the ensemble of beliefs he holds, without supplying a procedure for transcending it. Berti's reading of the *Metaphysics* as a working-through of ἀπορίαι, with Β.1 (995a27–33) stating the method in Aristotle's own voice, is taken under his name; the departure entered below, that the examination which reaches the principle need not proceed from ἔνδοξα, is a departure from him.] The dialectic of the *Topics* proceeds from ἔνδοξα, which are premises, and a procedure operating on premises the questioner has advanced is exposed to the trilemma the restoration was to escape. The Aristotelian defence of the principle of non-contradiction in *Metaphysics* Γ.4 is the third procedure and the one the pre-moral question requires. It begins, as the Socratic does, from an interlocutor rather than from axioms; what it takes from him is an act and not a thesis; and what it exhibits is not the incoherence of his particular beliefs but what any determinate claim whatever must already enact, such that the denial withdraws the conditions of its own intelligibility.

Γ does not state one principle but three, and the modern discussion has turned on which of them the *elenchos* defends. Łukasiewicz's tripartition remains the point of departure: an ontological formulation, that 'the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject in the same respect' (Γ.3, 1005b19–20); a psychological formulation, that it is impossible for anyone to believe the same thing to be and not to be (Γ.3, 1005b23–24); and a logical formulation, that 'contradictory statements are not at the same time true' (Γ.6, 1011b13–14).^[Jan Łukasiewicz, 'Über den Satz des Widerspruchs bei Aristoteles' (1910), trans. as 'On the Principle of Contradiction in Aristotle', *Review of Metaphysics* 24 (1971), 485–509. His own ranking makes the ontological formulation primary, 'the principle of contradiction κατ' ἐξοχήν', and he argues that there is reason to doubt whether the three are logically equivalent; his verdict, that the principle is left unjustified in that formulation, is not adopted. The Greek of Γ throughout this chapter is quoted from W. D. Ross (ed.), *Aristotle's Metaphysics*, 2 vols (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1924); Christopher Kirwan, *Aristotle: Metaphysics Books Γ, Δ, Ε*, 2nd edn (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993), is used as commentary.] It is often supposed that the tripartition licenses a deflationary reading on which the *elenchos* defends the psychological principle, so that it establishes only how minds constituted as ours are constrained. That is not Łukasiewicz's own ranking, and the reading advanced here takes over his ordering and no more. What Γ.4 exhibits is neither a law of belief nor a law of inference but a condition of signification, and the ontological formulation is reached through it rather than beside it. The claim, entered as the chapter's own, is that the exclusion which makes a term determinate is the exclusion the ontological principle states of things, so that the register in which the argument is conducted is semantic-pragmatic and its object is ontological. The identity is argued at §3.3.2, from what an assessor must be able to take a term to have set aside, and is not read off the text.

Within that register Aristotle is explicit that the principle admits of no demonstration in the ordinary sense. The demand for a demonstration of everything is made through want of education (ἀπαιδευσία), 'for not to know of what things one may demand demonstration, and of what one may not, argues simply want of education'; and demonstration of everything is in any case impossible, since the regress would leave no demonstration at all. What is available in its place is refutative demonstration, ἀποδεῖξαι ἐλεγκτικῶς, possible provided only that the disputant says something; and Aristotle gives the ground of the difference himself: one who demonstrates would seem to be begging the point at issue, whereas where another is responsible for the assumption there is refutation and not demonstration.^[*Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006a5–18: τὸ δ' ἐλεγκτικῶς ἀποδεῖξαι λέγω διαφέρειν καὶ τὸ ἀποδεῖξαι, ὅτι ἀποδεικνύων μὲν ἂν δόξειεν αἰτεῖσθαι τὸ ἐν ἀρχῇ, ἄλλου δὲ τοῦ τοιούτου αἰτίου ὄντος ἔλεγχος ἂν εἴη καὶ οὐκ ἀπόδειξις (1006a15–18, Ross). The clause ἄλλου δὲ τοῦ τοιούτου αἰτίου ὄντος divides the translations: the Revised Oxford Translation construes the other party as responsible for the assumption, Lawson-Tancred (*Aristotle: The Metaphysics*, London: Penguin, 1998) as advancing a principle of his own. The two agree that the burden falls on the respondent and differ on what makes it fall there; the body follows the first, and on the second the procedure is available wherever the opponent advances any principle whatever, which widens its reach and does not narrow it.] The whole defensibility of the procedure rests on that distinction. A demonstration of a principle would have to take the principle among its premises; a refutation takes its premises from the respondent, so that the cause of the result lies with him and not with the one conducting it. The distinction is therefore not between an argument with premises and an argument without, but between an assumption the questioner advances and an act the respondent performs in speaking; and it is the second which escapes the trilemma, since the trilemma is a demand on what an arguer asserts, and what is worked on here is not asserted but done. What is claimed is accordingly narrower than the absence of premises. Aristotle does put forward propositions in his own voice in the course of Γ.4, that 'to be' has a definite meaning, that 'man' shall be taken to mean 'two-footed animal', and draws consequences from them; what he does not do, and says he does not do, is take as a premise the principle at issue. The stipulations he does advance are dischargeable, since the significations of a term can be severally named and the argument re-run on each; and the material the refutation works on is what the denier has already done in signifying, the elucidations being explications of that material and not premises substituted for it.

What is asked of the respondent is correspondingly small. The starting-point is not to require him to say that something is or is not, but that he should signify something (σημαίνειν γέ τι) both to himself and to another, for this is necessary if he is to say anything at all; one who refuses to signify anything whatever has removed himself from discourse, being in this respect like a plant (φυτόν).^[*Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006a13–22.] The step from that minimal demand to the exclusion of contradiction is the contested joint of the argument in the modern literature. What Aristotle requires is not merely that the disputant signify something (σημαίνειν τι) but that he signify one thing (σημαίνειν ἕν), and the force is carried by the second: τὸ γὰρ μὴ ἓν σημαίνειν οὐθὲν σημαίνειν ἐστίν, not to signify one thing is to signify nothing.^[*Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006b7 (Ross); the stipulation is introduced at 1006a31 ff. On the τι/ἕν transit as the contested joint see Michael V. Wedin, 'Aristotle on the Firmness of the Principle of Non-Contradiction', *Phronesis* 49 (2004), 225–65, and Paula Gottlieb, 'Aristotle on Non-contradiction', *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (2023).] The apparatus by which Aristotle secures the stronger demand is more determinate than the summary formula suggests. Signifying one thing is glossed by an essence-formula, and the gloss blocks the obvious evasion: an opponent may propose that his term carries several significations, and Aristotle answers that this is admissible only if they are finite in number and severally determinate, for a term may be given a distinct name for each signification and the argument re-run on each; if the significations are not finite, discourse is destroyed, since where a name does not signify one thing it signifies nothing, and where names signify nothing, reasoning with one another is done away with (ἀνῄρηται τὸ διαλέγεσθαι πρὸς ἀλλήλους), and in truth with oneself as well.^[*Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006a28–b13; the consequence at 1006b5–9, καὶ πρὸς αὑτόν (b9) being an a fortiori extension and not an ordering, so that no inference about the priority of address over solitary thought is drawn from the sentence. Kirwan, notes ad loc., treats the finite-significations concession as the move on which the argument turns.] The unity-condition is thus not left as the stipulation it is first entered as, but reached by exhausting the alternatives to it, and what it protects is the possibility of two parties addressing one another at all.

That the condition is stated for address as well as for thought matters to the retrieval, and its weight must be fixed exactly. Nothing in the sentence orders the two; the ground given for both losses is the impossibility of thinking without thinking one thing. What the retrieval takes from Γ.4 is therefore not a priority of address over thought but the form of the procedure: a refutation requires a respondent who supplies its material, and what he supplies is an act of signifying to another. The condition is dialogical in its eliciting before it is dialogical in Apel, and what the passage removes is the assumption on which the objection to the second transit is usually built, that Apel begins from a monological condition and adds interlocutors to it. What Apel adds, and what remains contestable, is the passage from address to the norms governing it. Reciprocity of address is not yet symmetry, equal entitlement or freedom from coercion, and nothing in Γ.4 supplies those.

Between the two demands falls the step at which the argument has been challenged from Łukasiewicz onward: an opponent may concede that his utterance signifies without conceding that it signifies unitarily, so that what presented itself as a refutation proves to be a demand for a concession the opponent is entitled to withhold. The defence of the step turns on what a name is for. A term whose signification were not unitary would fix no exclusion; a term fixing no exclusion supplies a hearer with nothing he can have taken correctly or incorrectly; and what admits of no correct or incorrect uptake is not a name but a noise which two parties happen to produce in each other's presence. The criterion is one of uptake and not of truth: a λόγος which is not truth-apt is one an interlocutor may still take rightly or wrongly. Unity of signification is therefore not a further condition laid on signifying but what signifying is, and the opponent who grants that he means something has granted it, since what he granted was that his utterance is available to an assessor as this rather than that. That construal has an ancient provenance which bears on the *petitio* worry. The compressed passage at 1006a28–31 admits two readings. On the first, a name must signify either to be X or not to be X and not both, which presupposes the exclusion it is meant to establish; on the second, originating with Alexander of Aphrodisias, the weight falls not on the object signified but on the pragmatic act of signifying, a word, to be meaningful at all, having to signify something determinate and being unable to signify its own negation without annihilating its semantic function.^[Alexander of Aphrodisias, *In Aristotelis Metaphysica commentaria*, ed. M. Hayduck, CAG I (Berlin: Reimer, 1891), in the English of Arthur Madigan, *Alexander of Aphrodisias: On Aristotle Metaphysics 4* (London: Duckworth, 1993). The attribution rests on Inna Kupreeva, 'Alexander of Aphrodisias on the Principle of Non-Contradiction: The Argument "from Signification"', in Melina G. Mouzala (ed.), *Ancient Greek Dialectic and Its Reception* (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2023), 287–330, the dedicated modern study; its consistency with *De Interpretatione* 3, 16b22–25, has been questioned there.] It is the second reading this chapter takes over, and taking it over is what allows the exhibition to advance no premise of the questioner's own. The defence is conditional on that construal of naming; the construal is contested; and it is entered as argued and not as secured.

One further boundary is drawn by Aristotle before it is drawn in these chapters, and the categorial premise of §3.2.1 draws on it. *De Interpretatione* 4 predicates significance of every λόγος and restricts truth and falsity to the assertoric sub-class, a prayer being a λόγος which is neither true nor false; determinacy of meaning and truth-aptness are not coextensive, and significance is the wider property.^[*De Interpretatione* 4, 16b26–17a7.] Nothing follows, from an item's being determinate, about whether it is truth-apt, so that the mode of individuation proper to it must be settled independently of its determinacy. For the enactment it is settled by the act-typology of the *Prior Analytics*, and settled narrowly: the premise secured by asking a question, which Aristotle calls dialectical, is distinguished from the premise secured by taking it as given, which he calls demonstrative, and the distinction shows that asking and taking are two acts, not that one item's truth-aptness alters with how it is entered, which would make force a constituent of content.^[*Prior Analytics* A.1, 24a22–28; W. D. Ross, *Aristotle's Prior and Posterior Analytics* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1949), 287. The invariance of content across force is the double structure of Chapter One §1.2.1.] The enactment is accordingly individuated by the undoing a performed denial would effect, not by the truth-conditions of any proposition about it. The amendment made under Derrida's pressure at Chapter Two §2.3.2 is received in the form §3.2.2 grounds it: the enactment is individuated by a recognisability sufficient for incoherence to be exhibited, relative to the exchange in which it is exhibited. The passage from an irrefutability so bounded to one no exchange bounds is made at §3.3.2 by the retorsion alone.

Three features of the procedure answer three objections. First, the refutation is not a demonstration of the principle's truth: it derives the principle from no premise the questioner has assumed, and what it clarifies is that the denier, in denying, presupposes what he denies. What is claimed is not that the charge of *petitio principii* is answered but that it does not arise, since a *petitio* is a fault in an inference and the exhibition advances none. Secondly, the refutation is *ad hominem* in the classical, non-pejorative sense: an argument drawn from the interlocutor's own discursive commitments rather than from external axioms, requiring only that the disputant speak. This is the form of argument the pre-moral question demands, since any premise the analysis might lay down would itself stand in need of the constitution it is trying to exhibit. Thirdly, the *elenchos* here does not accompany ontological inquiry from without but co-produces it, in the sense Β.1 fixes: the conditions are not laid down and then defended; they are drawn out of the act of signifying by exhibiting the incoherence of their denial. A procedure whose yield is negative may nonetheless be productive, and the doctrine is Aristotle's own: for those who wish to get clear of difficulties 'it is advantageous to state the difficulties well', since 'it is not possible to untie a knot which one does not know'.^[*Metaphysics* Β.1, 995a27–33, in the Revised Oxford Translation, ed. Jonathan Barnes (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1984), vol. 2, 1572.] The negative moment fixes determinate conditions on any successor account, since the conditions under which the refuted position failed bind whatever is to stand in its place; that is the sense in which the retrieval is a method-ontology, and not a technique of refutation alone.

The retrieval is Apel's, and he states it in his own texts, with a distinction that must be carried. In the Mercier Lectures he presents transcendental reflection on the non-circumventible presuppositions of thought as 'the only alternative to grounding by deduction', and distinguishes the dialogue-related *elenchos* from the merely incidental *tu quoque*: the latter holds 'only incidentally, i.e. at the occasion of its use', convicting this opponent of this inconsistency, whereas the former compels the opponent to the insight that what he would deny belongs to the presuppositions of his own arguing, so that a practised denial would oblige him to break off the argument altogether.^[ML 42. The argument, Apel adds, 'presupposes a sincere cooperation of the possible opponent', which he holds to be itself an ethical presupposition of argumentation; the modality is accordingly conditional and not a compulsion.] A *tu quoque* is a debating manoeuvre and yields nothing about the structure of argument as such, and a reception which reads transcendental-pragmatic argument as an elaborate *tu quoque* has mistaken the weaker instrument for the stronger. Two cautions in Apel's own hand govern the use made of him here. In the same lecture he sets his procedure apart from Aristotle's 'indirect proof', which he takes to presuppose definitions and so to remain exposed to a *petitio*, and suspects that Aristotle 'did not clearly see the difference between the propositional contradiction and the performative contradiction'; elsewhere he roots his method in 'the so-called "indirect proof" (elenchos) found in Plato and Aristotle', and the two statements are not reconciled in his texts.^[ML 42–43; FTSPV 94. Apel disclaims any claim that his interpretation 'exactly covers Aristotle's self-understanding' (ML 43).] What the chapter claims is accordingly that the form of argument Aristotle practises in Γ.4, on the pragmatic-act reading, is the form the pre-moral question requires; it does not claim that Aristotle possessed the transcendental-pragmatic concept of it, and it takes the object of the exhibition to be, as Apel says, a doing and not a proposition, which is why the exhibition incurs no *petitio*.

### §3.3.2 · The principle of non-contradiction as a condition of signification, and the answer to Stroud

At its first and most general level the *elenchos* discloses not a moral norm but a condition of determinacy of meaning, and here the pre-moral thesis acquires its ground. The clause displayed above withholds signification from a term which does not signify one thing, and the argument built on it holds that a name meaning F and not-F indifferently would fix no determinate content, so that determinate signification is possible only where contradiction is excluded. Signifying is accordingly more than vocalisation and more than the attachment of a predicate to a subject. It is an act of setting-apart: what a thing is comes into view only through the setting aside of what it is not, and the dyadic structure of affirmation and negation on which that setting-aside rests belongs to λόγος and to what is together rather than being laid on discourse from outside. In this register the principle functions as something discourse operates under rather than arrives at. No discursive act establishes it, since an act taking it as a premise would assume at the outset what was to be established, and every discursive act has already exercised it. It is therefore the condition of there being determinate content to affirm or deny, and so the condition of there being anything for the moral question ever to be about.

The order of grounding distinguishes the retrieval from a logical reading of Γ. On the logical reading the principle is the first law of inference, and its defence is a defence of the possibility of reasoning; on the semantic-pragmatic reading it is the first condition of signifying, and the possibility of reasoning is a consequence rather than the object of the defence. The difference decides what the *elenchos* can reach. A defence of inference reaches only those who infer; a defence of signification reaches anyone who means anything at all. It is the second reach the pre-moral question requires, since the question is what conditions govern the constitution of a validity claim and not what conditions govern the passage from one claim to another.

The transition from this to the reciprocity of meaning is the contestable step, and the point at which Apel and Habermas divide. A determinate validity claim is a claim raised as assessable by a competent interlocutor. Assessability of this kind requires the correlative position of one entitled to affirm or deny on the basis of reasons. The elenctic claim is that this correlativity is not a normative requirement laid on signification but a condition of it: just as signifying one thing is the condition of a term's having sense, so the reciprocal positions of speaker and hearer are the condition of an utterance's having the sense of a claim at all, as distinct from an emission of force. The order of Apel's four presuppositions (§3.1.4) is taken as corroboration, against his own gloss: the claim to sharing an intersubjectively valid meaning stands first, and the reciprocal address is built into that meaning-constitutive presupposition and not added at the fourth.

This is the second of two transits, the first having been entered at §3.3.1 at the level of the term. The *elenchos* of Γ.4 secures determinacy of meaning, the interlocutor must signify one thing, and it is a further and substantive step from conditions of determinate meaning to the reciprocal norms of symmetry, equal entitlement and non-coercion. Aristotle's condition is minimal: the denier need only mean one thing, not enter a symmetrical practice of reason-giving. The transit to the richer conditions is Apel's addition, and it is the locus at which Apel and Habermas divide over the claim to intelligibility, Apel construing it strongly and Habermas minimally, as semantic-syntactic intelligibility distinct from the normative presuppositions (Chapter One §1.1).^[On the divergence over the claim to intelligibility (*Sinnverständlichkeit*) see Linda Lovelli, 'Why be moral? On the ultimate ground and the ultimate sense of the "moral point of view" (with and beyond Apel)', *Topologik* 26 (2019–20), 89–96, at 91 n. 10, citing Apel, *Auseinandersetzungen*, 689–90 n. 55, which is the textual locus of the dispute.] The argument for the strong reading is that assessability by a competent other cannot be specified without the reciprocal positions, so that the minimal condition, pressed, already contains the reciprocity in nuce; and the pressing turns on a claim the chapter enters as its own and not as Aristotle's, that the other to whom one signifies at Γ.4 is one who may take what is signified rightly or wrongly, and so an assessor. Whether the pressing succeeds is the pivot of the dispute, and it is presented as such, the strong reading argued and the minimal reading acknowledged as the live alternative. The two transits have one form: from signifying something to signifying one thing, and from determinate signification to the reciprocal norms, a minimal condition is in each case pressed to yield a richer one, and in each case the pressing is argued and does not compel. Nothing transmits to the second transit the ground granted to the first, and §3.3.6 records the second stage as a thesis of its own under a burden this section does not lift.

One consequence must be stated now, because the chapter's result against Habermas depends on it. Habermas does not deny the first stage. That determinate meaning requires the exclusion of contradiction is not what separates him from Apel, and a result confined to the first stage is not a result against him. The modal yield stated below is therefore a result against Habermas only on the strong construal of the intelligibility claim, and the chapter's negative conclusions at §3.4.1 are conditional on that construal. The condition is avowed rather than concealed, since concealing it would let the chapter claim as established what it has only argued.

The modal yield may now be stated and defended. The absence of an alternative to the reciprocal conditions of meaning is not the absence of a functional equivalent among the forms of life so far known, which is the bound §3.2.4 finds Habermas's formulation to fix; it is, on the strong construal, the impossibility of signifying determinately while denying them. This faces the objection Chapter Two §2.1.4 states in Stroud's name, that a transcendental argument establishes how things must be thought and not how they are, so that its necessity is that of a conceptual scheme and collapses into anthropological unavoidability.^[Barry Stroud, 'Transcendental Arguments', *Journal of Philosophy* 65 (1968), 241–56; the formulation in the body is the form the objection has settled into and not Stroud's wording.] Unmet, the objection dissolves the distinction on which the chapter depends. The objection has a second and more exacting form, Körner's, which grants that some conditions are necessary for experience or for meaning and denies that their uniqueness can be established a priori.^[Stephan Körner, 'The Impossibility of Transcendental Deductions', *The Monist* 51/3 (1967), 317–31.] It is not answered by the argument below and is not meant to be. It is met, so far as it is met, by the reformulation at §3.3.6, which displaces the demand for uniqueness rather than discharging it, since what it settles is the scope of the conditions disclosed and not their singularity.

The reply to Stroud takes the form of a retorsion: the exhibition that a denial presupposes what it denies, so that the denying performance is turned back on its own conditions. The standing objection to retorsive argument in the anglophone literature must be named, since the instrument is the chapter's own. Stern proposes a dilemma for arguments of this form, with Apel and Kuhlmann among its proponents: 'either arguments of this retorsive form collapse into deductive transcendental arguments, in which case they are no better off', or 'they have to abandon their use of a transcendental claim at all, and so cease to be transcendental arguments'.^[Robert Stern, 'Silencing the Sceptic? The Prospects for Transcendental Arguments in Practical Philosophy', in Brune, Stern and Werner (eds), *Transcendental Arguments in Moral Theory*, 9–26, at 17; Apel and Kuhlmann are named at 11 among the proponents of the retorsive strategy, and the optimism Stern questions at 17 is Finnis's and Illies's.] The first horn does not close: the exhibition advances no premise of the form 'X is a necessary condition of Y' from which its result is deduced, so there is no transcendental claim to be weakened, the self-removal showing itself in the performance rather than being inferred. The second horn is conceded, and the concession is the chapter's position: what remains is an exhibition and not a transcendental argument in Stern's sense, which is what §3.3.6 calls an *Aufweis*. The Stroudian move is that these conditions are merely how a thinker must think and not how things must be. To assert this, however, is to raise a determinate validity claim, one which states how things stand with the conditions of thought and is put forward as assessable, and so to exercise the assessable determinacy the assertion would confine to a scheme. The relativising conclusion cannot be stated without self-refutation. What is thereby secured is stronger than anthropological unavoidability because it is not scheme-relative: not that these are the conditions of one form of life, but that they are the conditions of there being any determinate content to relativise to a form of life in the first place. Whether what is so exercised includes the reciprocal conditions turns on the strong construal and is not settled by the retorsion. The word necessity is withheld on purpose. Having conceded Stern's second horn, the chapter claims no transcendental necessity of the deductive kind; it claims the standing an exhibition confers, that the relativising claim cannot be executed as a determinate claim, and the difference between that standing and the necessity a transcendental argument would conclude to is the difference §3.3.3 draws between the first level and any proposition about it.

A Habermasian rejoinder remains: the retorsion secures only that the conditions cannot be coherently denied, which is a fact about discourse and not about the world; what is wrung from the sceptic carries, in Schönrich's words as Habermas reports them, no more than quasi-empirical validity. The reply is that at this level uniquely the distinction Stroud's objection exploits has no purchase, the conditions being those of determinacy of meaning. To think determinately about anything, including about 'the world' as what a scheme is alleged not to reach, is already to stand under the conditions of assessable meaning; there is no going behind them to a standpoint from which they might appear a local imposition, any such standpoint being one of determinate thought. This is the content Apel gives *Nichthintergehbarkeit*, and the content claimed for the conditions here: not that they are the deepest layer of a scheme but that there is no 'behind' them. Apel reaches the same confinement in his own answer to Stroud: if 'the language does not exist in the sense that all arguing presupposes it', then 'no version of the verification principle can be formulated' and 'no criteria can be used to establish the existence of language', so that the transcendental argument 'remains the foundation of every critical argumentation about meaning', Stroud's own version of the verification principle included.^[Apel, *Understanding and Explanation: A Transcendental-Pragmatic Perspective*, trans. Georgia Warnke (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1984), 90–91.] The literature's distinction between world-directed arguments, vulnerable to Stroud, and self-directed arguments turning on the conditions of the sceptic's own performance marks this difference; the elenctic disclosure is of the second kind.^[The terms are Christopher Peacocke's, *Transcendental Arguments in the Theory of Content* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), 4, and Quassim Cassam's, 'Self-Directed Transcendental Arguments', in Robert Stern (ed.), *Transcendental Arguments: Problems and Prospects* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1999), 83, as Robert Stern, 'Transcendental Arguments', *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (2015), §2, records.]

A second rejoinder is stronger and must be met, since it charges the retorsion with proving too much. If the impossibility of coherently stating a relativising claim suffices to establish that what the claim employs is non-scheme-relative, the manoeuvre certifies whatever the sceptic's sentence happens to require, its idiom, its distinctions, its conceptual furniture, and a criterion certifying all of that has certified nothing in particular. The hazard is real, and it is answered by a restriction the retorsion carries. What it certifies is not everything the denying performance employs but what the denying performance is: the conditions in whose absence there would be no determinate claim to have been made, as against those in whose absence this claim could not have been made in these words. The sceptic's idiom is dispensable, since he may say it otherwise, in another language and with other distinctions, and the claim survives the substitution. The test therefore asks whether there is a reformulation of the sceptic's claim in which the claim survives the absence of reciprocal determinacy, and the answer returned is that there is none, a formulation lacking it individuating no content for a reformulation to preserve. The exposure of that answer must be stated: its ground is the reciprocity thesis itself, so that the test does not independently establish what it certifies. What the test does is convert the demand for a restriction into a determinate question, whether this is dispensable in the way the idiom is, and answer it by the argument above rather than by a criterion drawn from outside it. The restriction is accordingly entered as specified and not as discharged, and what would discharge it can be named: the demonstration identified at §3.3.6 as belonging to the theory of meaning, that any specification of semantic-syntactic intelligibility sufficient to individuate a determinate content already quantifies over possible assessors. Until it is supplied, the retorsion certifies what passes the substitution test and nothing further, and whether the reciprocal conditions pass it is the same question the chapter has declared open at the level of the thesis.

The limit of what the retorsion secures must be stated with equal care. It secures the non-scheme-relativity of the conditions of determinate meaning: that there is no coherent standpoint from which these can be relativised to a form of life. It does not of itself secure the specific reciprocal norms, for the transit to them holds only on the strong construal. The retorsion and the reciprocity argument are joined in what they jointly deliver and not in their warrants: the retorsion establishes that whatever the conditions of determinate meaning are, their necessity is not scheme-relative; the reciprocity argument, on the strong construal, places among those conditions the correlative positions of speaker and hearer. Against Habermas the yield may then be stated in the conditional form the previous paragraphs require. If the strong reading holds, the modality of the reciprocal conditions differs in kind and not in degree from the anthropological unavoidability of his weaker sense: his claim quantifies over the sociocultural forms of life so far known and is indexed to them, whereas the retorsive claim denies that the relativising move can be coherently made at all. If the strong reading does not hold, the first stage is secured and nothing follows against him. The confinement of Stroud's objection which Chapter Two §2.1.4 entered at the conditions of argumentation is here grounded from the other side, by showing why the conditions of signification are the one class of case in which the distinction between how we must think and how things are lacks purchase. What the retorsion secures is a condition of signification, and not the passage from the necessities of thought to the constitution of the world which Stroud denies can be made.

### §3.3.3 · The three levels, and the middle concept between strict reflection and negative justification

Reflexive irrefutability is the name this chapter gives to the standing of a presupposition whose every attempted refutation miscarries by enacting it. The name must be introduced with care, since on the criterion of §3.2.1 refutation is an operation on propositions and an enactment is not a proposition. What the predicate 'irrefutable' attaches to is therefore not the enactment as such but the enactment by way of the class of its denials: a presupposition is reflexively irrefutable when no denial of it can be executed as a determinate claim. Two things follow at once, and both are conceded. The token miscarriage of any particular denial is exhibited in the denying and is not a matter of hypothesis; the claim that every denial of the kind miscarries is a claim about a type, and it belongs, on the chapter's own criterion, to the register of propositions and inherits their corrigibility. The concept is accordingly not a third modal predicate for propositions, standing between corroborated hypothesis and apodictic certainty. It is a structure with three internally related levels, and its interest lies in what it refuses: the transfer of standing from the first level to the second, which strict reflection makes and which the account this chapter defends declines while keeping what strict reflection secures.

The first level is performative non-contingency: the conditions of determinate signification, disclosed elenctically, and on the strong construal the reciprocal conditions among them, whose denial cannot be performed as a determinate claim. What a defeated denial confers on a claim, by the burden rule, is a provisional standing; what is claimed at this level is conferred on no claim at all, since it attaches to no proposition, so that the rule is respected and not exceeded. The second level is the fallible propositional articulation of what is thereby enacted, revisable and perfectible without limit, and including the transcendental-pragmatic articulation Apel advances against Habermas: Apel's characterisation of the presuppositions is a propositional performance and inherits the corrigibility of that register. The third level is not a further kind of item but the standing of the first two under iteration, at the point at which the iteration would generate a regress, since the statement that the articulation is fallible is itself a fallible articulation, of which the same may be stated in turn without end. The regress is arrested not by a terminal proposition but by the recognition that each step re-enacts the same non-contingent elenctic form: the form is invariant across the ascent and the content asymptotically refined.

The arrest invites a deflationary objection, and it strikes the element of the structure claimed as an addition to the tradition. The invariance of the form is itself asserted, belongs to the second level, and is therefore fallible; the arrest is then either a second-level claim, corrigible and arresting nothing, or a first-level enactment, not a claim about the series and again arresting nothing. The dilemma is answered by declining the assumption that an arrest must be effected by a statement. What halts the regress is not the proposition that the form is invariant but that at each level the conditions are exercised in making the assertion rather than certified by the assertion above it. A regress is vicious where the standing of any member waits on the completion of the series; here each enactment's standing is secured in the enacting, so that the unending series of statements costs the enactments nothing. The invariance claim is accordingly a report on that structure, inheriting the corrigibility of the second level; it is not a concession the structure must survive, since what the report describes is enacted whether or not the report is accurate, and it is not exempt from the corrigibility it inherits, since a structure does not make a description of itself correct. The discipline applies to this sentence as to any other.

The collapse objection must now be met directly, since the concept has been introduced so as to invite it. If the type-claim is corrigible and only token miscarriages are exhibited, the position appears to be Habermas's own, stated at §3.2.3 as indexically self-verifying and universally hypothetical, and the concept adds a name and nothing else. The reply is that the difference between the position and Habermas's lies not in the modality assigned to any proposition, where the two agree, but in what a correction of the type-claim answers to and in what the type-claim is about. For Habermas the corrective is the discussion to which any reconstruction is submitted, and the type-claim ranges over the practice of argumentation as encountered, so that a counterexample would be a form of life which does without the presuppositions. On the account defended here the type-claim ranges over determinate signification as such, and its corrective is the performative evidence of the act, so that a counterexample would be a determinate denial which does not miscarry, and the retorsion of §3.3.2 shows, within its declared limits, that none can be stated. That is a difference in the ground of correction and in the scope of the quantifier, and it is all the difference the concept claims; it is not a difference in certainty. The advance on Habermas is accordingly real but modest, and it is conditional at the point §3.3.2 declared: for the first stage the corrective is secured, and for the reciprocal conditions it is secured only on the strong construal.

One acknowledgement is owed before the structure is set against its rivals. The categorial separation of a propositional from a performative-pragmatic level of the conditions of sense is not first proposed in these pages. Niquet states it as the postulate of the dual character of transcendental sense-critique, or a methodological principle of bifurcation, a designation he records as proposed by Apel: contentful conditions of sense can be given at two levels, the semantic-propositional content of utterances and the general and necessary performative-pragmatic structures of their felicitous execution.^[Niquet, *Transzendentale Argumente*, 10–11, with the designation credited to Apel at 11 n. 7. The operative–propositional asymmetry on which these chapters turn is a received distinction, so cited; what the three-level structure adds is the third level, since neither Niquet nor Apel, in the loci consulted, addresses the regress the iteration of the first two generates.]

Against Kuhlmann, the structure retains the insight of *strikte Reflexion* while refusing what that insight is made to yield. The objection must be stated against his own formulation rather than against the position commonly ascribed to him, since the two differ at the point which matters. Kuhlmann's achievement is to show, against the charge that every grounding falls to the trilemma, that certain presuppositions of arguing are not to be gone behind, their denial being self-contradictory in the enactment rather than in the proposition. What strict reflection adds to reflection as such is stated by him three times over, and each time as a prohibition: the arguer is required to bring the thematic contents together with the action-knowledge he mobilises in order to handle them at all, 'ohne die Differenz zwischen dem Thematischen und dem in Anspruch Genommenen zu nivellieren'; to bring the level of communication together with that of metacommunication, 'ohne die Statusdifferenz zu verwischen'; and to bring the level of propositional contents together with that of performative sentences, 'ohne die funktionale Differenz, die beide Ebenen für ihn in seiner aktuellen Argumentationssituation haben, zu nivellieren'.^[Wolfgang Kuhlmann, 'Reflexive Letztbegründung versus radikaler Fallibilismus: Eine Replik', *Zeitschrift für allgemeine Wissenschaftstheorie* 16/2 (1985), 357–74, at 367, where the naming sentence follows: 'Ich nenne das Geforderte strikt reflexives Argumentieren'. The restriction of the certainty claim to philosophical statements 'in denen kritisch über Recht und Reichweite von Geltungsansprüchen gehandelt wird' stands at 361. The German is retained because the objection turns on *nivellieren* and *verwischen*. For the fuller engagement with Albert, Kuhlmann, *Reflexive Letztbegründung*, ch. II; the thesis of the non-circumventibility of the argumentation situation is announced in the title of 'Reflexive Letztbegründung: Zur These von der Unhintergehbarkeit der Argumentationssituation', *Zeitschrift für philosophische Forschung* 35 (1981), 3–26.] Strict reflection is thus not, in his own hand, a fusion of the two levels. It is their explicit conjunction under an express prohibition against levelling them, and the received charge that Kuhlmann identifies act with proposition does not reach it.

The difficulty lies elsewhere, and it takes the form of a dilemma internal to that formulation. Kuhlmann requires that the two levels be brought together without their functional difference being levelled, and holds that what the bringing-together secures is an ultimate grounding, a certainty which he restricts to philosophical statements in which the right and the reach of validity claims are critically at issue. If the functional difference is preserved, the certainty carried by the enactment does not pass to the proposition which characterises the enactment, and the grounding is not ultimate. If it does pass, the difference has been levelled, since a standing crosses the boundary only if what stands on either side is, for the purpose of the crossing, of one kind, and the prohibition has been broken in the act of satisfying it. The requirement is stated; what is not shown is how it can be met. The dilemma answers to the fault-type Apel names the abstractive fallacy, run in the direction opposite to the one his own diagnosis takes: what is abstracted from is the categorial difference between the type of act which enacts a condition and the type which characterises it. That the same person performs both no more makes them one act than a man's promising and his describing his promise are one act, and simultaneity is no more identity than agency is. A transfer of certainty across the difference must be earned rather than assumed.

An objection is entered against the procedure of this section: the distinction of act-types on which the refusal of transfer rests is nothing other than the operative–propositional asymmetry, and that asymmetry is what the argument was supposed to establish, not what it may help itself to. The answer attends to what kind of standing the asymmetry has. It is not a premise from which the refusal of transfer is deduced. It is what §3.2.2 exhibits in the asymmetry's own denial: the denier's performing and his saying come apart in the act, and the coming-apart is visible in the performance and not inferred from a prior principle. What this section adds depends on that exhibition: a distinction so exhibited will not license the transfer Kuhlmann requires. Two consequences follow, and the second is a limitation. No circularity arises, for the reason §3.3.1 gives of Γ.4: nothing is taken as a premise, and what is invoked is what the objector enacts in objecting. And the exhibition establishes that the asymmetry holds wherever anything is asserted; it does not establish it as a proposition secured independently of any enactment, and no argument of this form could. The chapter claims the first and does not claim the second, which is the restriction it has imposed on Apel and on Kuhlmann, now imposed on itself. Reflexive irrefutability answers Kuhlmann's dilemma by taking its second horn openly: it keeps the strict, performative non-circumventibility of the first level, declines the transfer, and leaves the propositional articulation at the second level as fallible as Habermas insists. The prohibition against levelling is then not a constraint the account struggles to honour but what it asserts.

Against Wellmer, the structure retains a positive reflexive ground while refusing mere exemption. Wellmer holds that morality and reason 'do not stand or fall with the absolutism of ultimate agreements or fundamental groundings', that 'the idealizing assumptions of argument contain neither an ultimate foundation for morality nor the promissory glimpse of an ultimate reconciliation', and that the general norms of argument 'do not amount to universalistic moral norms or meta-norms for morality', the 'must' they carry being of the kind associated with constitutive rules rather than a morally substantial one.^[Albrecht Wellmer, 'Ethics and Dialogue: Elements of Moral Judgement in Kant and Discourse Ethics', in *The Persistence of Modernity*, trans. David Midgley (Cambridge: Polity, 1991), 113–231, at c. 115–16, 182 and 177–81; German: *Ethik und Dialog* (Frankfurt: Suhrkamp, 1986). Page numbers are to be checked against the print edition. 'Negative justification' is not Wellmer's phrase; his own formulations are that the moral 'ought' is derived from the categorical imperative 'only *via negationis*' and that the principle of moral progress 'is not the perfection of sense, but the elimination of nonsense'.] He grants the inescapability of the presuppositions of argument and the performative self-contradiction of their denial; what he denies is that norms so grounded are moral norms. The attraction of the position is that it dispenses with any passage from a fact to an 'ought', since a justification by negation infers no norm from any fact. Its cost is that it leaves the presuppositions exempt from grounding altogether, as no more than what is found to admit of no rational denial, without an account of why their denial fails which would distinguish a performative impossibility from a stubborn intuition. A stubborn intuition may be dislodged by better argument, by a change of sensibility, or by the discovery that its hold has a genealogy; a performative impossibility of the first level cannot be dislodged, because any dislodging would exercise the conditions it removed. Wellmer does locate a ground: no successful individuation, and so no person, is conceivable outside structures of reciprocal recognition. The ground is a condition on persons and not on signification, and a denier who forgoes successful individuation is not thereby self-removing, whereas the elenctic ground is removed in the denying itself. The objection is not that his conclusion is false, since this chapter shares it at the second stage; it is that a ground lodged in the conditions of individuation does not deliver the self-removal on which the difference between a performative impossibility and a conviction that the denial is unthinkable depends.

What results is best described not as a third position between the two but as strict reflection under an express prohibition on propositional transfer. It takes from Wellmer the fallibilism which the refusal of transfer entails, and puts to the use he declines the performative non-circumventibility he concedes. No proposition is certified here which strict reflection does not certify; what is declined is a transfer which, on the reading defended above, strict reflection was never entitled to make. Against the binary of reconstruction and strict reflection the concept stands to one side of each: reconstruction cannot account for the non-contingency of the first level, and strict reflection over-extends that non-contingency to the propositional level.

Three cautions mark the limits of what the *elenchos* delivers, and the third is Wellmer's. First, the transit to the specific reciprocal norms is the substantive step §3.3.2 argued and did not settle. Secondly, the *elenchos* establishes the inescapability of the conditions of determinate signification, and on the strong construal of the reciprocal conditions among them; and inescapability is not yet value. The sceptic who concedes that he cannot signify while denying them may still ask what worth their inescapability has for him, and that residue is displaced, not removed; its discharge is not undertaken in these chapters and is entered among the questions the Conclusion leaves open. Thirdly, a formal condition of argument, however secure, does not supply the criterion by which one reason is better than another, so that the question §3.1.4 left unresolved is not answered by the irrefutability of the conditions under which reasons are exchanged, even if that irrefutability is granted in full. The caution is accepted. What the three levels secure is the standing of the conditions and not a measure of the reasons offered under them, and the scope question of §3.3.6, decided either way, would leave the criterion where §3.1.4 found it. That a positive account of the criterion is owed, and owed by this thesis, is the second of the tasks the Conclusion names.

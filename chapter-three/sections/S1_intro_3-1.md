# PART TWO · THE APORETIC CORE

# CHAPTER THREE

## The Formal-Pragmatic Threshold: The Apel–Habermas Dispute, Reflexive Irrefutability, and the Aporia of the Meaning of Truth

*Conventions.* Works by Habermas and Apel are cited by the abbreviations fixed in the front matter (MCCA, JA, TJ, BNR, TCA I, OPC, BFN, PSC for the essay of 2020, SE1 and SE2 for the two volumes of Apel's *Selected Essays*, TTP for *Towards a Transformation of Philosophy* in the edition named in the front matter, FTSPV for *From a Transcendental-Semiotic Point of View*, DV for *Diskurs und Verantwortung*, and ML for the Mercier Lectures). Where an inference is drawn from a cited passage and not stated in it, the inference is the chapter's; the apparatus records the cited wording and does not repeat this disclaimer at every note. Aristotle is quoted from the Revised Oxford Translation unless another rendering is named; the Greek of *Metaphysics* Γ is that of Ross's edition of 1924, and Bekker numeration is used throughout.

### Introduction

This chapter conducts the dispute between Apel and Habermas over the ultimate justification (*Letztbegründung*) of ethics, and it is the ground on which the thesis reaches the aporia stated in §3.4. Its finding is that the dispute has been mislocated by both its parties' readers and, at moments, by the parties themselves. It is not a contest between a foundationalist and an anti-foundationalist method. Both thinkers have executed the pragmatic turn; both convict the denier of the presuppositions of argumentation out of his own performance; and neither grounds by deriving one sentence from another, which is the conception of grounding Apel takes Albert's trilemma to presuppose. What divides them is what the shared method yields: the location and the modal standing of the presuppositions it discloses. Once the dispute is placed there, it ceases to be a quarrel about how strongly the presuppositions bind, on which the reception has circled without result, and becomes a question of scope with a determinate burden of proof on each side: whether the conditions of determinate signification already carry the reciprocal norms which the rules of discourse articulate. That reformulation is the chapter's principal result.

The chapter has four parts. §3.1 locates the dispute: the paradigm of language which both programmes share and the counter-programme Apel raises upon it; the diagnosis of an abstractive fallacy which both level at the tradition and neither at the other; the site at which the two divide, which is a difference between two concepts of grounding and not a difference of degree; and the state of the question in the literature. §3.2 takes up the instrument with which the modal question is to be adjudicated. The categorial premise which Chapter Two §2.2.2 states, that what a speaker enacts in arguing and the proposition in which a theorist states it are items of different kinds, is defended here against the objections an inferentialist and a transcendental pragmatist would raise, is declared falsifiable, and is used to show that no resource of a reconstructive science carries the modal standing of an enactment across to a proposition about it. §3.3 supplies the form of investigation which the question then requires, which is elenctic and not demonstrative, fixes what such an investigation yields under the name of reflexive irrefutability, and converts the modal quarrel into a question of scope. §3.4 carries the result into the theory of truth, where the aporia of the meaning of truth appears as the modal fracture of §3.1 in another register.

Two disciplines hold throughout. The universalisation principle (U) and the discourse principle (D) are distinguished wherever either appears, the first in its wording of 1983 (Chapter One §1.5.3) and the second in that of 1992 (Chapter Four §4.2.1). Performative self-contradiction is used in the bounded sense fixed at Chapter Two §2.2.2, on which a speaker's assertion is defeated not by a contrary proposition but by the conditions of his own asserting, and in no wider sense. From Chapter One the chapter takes the illocutionary bond (§1.4.2), the retraction of the epistemic conception of truth (§1.5.6), and Apel's counter-programme as it stands upon the interval between reconstruction and bindingness (§1.6). From Chapter Two it takes the three constitutive commitments of reconstructive science and the categorial premise. To Chapter Four it hands the second-stage question on which the dropout turns, and to the Conclusion the aporia.

## §3.1 · The Dispute Correctly Located

### §3.1.1 · The paradigm of language and the counter-programme

The later of Habermas's two direct replies to Apel credits him with a displacement rather than a doctrine. Habermas regards 'his critical step from logical semantics to formal pragmatics (and the paradigm shift from the philosophy of consciousness to the philosophy of language built upon it) in any case as his groundbreaking achievement', and the essay's abstract adds a complaint: that the achievement, which consisted in completing the linguistic turn, 'has been unjustly overshadowed by the tenacious debate over the "ultimate justification" of ethics'.^[PSC 643 for the credit; PSC 627 for the abstract. The essay is 'From formal semantics to transcendental pragmatics: Karl-Otto Apel's original insight', trans. Ciaran Cronin, *Philosophy and Social Criticism* 46/6 (2020), 627–50. Habermas nonetheless calls Apel's position 'the foundationalist thesis' at PSC 642; the word is predicated of a thesis about the ethos of discourse and not of a method, and it is the transposition into a contest of methods which this section denies.] The complaint is entered by one of the two parties, and it is granted here in the following form: the step the debate has overshadowed is stated before the debate is conducted, and the debate is conducted upon it. That what the two thinkers share is not a preliminary to their disagreement but the achievement on which their disagreement depends is a further claim, argued at §3.1.2.

The shift from the philosophy of consciousness to the philosophy of language is not a change of subject-matter but a change in what is taken as given. Where the philosophy of consciousness begins with a subject's representations and asks how they reach a world and other subjects, the philosophy of language begins with an achievement which is already intersubjective and asks what a subject must be in order to participate in it. The two questions are not versions of one another, and an answer to the second is not to be had by accumulating answers to the first. Both programmes stand on the far side of that reordering, Apel in the Preface of 1978 quoted below and Habermas in the acknowledgement quoted above, and where they part is fixed at §3.1.3.

Apel presents his counter-programme as a paradigm and not as a position within one. In the Preface to the English edition of *Towards a Transformation of Philosophy* he describes the integration of a transcendental pragmatics of language into a transcendental semiotics 'which is considered as a new (third) paradigm of First Philosophy (after pre-Kantian ontology and the post-Kantian transcendental philosophy of consciousness)'.^[TTP, Preface to the English edition, p. x, dated Frankfurt am Main, July 1978; the derivation of the three paradigms from the triadic sign-relation is given at FTSPV 42–44 and, at greater length, SE1 240–41. The two English editions of TTP (Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1980; Marquette University Press, 1998) are differently paginated; the edition used is declared in the front matter and the page numbers given here belong to it.] The sequence is not a chronicle. Elsewhere he derives it from the triadic relation of the sign-function by what he presents as a logic of abstraction, each earlier paradigm characterised by what it fails to thematise and its difficulties following from that failure.^[FTSPV 43–45, where the three paradigms are derived from the triadic sign-relation 'according to a logic of abstraction, so to speak' (44); SE1 240–41. The derivation does not stand in the Preface of 1978, to which an earlier draft of this chapter attributed it.] The diagnosis of an abstractive fallacy, stated at §3.1.2, is accordingly Apel's own instrument and not one applied to him from outside; §3.3.3 turns it upon the counter-programme's strongest form.

Beneath the paradigm-claim lies a thesis about meaning on which the modal claims of the counter-programme rest and which §3.3.4 examines: that the sign-function which mediates the intersubjective validity of any constitution of meaning 'turns out to be a transcendental condition of the possibility, rather than a possible object, of sign-mediated knowledge'.^[SE1 115, where the predicate is Apel's and is said of language in its office as 'a mediation of interpretation'; TTP 48 for the sign-mediation of the intersubjective validity of any constitution of meaning.] The paradigm-claim turns on the constitution of intersubjective validity in sign-use and not on the commonplace that signs are used. Only the first denies that a solitary subject's meaning is a prior achievement which language then transmits, and no party disputes the second.

Apel also enters a charge about the refusal of ultimate justification, and it fixes from his side where the disagreement lies: Habermas seems more concerned, as he puts it, to avoid the term ultimate foundation than to avoid what the term refers to.^[The sentence is Apel's and is reported, not endorsed. An earlier draft placed it at FTSPV 80, which is a page of endnotes; the locus must be re-established from the print copy before submission, and the sentence is to be deleted if it cannot be.] What the charge locates is not the method, which both practise, but the standing of what the method yields. The reluctance it reports attaches to the term and not to the thing named, so that what stands between the two thinkers is what may be claimed for the yield and not how the yield is reached. The remainder of §3.1 locates that claim.

### §3.1.2 · The abstractive fallacy as shared diagnosis, and the shared instrument

Apel's name for the fault which the three abstractions of formal semantics commit (Chapter One §1.3.1) is 'abstractive fallacy', and the name is semiotic before it is epistemological.^[The English phrase is Apel's own, in the authorised translation of 1975 made in collaboration with him: 'The Problem of Philosophical Fundamental-Grounding in Light of a Transcendental Pragmatic of Language', trans. K. R. Pavlovic, *Man and World* 8/3 (1975), 239–75, at 249, 263 (three times) and 273 (n. 63); reprinted as 'The Question of Grounding: Philosophy and Transcendental Pragmatics of Language', SE2 68–102. The semantic form of the diagnosis is reported at SE2 34: an abstractive fallacy results 'if one sought to interpret truth as the predicate of propositions in a semantic system and not as the predicate of statements that are asserted by arguing human subjects in speech acts'. No German coinage is used for the term in this chapter; where the German is wanted it is to be taken from *Transformation der Philosophie* and not back-formed from the English.] The fallacy is committed wherever the triadicity of semiosis is reduced: wherever the third place in the sign-relation, held by an interpreter for whom something stands for something, is dissolved into the sign-system instead of being reflected upon. The dissolution is intelligible as a methodological economy rather than as a blunder. A two-term relation between a sign and its designatum is tractable by the instruments the tradition had refined, whereas the third term cannot be specified without reference to a community of interpreters and to the conditions under which what a sign stands for is available to them as this rather than that. What the economy costs is settled by the place it vacates: meaning is analysed without the interpreter, and the categories of the analysis are categories in which no interpreter is named.

Two consequences follow for what a critic of the tradition may then do. First, the fault lies in what was set aside and not in the treatment of what remained, so that the semantic apparatus built under the abstraction stands and is available for adoption entire; what is contested is a claim to completeness, not a body of analysis. Second, a domain narrowed by omission is repaired by supplement, and the additions may be, as the sequence Chapter One §1.3.1 sets out shows they were, the tradition's own. The species of the fault which operates in this chapter is not the semantic one but its counterpart in the theory of grounding. Where the interpreter is absent from the account of meaning, the arguer is absent from the account of justification, and the trilemma is the shape the grounding problem assumes once he is gone. Apel's own statement is that the reduction of grounding 'to the sense of the deduction of sentences from sentences' rests 'fundamentally on an "abstractive fallacy"', the abstraction being from the pragmatic dimension of argumentative language use.^[Apel, 'Fundamental-Grounding' (1975), 263; the same page holds that the logic of science so absolutised 'banishes into the jurisdiction of empirical psychology the pragmatic dimension of argumentation insofar as it cannot be objectivized and formalized'.] On the formulation this chapter adopts, and which Apel does not state in this form, the trilemma is generated by the abstraction rather than discovered by the analysis: where the performative dimension is bracketed, every candidate ground appears as one more sentence in need of a further one, and regress, circle and arbitrary break-off exhaust the field by construction. With the bracketed dimension restored a fourth possibility appears which the trilemma cannot classify, a condition which is not asserted but exercised.

The fourth possibility is available to any foundationalist who cares to claim it, and a criterion which cannot tell an elenctic exhibition from a dogmatic break-off dressed in performative vocabulary is no criterion. One mark distinguishes them, and §3.3.1 applies it: whether the procedure advances a proposition of its own. What is claimed at this stage is narrow, and its failure condition goes with it. The exhaustiveness of the trilemma was secured by an abstraction; should §3.3 fail to show that an exhibition of the elenctic kind escapes the trilemma, the thesis fails at its constructive step and not at its diagnostic one, the deficit having been derived in Chapter Two independently of the exhibition.

Both thinkers stand on the same side of this diagnosis and employ the same instrument. Apel formulates the shared method in 1993: discourse ethics 'gives a rational foundation of its claim to universal validity without making use of the traditional type of grounding through deriving something from something else, i.e. through deduction, induction or abduction', and 'makes use of a transcendental-reflexive and communicative type of rationality' instead.^[Apel, 'How to Ground a Universalistic Ethics of Co-responsibility for the Effects of Collective Actions and Activities?', *Philosophica* 52 (1993/2), 9–29, at 19; his n. 17 (p. 29) refers the formulation back to the article of 1975.] Habermas acknowledges in 1983 that his own procedure of exhibiting the idealising content of unavoidable presuppositions follows Apel's strategy of confirming presuppositions indirectly, by identifying the performative contradictions into which their denial falls.^[MCCA 80–82. That the performative-contradiction test identifies the rules of discourse rather than justifying them is the settled reading; see J. G. Finlayson and D. H. Rees, 'Jürgen Habermas', *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (2023), §1.3.] Apel levels the fallacy at Carnap, Popper and Albert under that name; Habermas, on the acknowledgement of 1983, took from Apel the method which repairs it. Neither levels the fault at the other.

The recalibration secures at the outset that the dispute is not, in the first instance, methodological. Both thinkers have executed the pragmatic turn; both practise the confutative method; and both proceed non-deductively, Habermas recording in 2003 that he follows 'the nondeductive model of justification developed by Apel'.^[BNR 88, in 'On the Architectonics of Discursive Differentiation' (2003); the point of contention is there placed not at the justification move but at its status, which §3.2.3 takes up.] They do not, however, abandon the same thing. Apel abandons the deductivist conception of grounding on which the trilemma depends. What Habermas surrenders, later and more narrowly, is the aspiration to derive (U) from the rules of discourse by strictly formal means, in favour of an abductive justification (Chapter One §1.5.5). The disagreement presupposes a shared achievement and concerns the location and modal status of what that achievement yields; it does not concern the general form of inquiry by which the yield is reached.

### §3.1.3 · The site of the disagreement: two concepts of grounding, the asymmetry of provenance, and the socio-ontology of the moral 'ought'

The reception has cast the dispute as reconstruction, on Habermas's side, against strict reflection on the side of Apel and Kuhlmann.^[Peter Dews, 'A Question of Grounding: Reconstruction and Strict Reflexion in Habermas and Apel', in Peter Uwe Hohendahl and Jaimey Fisher (eds), *Critical Theory: Current State and Future Prospects* (New York and Oxford: Berghahn, 2001), 205–28. The contrast is announced in the title.] The contrast is correct about the two methods and too coarse about what divides them, because the deeper dispute opens only after the pragmatic turn has been made and the confutative procedure accepted by both, and it concerns what that procedure yields. At issue is neither whether moral norms must be exposed to practical discourse nor whether validity claims require intersubjective assessment, both of which Apel affirms. The question arises one level earlier: what makes a reason capable of grounding a validity claim, and by what criterion reasons are ranked without validity collapsing into effectiveness, convenience, or the bare success of a proposal in securing assent. McCarthy put the point in 1978 against the consensus theory of that time: 'if the criteria that serve to distinguish a "grounded" from an illusory consensus themselves require discursive justification, we are moving in a circle; if not, we have transcended the consensus framework'.^[Thomas McCarthy, *The Critical Theory of Jürgen Habermas* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1978), 304. The adjacent circle, of any attempt to justify argumentatively the decision to argue, is put at 320–21 and answered there by the transcendental *tu quoque* McCarthy takes from Apel; he does not return to the first circle, and no discharge of it in the form put has been found in the literature the Introduction surveys.] A reason may be the more effective in producing agreement without being the more valid, and the relation between a reason's efficacy and its validity is not a scale on which the second is a higher degree of the first.

Apel located his objection at this level himself, in the programmatic paper of 2014, so that the placement need not be inferred on his behalf. Reviewing the divergence, he charges, with the reservation 'wenn ich recht verstehe', that Habermas abandoned the grounding of ethics in a presupposition of discourse and reduced the normative relevance of the discourse a priori to 'die moralisch neutrale Funktion einer »formalen« Diskursbedingung'. Were such a reduction possible, the possibility of a rational grounding of morality would be surrendered altogether, because only the structure of argumentative discourse is, in any rational discussion, 'ein nicht hintergehbares und daher unbestreitbares Apriori': an a priori which cannot be gone behind and is for that reason indisputable.^[Karl-Otto Apel, 'Transzendentalpragmatik – Drittes Paradigma der Ersten Philosophie', in *Habermas und der Historische Materialismus*, ed. Smail Rapic (Freiburg and Munich: Alber, 2014), 97; his n. 7 refers the charge to 'Auflösung der Diskursethik? Zur Architektonik der Diskursdifferenzierung in Habermas' *Faktizität und Geltung*', in *Auseinandersetzungen in Erprobung des transzendentalpragmatischen Ansatzes* (Frankfurt: Suhrkamp, 1998), 727–838, which is cited here only through the paper of 2014. The charge carries a second reservation, 'oder vielleicht, genauer gesagt', as to whether its target is universal or formal pragmatics. Translations from the paper are the chapter's.] The formulation is directed at the neutralisation of the discourse a priori and not at any doctrine about moral norms; what is at issue is the possibility of rational grounding as such; and its warrant is a claim about the standing of a structure and not about how strongly the presuppositions bind.

The same paper makes the underlying commitment explicit in a form which settles what kind of dispute this is. Apel records that he never contested Albert's trilemma as such; what he contested were Albert's presuppositions concerning the philosophically relevant concept of grounding. Albert assumes a deductive concept, or more broadly a concept of derivation from something else; Apel had from the outset assumed a reflexive concept, whose logical point lies in the avoidance of the performative self-contradiction of argumentation, the procedure for which Kuhlmann later supplied the term *strikte Reflexion*.^[Apel, 'Transzendentalpragmatik' (2014), 98, crediting the term in his n. 10 to Wolfgang Kuhlmann, *Reflexive Letztbegründung* (Freiburg and Munich: Alber, 1985). Hans Albert, *Traktat über kritische Vernunft*, 5th edn (Tübingen: Mohr, 1991; first published 1968), ch. 1.] The commitment is not a late one. Reconstructing the trilemma in 1975, he had held that 'the possible argumentation against the evidence postulate of classical rationalism has no direct connection with the third alternative of this formal-logically deduced trilemma', what is contested being the concept of grounding under which an arrest of the regress counts as a suspension.^[Apel, 'Fundamental-Grounding' (1975), 244, in §II, 'Critical Reconstruction of the "Muenchhausen-Trilemma"' (the spelling is the printing's).] The dispute is therefore, in the last analysis, not a disagreement about morality but a disagreement about what it is for a reason to be grounded, conducted between two rival concepts of grounding. It is not adjudicable by the arguments so far offered within moral theory, which is where the literature has largely conducted it, and that is part of why it has not been resolved.

What both programmes take as their ground is a practice, which Habermas calls the practice of argumentation: in 1981 the court of appeal which continues communicative action with other means when routine repair fails, in 2020 'this practice of giving and asking for reasons', for which no functional equivalent is known 'in any of the sociocultural forms of life that we know of'.^[TCA I 17–18; PSC 646 (Chapter One §1.4.5). What the practice requires of its participants is set out in 1983 as the catalogue of rules Habermas takes from Alexy, MCCA 89 (Chapter One §1.5.3).] Apel describes the same practice under a stronger predicate and presents the predicate as demonstrated. In the provisional assessment of 1986 he takes up an objection which would have the presuppositions of argumentation established empirically, by interrogating competent speakers after Chomsky's manner under the licence of the reconstructive sciences, and answers it by a retorsion on the proposal itself: the methodical sense of the proposed procedure cannot be comprehended without the validity claims and their possible discursive redemption being already presupposed. With this, he writes, 'it is patently demonstrated' that the presuppositions are, for everyone who argues, 'uncircumventable, untranscendable, (*nichthintergehbar*) and inasmuch, undebatable, unquestionable'; and their affirmation 'is infallible inasmuch as they make possible that fallible hypotheses and their demonstration have meaning'.^[SE2 196–97, in 'Limits of Discourse Ethics? An Attempt at a Provisional Assessment' (SE2 192–218; German 1986). The objection is reported by Apel from a discussion (his n. 10) as one which 'could appeal to the Habermasian concept of reconstructive science'. The ground of the predicate is given at FTSPV 238: 'while arguing, one cannot, in principle, get behind the rationality of arguing'.] The predicate 'infallible' is not Habermas's. The demarcation between the two programmes is, however, one of claim and not of vocabulary, as §3.2.4 shows of *nichthintergehbar*: unavoidability for a practice which has no known functional equivalent, and untranscendability, are claims of different kinds and not two grades of one claim.

The difference of kind has a provenance, and the present reading locates it there. Habermas reaches the ineliminable second person through the acceptability conditions of speech acts, which 'cannot be satisfied one-sidedly, either relative to the speaker or to the hearer', being conditions for the intersubjective recognition of a linguistic claim; the resulting principle of formal pragmatics, he writes in 2020, 'generalizes Apel's reflection on validity (*Geltungsreflexion*) from "truth" to a variety of rationally redeemable validity claims'.^[TCA I 298; PSC 637; and OPC 197, in 'A Reply' (1991), for the four steps by which Habermas records the link from Apel to Austin (Chapter One §1.3.1).] Apel reaches the same second person through the interpretant, the third place in the sign-relation whose dissolution §3.1.2 diagnosed, without which nothing is a sign. Both locate the ineliminable other in the internal relation of meaning and validity, Habermas in the conditions of a practice, Apel in those of signification as such. The difference is named here the *asymmetry of provenance*: an asymmetry not in what the two programmes conclude but in the standing of the premise from which each reaches its conclusion. A practice may in principle have been otherwise; a condition of signification may not, on pain of there being nothing with which to imagine the alternative. Apel's Peircean argument, examined at §3.3.4, is conditional in form: if the subject of truth-claims is conceived as a subject of sign-interpretation, then his claims are not redeemable by his own apperception; and its antecedent is a claim about what it is to mean anything at all. Habermas's argument begins from what participants in a practice must concede to one another if the practice is to go on, and its warrant is a reconstruction of that practice. The first kind of premise is not defeated by exhibiting a community which does otherwise, if the argument of §3.3.2 holds, since a community which did otherwise would not be signifying; the second can be so defeated, and the indexing of the presuppositions to the forms of life so far known leaves it open to that defeat. The modal disagreement is seated there and not in a difference of confidence about a shared result.

Habermas himself objects to a reading of this shape, and the objection must be met before the reading is used. A detranscendentalised conception 'of de facto unavoidability for our species', he holds, 'also follows from Apel's own anthropological grounding of epistemology', since it was Apel who argued, against Gehlen, that a species released from instinctual guidance finds its stabilisation in language rather than in repressive institutions; and Apel, he adds, 'was wary of the relativistic implications of an unacknowledged detranscendentalization of reason, although in fact he could not avoid it either'.^[PSC 646 for the first sentence; PSC 630 for the second; PSC 633 for the argument against Gehlen, where Habermas at once qualifies the point by observing that Apel does not stress the prejudicing power of a pre-interpreting world-view but the anti-authoritarian feature of rational discourse.] If the remark is right, both provenances lie on the side of a practice, Apel's conditions of signification being themselves the conditions of a species-specific form of life, and the asymmetry collapses into a disagreement about emphasis. Three replies are available, and the third concedes what the objection is entitled to. The remark concerns the modality of the conclusion, whereas the present reading concerns the provenance of the premise, and an argument from conditions of signification may terminate in a claim indexed to the human species without its premise being a claim about that species. The remark is, further, an inference Habermas draws about what follows from Apel's anthropology and not a report of what Apel argues, and Apel's own statements at §3.3.4 do not take that route. Whether a condition of signification can ground more than de facto unavoidability is, however, the question §3.2.4 localises and §3.3 tests, and until it is settled the asymmetry of provenance is a location and not a result. No result below is inferred from it. Where the difference between an indexed claim and one that is not does work, at §3.3.2 and §3.3.6, it is carried by the retorsion and by the strong reading of the claim to intelligibility, and not by the provenance of either premise.

Habermas's essay of 2020 locates the disagreement at this same level, in terms of the mode of being of moral norms. The thesis it announces states the diagnosis of this chapter in his voice: the 'direct strategy' of an ultimate justification of moral demands fails 'because the strong obligatory meaning of the moral "ought" (*Sollgeltung*) cannot be derived from the idealizing contents of what participants must conceptually presuppose when they enter rational discourse'.^[PSC 630, in the essay's opening statement of its tasks. The qualifier 'direct' is Habermas's and is kept, since without it the claim would be wider than his.] What is declared to fail is a determinate strategy, and the essay's closing movement offers an answer of another kind rather than abandoning the question. The positive account of where the obligatory meaning resides is socio-ontological, and it is reconstructed here in three moments.

The first moment grants Apel the reciprocal structure of discourse in the concessive voice. 'I do agree', Habermas writes, 'that Apel's method of involving the sceptic in performative self-contradictions allows us to uncover in the general and necessary pragmatic presuppositions of rational discourse a normative layer comprising a few strong idealizations, such as the mutual expectation of equal treatment, comprehensive inclusion, as well as equal and unforced treatment of everybody possibly affected.'^[PSC 643.] The concession is unstinting, and it furnishes the premise from which §3.1.4 turns his own argument against him. The second moment withholds from that structure the moral 'ought'. 'The meaning of the moral "ought" as such cannot be extracted from the presuppositions of discourse', because the orientation to reaching understanding which those presuppositions carry 'is bound up with the discursive examination of a truth claim and therefore has a purely epistemic meaning'; even in practical discourses, which require participants to adopt one another's perspectives, 'what is at stake is mainly the epistemic meaning of the moral-cognitive question'.^[PSC 643 for the first sentence; PSC 644 for the second and third.] The restriction is not the claim that moral normativity is weakly present in the presuppositions. It draws a categorial line between the conditions under which reasons of any kind may be assessed and the deontic force of the norms assessed, and this chapter reads it as categorial and not as gradual. The presuppositions are geared to the testing of validity, not to the binding of conduct; and the page which carries the restriction tells against any reading of Habermas as a covert naturalist, since what it refuses is precisely the extraction of an 'ought' from a fact about how a linguistically socialised species reproduces itself. The third moment supplies the positive account, and here the argument becomes socio-ontological in the strict sense. 'Moral norms can only exist insofar as they are recognized as valid by social subjects and, in virtue of this intersubjective recognition, can actually expect to meet with compliance on the part of their addressees'; what makes moral utterances binding is traced to 'the obligatory meaning and the socially integrating force of social norms that demand recognition and actual respect from acting subjects'; and 'it is only whether they are worthy of recognition that is up for discussion in discourse'.^[PSC 645, all three sentences.] The moment identifies a social site from which norms enter discourse and acquire the force to demand recognition. It does not offer, and does not present itself as offering, a theory of how reasons are ranked once they are there.

What the 'weak-versus-strong' framing conceals is that the argument does not, at the place where the underivability is stated, concern the strength of grounding but the categorial difference between two modes of being. A truth-claim brings its claim-content with it. Habermas enters the point under a hedge which the argument must carry: 'It seems that the assertoric validity claim raised for descriptive statements does not first have to be introduced into the discourse from outside, because the semantic form of a descriptive statement per se refers to the meaning of the claim to truth'; such claims 'form a component immanent in discourse, as it were, even though one must refer to something "existing" in the objective world to explain the meaning of the claim to truth in greater detail'. By contrast, the meaning of the claim to normative validity 'can only be explained with reference to validity claims that already exist or are still to be realized outside of discourse'.^[PSC 645. The immanence is of the claim's constitution and not of its full explication, and the argument requires the first alone.] A moral norm, on his account, exists only as a socially recognised, compliance-expecting fact, whose integrating force discourse does not confer but finds already in force and takes over from the lifeworld when it thematises the norm. The presuppositions of argument can test a norm already imported; what they cannot do is confer the socially integrating force by which it obliges. Whether that force is the whole of the norm's deontic validity or no more than the facticity of its acceptance is the question §3.1.4 puts. Any answer confined to a modal-gradient reading of Habermas, which contests only how strongly the presuppositions bind, leaves this argument untouched, since it is not an argument about how strongly anything binds.

The point at which Habermas's argument terminates confirms that the dispute concerns the form of answer the question admits and not the strength of grounding. The question 'why should we be moral at all?', he announces, 'cannot be dismissed as meaningless, but it cannot be answered within the conceptual framework of any deontological moral theory either'; some sixteen pages later he supplies the reason, that the question 'calls for a teleological answer', and routes moral bindingness through autonomy: everyone can answer the question 'by realizing that only an affirmative answer enables them to understand themselves as "autonomously acting" subjects'.^[PSC 630 for the announcement, PSC 646 for the teleological answer, PSC 647 for autonomy. At PSC 646 Habermas also conjectures that 'perhaps it is this unresolved problem' which led Apel to supplement his moral philosophy with a 'Part B', and declines to address that part; he had examined it at JA 84–86, and its evaluation stands at Chapter Four §4.5.3.] The answer is offered as a good reason to be moral in the deontological sense, so that what is routed through the self-understanding of the person is the ground of a deontological obligation and not a substitute for it. This fixes the disagreement: not, in the first instance, over whether the presuppositions are strongly or weakly necessary, but over whether the bindingness of morality is disclosed in the reciprocal conditions of discourse (Apel) or imported from the lifeworld (Habermas), the further question why one should be moral receiving, on Habermas's side, a teleological answer.

### §3.1.4 · Why the socio-ontological argument leaves Apel's question standing

An irenic reading would conclude that each thinker is right about a different question, Habermas about the provenance of moral bindingness and Apel about what the statement of any grounding already presupposes, so that the dispute amounts to a reframing rather than a refutation. The reading becomes inadequate at a determinate point, when the socio-ontological account is taken to explain the mode of being of moral validity independently of the discourse-presuppositions. The claim defended below is correspondingly narrow. Two things must be held apart which the word 'normativity' runs together: a norm's *standing as a candidate for validity*, its being the kind of item of which rightness or wrongness can be predicated, and its *deontic force*, the obligation it lays on an agent. Habermas's thesis of 2020 concerns the second, and this section does not contest it: the deontic force of a norm is not extracted from the presuppositions of argumentation. What the section contests is that the socio-ontological account explains the first independently of those presuppositions. It does not, and because it does not, Apel's question about the legitimacy of the criteria by which reasons are evaluated is left standing.

The pre-moral reading has a textual warrant in the order of Apel's own derivation. The necessary presuppositions are, he states, all of them implied in the fundamental claim of each argument, and he characterises 'the four presuppositions of consensus formation by argument' thus: '1) the claim to sharing an intersubjectively valid meaning with my partners; 2) the claim to truth as claim for a virtually universal consent; 3) the claim to truthfulness or sincerity of my speech acts taken as expressions of my intentions; and 4) the claim to the morally relevant rightness of my speech acts, taken as communicative actions in the broadest sense of addressing possible communication partners'.^[Apel, 'Discourse Ethics as a Response to the Novel Challenges of Today's Reality to Coresponsibility', *The Journal of Religion* 73/4 (1993), 496–513, at 509, where the characterisation is entered 'very roughly' and the fourth claim is said to imply, 'so to speak, the ethics of an ideal communication community', which Apel calls 'part A of discourse ethics'. The parallel enumeration in the *Philosophica* paper of the same year (21–22) differs in wording.] The reading placed on that sequence runs against the author's gloss, since his next sentence makes it 'especially the fourth claim' that matters. Apel locates the ethics in the fourth item; the reading defended below holds it a condition already operative in the first, so that what he assigns to the terminus of the derivation is disclosed at its origin. To mean something to a possible interlocutor is already to address one who may take what is meant rightly or wrongly, which is the correlative assessability §3.3.2 argues for and not the entitlement §3.3.6 leaves owed. That is the sense in which Apel's *Moralität* is pre-moral: the kernel is reached by reflection on what it is to mean, and names the reciprocal structure internal to the constitution of assessable validity claims, not the content of a moral theory erected on the fourth presupposition.

The inference runs in five steps, each from Habermas's own commitments. (1) A moral norm has its mode of being in its intersubjective recognition: it exists only so far as those subject to it hold it valid, and that holding underwrites its warranted expectation of compliance. (2) Recognition in the validity-conferring sense is not brute conformity. To recognise a norm as valid, as against complying with it from habit or coercion, is to accord it the acceptance which could be vindicated in the giving and weighing of reasons; this is what distinguishes the rationally motivated yes/no position from de facto acceptance throughout the theory of communicative action (Chapter One §1.2.1). (3) Recognition as reason-warranted is possible only under the reciprocal conditions Apel places at the level of meaning: the recognising subjects must occupy the correlative positions of reason-giver and reason-weigher, treating one another's assent and dissent as reason-bearing and not as force. Apel states the correlativity from the participant's position: the arguer 'must on the other hand transcend every particular community and anticipate the judgment of an indefinite ideal audience, which alone could be able to definitely understand and evaluate my universal validity claims', and 'must even address the real audience in a way as if it already represented the ideal one'.^[Apel, 'Discourse Ethics as a Response' (1993), 508. The reciprocity invoked at this step is the correlative assessability of §3.3.2 and not the symmetry, equal entitlement and non-coercion which the rules of discourse articulate; the second-stage thesis of §3.3.6 is not drawn on here.] Absent that anticipated assessability, what one has is not recognition-as-valid but compliance under a description. (4) The intersubjective recognition which, on Habermas's account, confers on moral norms their mode of being is therefore itself governed by the reciprocity of meaning which Apel identifies as the pre-moral kernel. (5) Habermas has not, then, exhibited a mode of being for moral norms which is independent of the discourse-presuppositions. What his account exhibits as independent of them is, so far as recognition in it is withheld from the reason-relation, the norm's social currency, the facticity of compliance; and currency is the mode of being of conventions and coerced regularities, not of moral norms as his own criterion individuates them.

The riposte available to Habermas, constructed from his own distinction and not entered by him, is that recognition admits of two senses which the inference conflates: social currency (*soziale Geltung*), the fact that a norm is generally accepted and can expect compliance, which on his account constitutes a moral norm's mode of being prior to any reason-relation; and validity-conferring recognition, which he may concede presupposes the reciprocal conditions.^[BFN 20: 'The validity we claim for our utterances and for practices of justification differs from the social validity or acceptance (*soziale Geltung*) of actually established standards and expectations whose stability is based merely on settled custom or the threat of sanctions.' Rehg glosses the first term on that page as *Gültigkeit*.] The inference, on the riposte, needs the second sense and is entitled only to the first. It is answered from Habermas's own criterion of the moral. What distinguishes a moral norm from a convention or a coerced regularity, on his account, is that it does not merely obtain but claims to deserve recognition, raising a claim to validity which transcends its factual acceptance, so that the question whether it is rightly accepted remains intelligible where it is universally accepted and the question whether it ought to be accepted where it is not. That is the cognitivist core of discourse ethics, and Habermas states it in the passage which grounds the socio-ontological argument: what is 'up for discussion in discourse' is whether norms are 'worthy of recognition', and worthiness is a claim, since a norm worthy of recognition is one whose recognition could be vindicated by reasons. A norm which merely received recognition would be a convention; a norm whose recognition is at issue as worthy or not claims to deserve it, which is to raise a validity claim and so to present itself as reason-warranted, and recognition as reason-warranted presupposes the reciprocal positions established at step (3). The riposte cannot hold both that moral norms are distinguished from conventions by the validity claim they raise and that their mode of being is a currency independent of the reason-relation.

The result is an explanatory dependence rather than a formal contradiction. If social recognition explains only a norm's currency, the norm's standing as valid remains dependent on the practice of reason-assessment, and the socio-ontological thesis is not an independent account of validity; if social recognition is meant to include recognition as reason-warranted, the reciprocal conditions of assessment re-enter, and it is again not independent. Habermas may consistently keep apart the social reproduction of norms and their discursive validation; what he may not do is let the first discharge the office of the second. Two qualifications forestall two misreadings. The closure grants that a norm's social currency is not conferred by argument: a norm may be socially binding long before it is thematised in any discourse, and even if a norm's social force is granted its lifeworld provenance without remainder, the independence claim fails, since what makes the norm a *moral* norm already stands under the reciprocal conditions of meaning. Nor is the closure a covert reintroduction of ultimate grounding: it establishes that the legitimacy of reason-assessment presupposes reciprocal conditions of meaning, and leaves open whether those conditions are apodictic, weakly transcendental, historically sedimented or otherwise modalised, a question which belongs to §3.3 and between whose answers the pre-moral thesis is neutral.

The neutrality of (D) may now be shown to answer a question Apel does not pose. *Faktizität und Geltung* introduces the discourse principle at a level of abstraction still neutral with respect to morality and law, since it refers to action norms in general, so that morality via (U) and law via the democratic principle are two specifications of it.^[BFN 107–10.] Were Apel's objection that (D) covertly contains moral content, the answer would be complete, since (D) is pre-moral. His charge, in the formulation of 2014, is that the normative relevance of the discourse a priori has been reduced to a morally neutral function, and a reduction is not a distribution. The neutrality thesis apportions an already-constituted validity across two domains; Apel's question is what constitutes the validity so apportioned, and whether agreement among participants in rational discourse carries the normativity by which invalid norms are excluded or merely records a negotiated *modus vivendi*. Habermas's thesis is a correct thesis about the architectonics of discourse differentiation, and it is orthogonal to the question Apel asks.^[Political liberalism in Rawls's form declines the question at this level from the other side, drawing its standard of justification from what a democratic society's political culture already holds and ordering it by a criterion of reciprocity: John Rawls, *Political Liberalism*, expanded edn (New York: Columbia University Press, 2005), Lecture I §2 and Lecture II §2; 'Justice as Fairness: Political not Metaphysical', *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 14/3 (1985), 223–51. The closure entered above reaches it by the same route, since a culture in which an idea is latent supplies the idea's currency and not its title; the exchange with Habermas of 1995 belongs to Chapter Four and is not anticipated here.]

Neither candidate answer settles the matter alone, and saying why fixes the question §3.3 inherits. Discursive procedure explains the form in which reasons are exposed to criticism; lifeworld recognition explains how norms acquire social presence and practical force; neither explains why one reason should count as better than another which is also intelligible, defensible and socially effective. One reply must be entered on Habermas's behalf and then bounded. A proceduralist will say that there is no criterion of the better reason beyond the procedure, and that to ask for one is to ask for what discourse ethics was constructed to do without. The reply is correct about what the theory claims and does not touch the question. Whether the procedure's verdicts carry validity rather than record effectiveness is a question about the standing of the procedure's own presuppositions, and it is that question, not a demand for a substantive criterion, which the pre-moral reading presses; that a positive account of the criterion is nonetheless owed, and owed by this thesis, is a commitment avowed at §3.3.3 and discharged, so far as it is discharged, in the Conclusion. The question has a reflexive shape: the criteria by which reasons are assessed are themselves open to criticism, but criticism of those criteria is itself an exercise of reason-assessment, and cannot proceed by producing a further criterion without regress, exemption, or a circle in which the criterion certifies itself. What *strikte Reflexion* proposes at this point is not a further criterion but a change in the kind of thing appealed to: the conditions of criticism are not supplied as additional propositions awaiting justification but enacted in the activity of criticising. Whether an appeal of that kind constitutes a mode of philosophical vindication, and what its yield would be, is the question §3.3 puts in its proper form.

### §3.1.5 · The state of the question: what ultimate justification claims, and the three charges

What an ultimate justification claims must be fixed before the location defended above is set against its neighbours, since the reception has lost the dispute by grading it. Apel's claim is that the presuppositions of argumentation, brought to explicit awareness by an arguer's reflection on his own arguing, cannot be gone behind: their standing is not a high degree of the certainty that attaches to a well-corroborated hypothesis but a standing of another kind, since every attempt to doubt them, to test them, or to derive them from something prior presupposes them. The double criterion of 1975 is that they can neither be contested without performative self-contradiction nor demonstrated without *petitio principii*.^[Apel, 'Fundamental-Grounding' (1975), 262–64; §3.3.5 quotes the passage.] Habermas's claim is that the same presuppositions are unavoidable for a practice for which no functional equivalent is known, and that every reconstruction of them has hypothetical status. The two claims do not lie on one scale. No strengthening of the second reaches the first, since a claim which quantifies over the forms of life so far known remains, however far the quantifier is extended, a claim of that shape, and Apel's claim denies that the quantification can be coherently made; and no weakening of the first reaches the second, since an ultimate justification which held *pro tempore* would not be one. The difference is categorial and not gradual, and a dispute conducted as though it were gradual has no terminus. What the chapter asks is accordingly not how strongly the presuppositions bind but what kind of standing the yield of a method both parties practise can have, and whether a yield of a third kind, neither corroborated hypothesis nor proposition immune to revision, is available. That is the question §3.3.3 answers under the name of reflexive irrefutability.

Three charges are in play, and they are stated once so that what is adjudicated in this chapter and what is not may be told apart. The first is methodological, and it is Apel's: that a programme which conducts itself as a reconstructive science runs together two reconstructions of unequal standing, one directed on what any argument must already presuppose and one on the course of cultural evolution. The charge reaches the method's description of itself; Chapter Two §2.1.1 answers it as a demand and not as a refutation, and it is not re-argued here. The second charge is modal, and it runs in both directions. In Apel's direction it is that the weak strategy neutralises the discourse a priori and that Habermas holds a fallibilism whose unrestricted statement is self-undermining, the liar-structure of which Chapter Two §2.1.2 displays.^[SE2 87; Apel, 'Fundamental-Grounding' (1975), 261: 'If the "Fallibilism" principle is itself fallible, it is to just that extent not fallible and vice versa.' The result is not that fallibilism is false but that it cannot be stated in unrestricted form: a result about a condition of statability and not about a truth-value. Its appearance at the level of a meta-thesis about reconstruction is the first indication that the method this chapter adopts is already operating in the materials it examines.] In Habermas's direction it has two formulations. In 1983 he diagnoses the ground of the counter-programme's claim, charging it with equating propositional truth with the experience of certitude, a move available only where the philosophy of consciousness still supplies the terms (MCCA 96; §3.2.4). In 2020 he diagnoses its strategy: the construction which is to lend the proof of entanglement in performative contradictions 'the foundational status of an "ultimate justification"' is 'compromised by its narrow, epistemological conception of the justification strategy and its failure to explicitly de-transcendentalize reason', Apel having held 'fast to the epistemological question even after he has accomplished the paradigm shift'.^[PSC 643.] The second charge reaches the standing of what the shared method yields, and it is the charge this chapter adjudicates: at §3.1.4, where the neutralisation limb is met; at §3.2, where the categorial premise is grounded and every route a transfer of standing might take is found barred to a reconstructive science; and at §3.3, where the question is put whether an exhibition of the elenctic kind can display what a reconstruction cannot certify. The third charge concerns scope, and it is the one the reformulation of §3.3.6 makes explicit: whether determinate signification already carries among its conditions the reciprocal entitlements which the rules of discourse set out, and whether anyone is bound to occupy the position in which those entitlements hold. It reaches the content of what is disclosed and not its standing, it is declared open at §3.3.6 with a determinate burden on each side, and in the form of the legitimacy question it is handed to Chapter Four. The three are not one charge at three strengths. The first would stand if the second fell; the third would stand if the first two were settled in Apel's favour, since a presupposition secured as irrefutable is not thereby shown to contain a norm; and the second is decided, so far as this chapter decides it, at a level neither of the others occupies.

A question is put to the whole undertaking from the anglophone side, and it belongs here. Finlayson observes that Habermas 'might also be correct to claim that transcendental-pragmatic argumentation can and should do without ultimate foundations, but these are different issues' from the derivation of the moral principle.^[J. G. Finlayson, 'The Persistence of Normative Questions in Habermas's Theory of Communicative Action', *Constellations* 20/4 (2013), 518–32, at 522. The observation is entered against Schnädelbach's objection and not against the counter-programme.] The observation invites the question whether the programme needs the irrefutability result at all. The answer these chapters give is that the programme needs the result in the measure that it makes the claims it makes. A theory which undertook to reconstruct the presuppositions of argumentation as unavoidable for a practice, and claimed nothing of what it reconstructed beyond its use in that practice, would need no irrefutability and would incur no deficit; the programme is not of that kind. It claims of the presuppositions that they are unavoidable for anyone who argues, in the modality Chapter Two §2.1.4 grades from Habermas's dated statements, and it undertakes to justify a principle whose validity is to bind. That the programme does without ultimate foundations is not disputed. What is disputed is whether, doing without them, it can say what it says of its own presuppositions, and that is a question about the programme and not about Apel.

The location defended above may now be set against its nearest neighbours. Dews's frame remains the anglophone point of departure, and it is superseded rather than rejected: it correctly identifies two methods and mislocates what divides them, since both methods are practised by both parties. Of the literature since, one volume makes the parties argue rather than reporting them. In *Transcendental Arguments in Moral Theory* Kettner regrounds the Apelian starting-point in a constitutive aim of argumentative discourse rather than in non-circumventibility as such; Werner defends a modest use of retorsive argument which makes explicit the constitutive commitments of the practice of justifying actions to one another and suffices, he holds, for practical purposes without forcing the sceptic to endorse moral principles; and Brune distinguishes two readings of performative inconsistency.^[J. P. Brune, R. Stern and M. H. Werner (eds), *Transcendental Arguments in Moral Theory* (Berlin and Boston: De Gruyter, 2017): M. Kettner, 'Argumentative Discourse: The Transcendental Starting Point of Apelian Discourse Ethics', 325–48; M. H. Werner, 'Social Constitutivism and the Role of Retorsive Arguments', 231–46; J. P. Brune, 'Transcending Value: Two Readings of Performative Inconsistency', 283–300.] The present chapter stands between Kettner's regrounding and Werner's modesty. It keeps non-circumventibility at the first stage, where §3.3.2 secures it within declared limits, and concedes to Werner that the second stage is not forced on the sceptic, which is the scope result of §3.3.6 reached by another route. Kettner's placement of the disagreement runs closest to the one defended here: he locates it at the transit from a normative to a specifically moral reading of the discourse principle, and in the additional resources each thinker holds necessary for that transit, a theory of transcendental argument for Apel and a sociological theory of modernisation for Habermas. He reports having shown elsewhere that the disagreement 'boils down to whether a clear sense can be given to calling some of the constitutive norms of argumentative discourse "moral norms"', and 'that Apel is right (against Habermas) in that there is such a sense'.^[Matthias Kettner, 'Discourse Ethics: Apel, Habermas, and Beyond', in Christoph Rehmann-Sutter, Marcus Düwell and Dietmar Mieth (eds), *Bioethics in Cultural Contexts* (Dordrecht: Springer, 2006), 299–318, at 315–16 n. 2.] §3.3.6 declares that question open rather than deciding it, and moves the examination one level down, to the constitution of the discursive validity from which any such status would derive. The stratification has been marked by Gronke as the difference between the *Begründungsfrage* and the *Architektonikfrage*: between the question by what right discursive validity is constituted at all, and the question how an already-constituted validity is apportioned across the domains of practical reason.^[Horst Gronke, 'Apel versus Habermas: Zur Architektonik der Diskursethik', in A. Dorschel, M. Kettner, W. Kuhlmann and M. Niquet (eds), *Transzendentalpragmatik* (Frankfurt: Suhrkamp, 1993), 273–96.] Habermas's mature architecture is an answer to the second question. The claim of this chapter is that it presupposes an answer to the first which it does not supply. That is not the claim that the architecture is defective as an answer to the question it addresses. It is the claim that the question it addresses is not the one on which its own authority depends.

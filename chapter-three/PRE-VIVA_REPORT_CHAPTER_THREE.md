## 1. Overall pre-viva assessment

The chapter is the strongest philosophical material in the thesis. It is also the chapter most likely to be attacked at viva, because it claims more than it establishes at three points.

Its principal result is sound and defensible. The Apel–Habermas dispute is not a contest of methods. Both parties practise the same non-deductive, confutative procedure. The dispute concerns the modal standing of what that procedure yields, and it is better posed as a question of scope than as a quarrel about degrees of necessity. The engagement with Kuhlmann's three prohibitions is the most original passage in the chapter. So is the token/type analysis of self-application, and so is the verdict that Apel's direct route to equal entitlement succeeds against the contester inside discourse and fails at the constitution of the class of addressees. Specialists will recognise these as contributions.

In its submitted form the chapter would not pass a demanding examiner without major corrections. The reasons are these:

- **Conditional results are stated as established.** The modal result against Habermas holds only on Apel's strong construal of the claim to intelligibility. The chapter says so in some places and forgets it in others.
- **The central concept is exposed to a collapse objection it does not face.** "Reflexive irrefutability" risks saying nothing Habermas does not already say.
- **One exegetical inference from Aristotle is unsound.** It concerns the word order at *Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006b.
- **The apparatus contains a significant number of citation errors.** Some are misattributions, some misquotations, and some page numbers are wrong.
- **The chapter is too long and its prose is too recursive.** It is roughly half as long again as a thesis chapter should be, and its self-referential signposting will read to an examiner as defensive, and possibly as machine-assisted.

Recommended outcome if submitted unrevised: **major corrections.** If the revised version is adopted and the open items in section 4 are closed, the chapter should be defensible at a leading institution.

## 2. Major conceptual weaknesses

**2.1 The result against Habermas is conditional, and the chapter does not consistently say so.**

- **The problem.** Habermas does not deny the first stage, that determinate meaning requires the exclusion of contradiction. A result confined to the first stage is therefore not a result against him. Everything the chapter claims against weak transcendentalism depends on the strong construal of the intelligibility claim. On that construal, assessability by an entitled other is already a condition of meaning. §3.3.2 says this is "argued and not established". Yet §3.3.4 says the semiotic a priori "now supplies" the conclusion §3.1.5 required, and §3.4.1 states the negative result with only a general avowal.
- **The cost.** An examiner will ask the candidate to state the result without the strong construal. Nothing against Habermas survives.
- **Revision.** A new paragraph in §3.3.2 states the dependence explicitly. The yield against Habermas is now stated in conditional form throughout.

**2.2 "Reflexive irrefutability" faces a collapse objection the chapter does not address.**

- **The problem.** By the chapter's own categorial premise, refutation is an operation on propositions and enactments are not propositions. So "irrefutability" cannot be predicated of an enactment directly. If it is predicated of the type-claim, that every denial of the kind miscarries, then the claim is a proposition and corrigible. The position is then Habermas's own, "indexically self-verifying and universally hypothetical". The chapter itself formulated that position at §3.2.3.
- **The cost.** The chapter's claimed addition to the tradition becomes a relabelling.
- **Revision.** The new §3.3.3 introduces the predicate as attaching to the enactment by way of the class of its denials. It concedes that the type-claim is corrigible. It then states where the difference from Habermas lies: in what a correction answers to and in the scope of the quantifier, not in certainty. The advance is described as real but modest.

**2.3 The five-step argument against the socio-ontology of the moral "ought" partly misses its target.**

- **The problem.** Habermas's thesis of 2020 concerns deontic force, the *Sollgeltung*. The original argument shows that a norm's standing as a candidate for validity presupposes the reciprocal conditions of assessment. It then concludes that Habermas "has not exhibited a mode of being for moral norms independent of the discourse-presuppositions". That runs the two senses of "normativity" together.
- **Revision.** §3.1.4 now separates *standing as a candidate for validity* from *deontic force*. It concedes the second to Habermas and contests only the first. The argument is narrower and no longer vulnerable to the reply that it attacks a thesis Habermas does not hold.

**2.4 The proceduralist reply to the "criterion of the better reason" is not faced.**

- **The problem.** The chapter repeatedly asks by what criterion one reason is better than another. A Habermasian will answer that there is no criterion beyond the procedure, and that asking for one begs the question against proceduralism.
- **Revision.** §3.1.4 now enters the reply and bounds it. What is pressed is the standing of the procedure's presuppositions, not a demand for a substantive criterion.

**2.5 The transcendental-semiotic resolution in §3.4.4 does nothing for rightness.**

- **The problem.** The original's own answer to the second objection shows that assessability is shared by truth and rightness claims. So locating transcendence in assessability cannot relieve the pressure on the cognitivist vocabulary for morality. The original nevertheless presents the resolution as bearing on "the aporia" as a whole.
- **Revision.** §3.4.4 now states that the resolution relieves the aporia, if at all, only on the side of truth.

**2.6 The abstractive fallacy is extended beyond Apel's usage without adequate control.**

The chapter applies Apel's term in two directions Apel does not: to Kuhlmann in reverse, and to Brandom's two horns. The first extension is retained because it does real work and is declared. The second has been removed from Chapter Three. It duplicates Chapter Two §2.3.3, and it inflates a technical term into a general-purpose diagnosis.

## 3. Exegetical corrections

**3.1 Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Γ.4, 1006b5–9.**

- **What the original claimed.** It inferred from the order in which the two losses are named that address to another is the "first casualty". It concluded that the unity-condition is "dialogical in Aristotle before it is dialogical in Apel".
- **Why this fails.** The Greek reads ἀνῄρηται γὰρ τὸ διαλέγεσθαι πρὸς ἀλλήλους, κατὰ δὲ τὴν ἀλήθειαν καὶ πρὸς αὑτόν· οὐθὲν γὰρ ἐνδέχεται νοεῖν μὴ νοοῦντα ἕν. The clause about oneself is an a fortiori intensification, "and in truth with oneself too". The explanatory clause grounds both losses in the impossibility of *thinking* without thinking one thing. If anything, the sentence makes thought prior. A classicist examiner would identify this at once.
- **Revision.** The inference is withdrawn. The dialogical character of the procedure is now drawn from its form: a refutation needs a respondent who supplies its material by signifying to another. It is no longer drawn from word order.

**3.2 The Greek text.**

- **The problem.** The original quotes the Greek of Γ.4 from a 2021 English translation (Topos Productions) of Łukasiewicz's 1910 monograph. That source antedates Ross's text. The monograph is a secondary source, and the translation is a small-press product.
- **Why this matters.** Quoting Aristotle's Greek from it signals second-hand engagement with the primary text.
- **Revision.** All Greek is now given from Ross's edition of 1924.

**3.3 Apel on the elenchos.**

- **The problem.** The chapter cites Apel as rooting his method in the Aristotelian elenchos (FTSPV 94). It does not register that in the Mercier Lectures (ML 42) Apel explicitly sets his dialogical elenchos apart from Aristotle's "indirect proof". He takes the indirect proof to presuppose definitions and so to remain exposed to a *petitio*.
- **Revision.** The two statements are now set side by side, and the chapter's claim is restricted to the form of argument on the pragmatic-act reading.

**3.4 Habermas's register in 2020.**

- **The problem.** The chapter attributes a "weakly transcendental" grade to Habermas's essay of 2020. The phrase does not occur there. Habermas's own term in that essay is "quasi-transcendental" (PSC 635, 636, 645), and the modality is unavoidability "in the weaker sense" (PSC 646).
- **Revision.** A note now marks "weakly transcendental" as the thesis's designation for the grade fixed from the texts of 1991 and 2003.

**3.5 Wellmer.**

- **The problem.** "Negative justification" is not Wellmer's phrase.
- **Revision.** His own formulations are now given in the note: the moral "ought" derived "only *via negationis*", and "the elimination of nonsense". The core claim is now quoted: the norms of argument "do not amount to universalistic moral norms or meta-norms for morality".

**3.6 Lafont.**

- **The problem.** The original presents the pressure on the rightness side "along the line Lafont's argument opens" without a locus.
- **Revision.** The argument is now anchored in *The Linguistic Turn in Hermeneutic Philosophy*, ch. 7, 333–35. Her formulation there is a dilemma between infallibility and a procedure that "allows for error". That is sharper than the chapter's paraphrase, and it is now used.

**3.7 Habermas's "unease".**

- **The problem.** The original says Apel was "uneasy" at a detranscendentalisation "left unavowed". Neither word occurs in the source.
- **Revision.** Habermas's words are "wary" and "unacknowledged" (PSC 630), and they are now used.

**4.5 Some features will raise questions about authorship.** Nothing in the chapter proves or disproves machine assistance, and none is alleged here. Three features are nonetheless the kind that prompt the question at viva:

- **Pervasive disclaimers.** About forty notes end with a disclaimer of the form "the inference is drawn in the body and is not X's".
- **Constant self-reference.** Displays are introduced by forms such as "in the sentence Chapter Two §2.1.4 displays". There are several hundred such cross-references in the original.
- **A uniform vocabulary.** The same terms recur without variation: "entered", "displayed", "the office of", "the reach", "stands where".

Taken together they produce a prose that is uniform, highly self-conscious about attribution, and repetitive at the level of formula. The revision replaces the disclaimers with a single convention statement at the head of the chapter. It cuts the cross-references to those a reader needs and varies the vocabulary. The candidate should be ready to explain the drafting process if asked, and should make sure every quotation used has been read in the edition cited.

## 5. Structural recommendations

**5.1 Length.** At about 54,000 words the original is a small monograph, not a chapter. The revision reduces it to about 32,000 words by:

- deleting §3.1.3, "the twin perils", whose substance is absorbed into §3.1.3 and §3.1.5 of the revision;
- moving the Rawls paragraph to a note;
- deleting the Bakhtin paragraph, a digression that the chapter itself says it does not use;
- compressing the excursus on the refutation of the Forms and the Łukasiewicz ranking;
- replacing the 1,100-word Brandom paragraph with a reference to Chapter Two §2.3.3, where the same argument is already made;
- cutting repeated statements of the three charges, which appeared three times.

**5.2 Order.** §3.4 on truth repeats material from Chapter One §1.5.6 and Chapter Two §2.7. It is retained, because the aporia is the thesis's fulcrum, but compressed. If the thesis is restructured again, the cleaner architecture is as follows:

- Chapter Three ends at §3.3.6 with the scope result.
- The aporia of truth opens Chapter Four as its first section.

The aporia would then stand where both routes to it, truth and the discourse principle, can be compared. The revision does not make this move, because it would require rewriting Chapter Four.

## 6. Line-by-line revision: principal changes

These are the changes an examiner would look for first. Paragraph references are to the original.

1. **Opening, paragraphs 1–2.** The original's opening sentence runs to 150 words and joins six clauses with colons. It is split into seven sentences. The discipline paragraph is shortened, and it no longer promises what later sections deliver.
2. **§3.1.1, the Habermas credit.** The original says Habermas "names" the step. The source reads "I regard". The quotation now matches PSC 643.
3. **§3.1.2, the German coinage.** "*Abstraktionsfehler*" is replaced by Apel's own English, "abstractive fallacy". The German form is not attested in the loci cited, and a back-formed German term invites the question of where it comes from.
4. **§3.1.3, the charge of naturalism (was §3.1.3, "twin perils").** The claim that Habermas's refusal is "anti-naturalist" is kept. It is now grounded in PSC 643–44 and no longer made to depend on the adverb "purely".
5. **§3.1.4, step (5).** The step is rewritten under the new distinction between standing and force (section 2.3 above).
6. **§3.1.5, Kettner.** The engagement is kept and shortened. Gronke's distinction is kept.
7. **§3.2.1.** The paragraph restating the premise "in the form Chapter Four will use" is compressed. Its claim that the categorial line is "confirmed" by Habermas's predicates at MCCA 32 is kept, since MCCA 32 was verified.
8. **§3.2.2.** The Derrida amendment is compressed from three paragraphs to one.
9. **§3.2.4, the register of 2020.** The note on "weakly transcendental" versus "quasi-transcendental" is added.
10. **§3.3.1, Γ.4.** The word-order inference is withdrawn (section 3.1 above). Apel's distinction between the dialogical elenchos and the indirect proof is added.
